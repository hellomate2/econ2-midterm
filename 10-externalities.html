Class 11 · Externalities

Externalities and climate change

An externality is a cost or benefit that lands on people outside a market, so the market price leaves it out and the market produces the wrong quantity. On the exam this shows up as a short answer that needs the words social marginal cost and social marginal benefit, and as a graph or calculation like Problem Set 2 question 3, where you find the market quantity, the efficient quantity, the deadweight loss, and the corrective tax.

The ideas, explained

What an externality is

In class Edwards put it simply: externalities are things other people do that affect you, even though you did not do them. The slide definition is that an externality is an additional cost or benefit triggered by market activity that is not paid or enjoyed by the market participants; it falls on external groups. A market moves a good from sellers to buyers at a price both agree on. If that deal creates spillover effects on people who were not part of the agreement, those effects are externalities, and nobody in the deal has a reason to count them.

His campus example: a scooter rider bought the scooter at the market price, so the scooter is worth at least that much to them. But that price contained no insurance against the small chance that someone trips over the scooter in a hallway during an evacuation. That risk is a cost to other people, so a scooter parked inside is a negative externality. Other examples from the slides and lecture:

The same activity can be both. In his bees story, a neighbor's hives had no water source, so the bees drank from a rental's swimming pool and stung two kids. The class poll answer was negative externality, even though pollination is a positive one. He added that if the renters had known about the bees, the rent should have reflected the risk.

Private versus social

"Private" means the people participating in the market: buyers, sellers, and the government if there is a tax or subsidy. "Social" means everyone, inside and outside the market. Edwards stressed that social does not mean socialism; it means costs or benefits "that the market does not naturally absorb" and that are felt broadly.

The demand curve is the private marginal benefit (PMB): each buyer's willingness to pay for one more unit. The supply curve is the private marginal cost (PMC): what one more unit costs the sellers. The external pieces are:

  • External marginal cost (EMC): the additional cost to people outside the market when one more unit is produced and consumed.
  • External marginal benefit (EMB): the additional benefit to people outside the market when one more unit is produced and consumed.

SMC = PMC + EMC and SMB = PMB + EMB. The market produces where PMB = PMC (demand meets supply). The efficient (socially optimal) quantity Q* is where SMB = SMC.

A negative externality raises social marginal cost above private marginal cost, so it lives on the supply side. A positive externality raises social marginal benefit above private marginal benefit, so it lives on the demand side. In his words, a positive externality is "a story about the marginal benefit, the demand curve," and a negative one is a story about "the marginal cost, the supply curve." Getting this right tells you which curve to draw a second copy of.

Negative externalities: the market overproduces

Take Edwards' gasoline market. Burning gas pollutes, so each gallon imposes a cost on people who neither bought nor sold it. Buyers and sellers only weigh their own benefit and cost, so they trade until PMB = PMC, at Q1. But at Q1 the true cost of the last gallon to society, SMC, is higher than the benefit. Society would be better off with less: Q*, where SMC crosses demand.

Notice the demand curve is labeled D = PMB = SMB. Edwards said he could not think of an external benefit of burning gasoline, so private and social marginal benefit are the same curve. That is why the optimum is where SMC meets the demand curve itself.

Where is the deadweight loss? Every unit between Q* = 4 and Q1 = 6 costs society more (SMC) than anyone values it (demand). The loss on each such unit is the vertical gap between SMC and demand, and adding those gaps up gives a triangle with its vertex at the social optimum (4, $8) and its base standing on the line Q1 = 6, running from demand ($6) up to SMC ($10). Edwards described it as a triangle that "points toward the social optimum, like you normally would see." Moving to Q* means moving northwest along the demand curve: the true cost is higher, so the optimal quantity is lower and the price is higher.

With a constant EMC, the deadweight loss of a negative externality is DWL = ½ × EMC × (Q1 − Q*). Here ½ × 4 × 2 = 4.

Positive externalities: the market underproduces

Now flu shots. Your shot protects you, which is why you would pay for it, and it also cuts the chance you spread flu to others. That extra benefit to other people is an external marginal benefit, so SMB sits above the demand curve (PMB). If nothing else costs society extra, supply is both PMC and SMC. The market stops at Q1, where demand meets supply, but at Q1 the social benefit of one more shot is still above its cost. Society wants more, up to Q*, where SMB crosses supply.

The deadweight loss triangle now sits between Q1 = 5 and Q* = 7, below SMB and above supply. These are shots that would be worth more to society ($9 at Q = 5, falling to $7 at Q = 7) than they cost to make, but the market does not produce them. The triangle points toward the social optimum again, this time to the right. In the vaccine case, willingness to pay is effectively higher once you count everyone, so the optimal quantity is higher.

Accounting for surplus at any quantity

When there is an externality, total surplus has to include the people outside the market. The external cost at any quantity is the area between SMC and PMC up to that quantity; with a constant EMC it is just EMC × Q.

Total (social) surplus = CS + PS + tax revenue − external cost (add external benefit instead, for a positive externality; subtract subsidy cost if there is one). The maximum possible total surplus is the area between demand and SMC from 0 to Q*. Deadweight loss = maximum total surplus − actual total surplus.

Check this on the gasoline numbers. At the market quantity Q1 = 6 and price $6: CS = ½ × 6 × (12 − 6) = 18, PS = ½ × 6 × 6 = 18, external cost = 4 × 6 = 24, so total surplus = 18 + 18 − 24 = 12. The maximum, at Q* = 4, is the area between demand and SMC from 0 to 4, a triangle with base 4 and height 12 − 4 = 8, so ½ × 4 × 8 = 16. The difference, 16 − 12 = 4, is exactly the deadweight loss triangle. This "two routes to the same DWL" check catches arithmetic mistakes.

The optimal amount of pollution is usually not zero

This is F25 question 1(g), and it surprises people. The slide says that if pollution costs can be quantified, there is an optimal level of polluting that equates social marginal cost and social marginal benefit. Gasoline pollutes, but the first gallons (getting to work, ambulances, trucks delivering food) are worth far more to people than their full social cost, including the pollution. Banning gasoline would throw away all that surplus. The efficient quantity is where SMC = SMB, which is usually a positive, finite quantity. It would be zero only if SMC were above SMB even for the very first unit.

The answer key words to hit: the socially optimal quantity is typically nonzero and finite, at the intersection of social marginal cost and social marginal benefit (demand, willingness to pay). It could be zero, but there is no reason to expect that it must be.

Private fixes from Coase and Ostrom

The slides list three kinds of remedies: private solutions (negotiation and compensation, social sanctions, education), government regulation, and taxes and subsidies.

Ronald Coase (Nobel Prize 1991) argued that if property rights are clearly defined, private negotiation will solve externalities. The people affected can bargain: the polluter pays the neighbors, or the neighbors pay the polluter to stop, until the efficient outcome is reached. The catch is that negotiation can be costly, so it helps to define property rights "in the right way." Edwards' gasoline example: if someone owned the air, they could say to every driver, you just degraded my air, and I am going to charge you for it. That would be a market solution, but it is extremely hard to set up for millions of drivers.

Elinor Ostrom (Nobel Prize 2009) studied the "tragedy of the commons," where a shared resource such as a pasture or a fishery gets overused because no one owns it. She found many communities that manage shared resources well on their own, through trust and their own rules, especially when the users interact for a long time. His summary was that "people talk to each other." In the class poll he gave three ways out of a commons problem: property rights, strong government enforcing rules that act like property rights, or trust between agents who routinely interact. He noted that global climate change has none of the three. He also said Ostrom is not likely on the exam unless the question explains it.

Pigouvian taxes and subsidies

Named after Arthur Pigou, a corrective (Pigouvian) tax makes people internalize the externality by putting it into the price. For a negative externality, charge a per-unit tax equal to the amount others suffer from each unit. For a positive one, give a per-unit subsidy equal to the amount others gain. If the tax or subsidy is the right size, private incentives line up with social ones.

The right size is the marginal external effect at the socially efficient point: Pigouvian tax = EMC at Q* and Pigouvian subsidy = EMB at Q*. If EMC is constant, the tax just equals EMC.

Graphically, a tax equal to EMC lifts the supply curve from PMC up onto SMC. Sellers now act as if they face the full social cost, so the market lands on Q* and the deadweight loss disappears. A subsidy equal to EMB lifts the demand curve from PMB up to SMB, so the market lands on the larger Q*.

Tax revenue is the tax times the new quantity: $4 × 4 = $16. Notice that with a constant EMC, the revenue ($16) equals the external cost of the 4 units still produced (4 × $4 = $16). That is a handy check, but it only holds when EMC is constant. The tax does not make the pollution disappear; it cuts quantity back to the point where the remaining units are worth their full social cost.

Why "at Q*" matters: if the external cost per unit grows as quantity grows, EMC at the market quantity Q1 is bigger than EMC at Q*. Setting the tax at the Q1 value overshoots and cuts output below Q*. Worked example 5 shows this with numbers.

Taxes, regulation, and permits in practice

  • U.S. gas taxes are earmarked for road maintenance, so they address the wear-and-tear externality only, not global warming.
  • Congestion pricing: some cities charge cars to enter crowded areas, a Pigouvian tax on the congestion each extra car imposes on everyone else. London started in 2003 and New York in 2025, and the slides show traffic moving faster in both after the charge began. You do not need the numbers; an exam case study would give them to you.
  • R&D tax credits are a subsidy to encourage innovation, which has positive spillovers, but not tied explicitly to the size of the positive externality.
  • Regulation is the most common response. Harmful products and actions are forbidden (harmful pollutants, dangerous goods, littering, speeding), and the penalty starts with a fine. A fine is economically equivalent to a tax but psychologically and socially very different. Emission limits like California's smog check are another kind. The problem is fairness when technologies vary: cutting pollution is cheap in some sectors and expensive in others. Electric cars exist but electric planes do not, so a uniform CO2 allowance could kill the high-value aviation industry.
  • Cap and trade: the government gives emitters permits for a fixed total amount of pollution, and they can trade them. Firms that can cut pollution cheaply cut more and sell their extra permits to firms for which cutting is expensive, so the cuts happen where they cost least. Everyone faces the same price of emissions. If the total number of permits is set so that the permit price equals the social marginal cost of the pollution, the system is efficient. It has been used for transitions: sulfur dioxide (acid rain) and CFCs (which deplete the ozone layer) were costly to ban overnight because substitutes take time, so they were phased out with shrinking allowances.

Climate change and the carbon tax

CO2 is a textbook negative externality: whoever burns fossil fuel gets the benefit, while the damage (extreme weather, disrupted farming, lost biodiversity) is spread around the world and into the future.

The economists' solution is a carbon tax equal to the marginal damage of CO2 (the slide cites a 2019 letter from economists in the Wall Street Journal). The hard part is measuring that marginal damage, because most of it happens in the future, so the answer depends on how much you discount the future. If people are impatient and discount the future heavily, future damage counts for little today, the estimated damage cost is small, and the optimal tax is small; on that view it is "desirable to let global warming happen." If the future is not discounted much, the optimal carbon tax is big, and unpopular.

Worked examples

Example: negative externality, from graph to deadweight loss

Demand (PMB = SMB) is P = 12 − Q. Private marginal cost (supply) is P = Q. Each unit causes $4 of pollution damage to others. Find the market outcome, the efficient outcome, the deadweight loss, and total surplus at each.

  1. Market: set demand equal to PMC. 12 − Q = Q, so Q1 = 6 and P1 = $6.
  2. Social marginal cost: SMC = PMC + EMC = Q + 4.
  3. Efficient quantity: set demand equal to SMC. 12 − Q = Q + 4, so Q* = 4, where the price would be $8.
  4. At Q1 = 6, SMC = 10 while demand is only 6, so the last units cost society more than they are worth. DWL = ½ × (6 − 4) × (10 − 6) = 4.
  5. Total surplus at Q1: CS 18 + PS 18 − external cost 24 = 12. Maximum total surplus at Q* = ½ × 4 × (12 − 4) = 16. The gap is 16 − 12 = 4, matching step 4.

Answer: The market produces 6 at $6, but the efficient quantity is 4. The market overproduces by 2 units, and the deadweight loss is 4.

Example: the Pigouvian tax that fixes it

Same market. What per-unit tax restores efficiency, and what happens to prices, revenue, and surplus?

  1. The external marginal cost is a constant $4, so the tax is $4 per unit (EMC at Q*).
  2. Sellers now need PMC + 4 = Q + 4 to supply, which is the SMC line. Set 12 − Q = Q + 4, so Q = 4 = Q*.
  3. Buyers pay the demand price at Q = 4: 12 − 4 = $8. Sellers keep $8 − $4 = $4.
  4. Tax revenue = $4 × 4 = $16.
  5. Surplus: CS = ½ × 4 × (12 − 8) = 8. PS = ½ × 4 × 4 = 8. External cost = 4 × 4 = 16. Total = 8 + 8 + 16 − 16 = 16, the maximum. DWL = 0.

Answer: A $4 tax moves the market to Q* = 4, buyers pay $8, sellers keep $4, revenue is $16, and the deadweight loss is gone.

Example: Problem Set 2, question 3(d), pollution with flat supply

Domestic oil: demand P = 5 − Q, supply MC = 1 (perfectly elastic, horizontal). Production causes pollution costing $2 per unit. (The problem calls this the marginal social cost of $2, but it is the external part; social marginal cost is 1 + 2 = 3.) Find the equilibrium, CS, PS, total external cost, and DWL without a tax, and the tax that restores efficiency.

  1. Without a tax, sellers only see their private MC of $1, so the market stays where demand meets MC: 5 − Q = 1, so Q = 4 and P = $1, the same as with no pollution.
  2. Producer surplus is zero: supply is flat at the price, so there is no area between price and supply.
  3. Consumer surplus is the triangle under demand and above $1: ½ × 4 × 4 = 8.
  4. Total external cost = $2 per unit × 4 units = $8.
  5. SMC = 1 + 2 = 3. The efficient quantity solves 5 − Q = 3, so Q* = 2.
  6. DWL is the triangle between SMC and demand from Q = 2 to Q = 4: ½ × 2 × 2 = 2. At Q = 4, social marginal cost ($3) is above willingness to pay ($1).
  7. Check: total surplus = 8 + 0 − 8 = 0. Maximum = ½ × 2 × (5 − 3) = 2. Gap = 2.
  8. The tax that restores efficiency is $2 per unit, the external marginal cost. With it, buyers pay $3, Q = 2, CS = 2, PS = 0, revenue = $4, external cost = $4, total surplus = 2.

Answer: Equilibrium (Q, P) = (4, $1); CS = 8, PS = 0, external cost = $8, DWL = 2; a $2 per-unit tax restores efficiency.

Example: Problem Set 2, question 3(f), monopoly plus pollution

Same demand, same $1 private MC, same $2 pollution cost, but now one monopolist sells all the oil. Remember the monopolist only cares about its private marginal cost. Find price, quantity, CS, PS, external cost, and DWL. Then: was it good that Standard Oil had a monopoly from 1890 to 1910?

  1. A linear demand P = 5 − Q has MR = 5 − 2Q, twice as steep with the same intercept.
  2. The monopolist sets MR = private MC: 5 − 2Q = 1, so Q = 2. It charges what demand will bear at Q = 2: P = 5 − 2 = $3.
  3. Social marginal cost is 1 + 2 = 3, and demand crosses SMC at Q = 2 as well. So the monopoly quantity equals the efficient quantity. The key calls this efficient "seemingly through magic," a lucky coincidence, but possible.
  4. CS = ½ × 2 × (5 − 3) = 2.
  5. Monopoly profit = (P − MC) × Q = (3 − 1) × 2 = $4. With zero fixed costs and a flat MC, this profit is the producer surplus. (The key says "producer surplus is still zero" in 3(e) but then says the monopolist "profits $4 as before" and counts the $4 in total surplus. Use $4.)
  6. External cost = $2 × 2 units = $4.
  7. Net social surplus = CS 2 + profit 4 − external cost 4 = 2. That equals the maximum possible (½ × 2 × 2 = 2), so DWL = 0.
  8. Standard Oil: the key does not give an official answer. A strong answer says no, you cannot conclude it was good. The match happened only because the monopoly's output cut ($2 between price and MC) was exactly the size of the pollution cost ($2). With a different external cost the monopoly would produce the wrong amount, and the monopolist cut output to raise profit, not to reduce pollution. A Pigouvian tax targets the externality directly without handing a firm market power.

Answer: Q = 2, P = $3, CS = 2, monopoly profit (producer surplus) = $4, external cost = $4, net social surplus = 2, DWL = 0. The efficiency is a coincidence, so it does not show that the Standard Oil monopoly was good.

Example: why the tax is EMC at Q*, not at the market quantity

Demand P = 12 − Q, private MC P = Q, but now the external cost per unit rises with output: EMC = Q (think of pollution that does more harm as the air gets dirtier). What tax restores efficiency?

  1. SMC = PMC + EMC = Q + Q = 2Q.
  2. Market: 12 − Q = Q, so Q1 = 6. At Q1, EMC = 6.
  3. Efficient: 12 − Q = 2Q, so Q* = 4. At Q*, EMC = 4.
  4. Try a $4 tax: sellers need Q + 4, so 12 − Q = Q + 4 gives Q = 4 = Q*. It works.
  5. Try a $6 tax (EMC at Q1): 12 − Q = Q + 6 gives Q = 3, below Q*. It overshoots and creates a new deadweight loss.

Answer: The Pigouvian tax is $4, the external marginal cost evaluated at the efficient quantity Q* = 4.

Common mistakes

Mistake: Saying the optimal amount of pollution is zero.

Correct: The optimal quantity is where social marginal cost equals social marginal benefit, which is usually positive and finite. It could be zero, but there is no reason it must be (F25 question 1(g)).

Mistake: Shifting the demand curve for pollution or the supply curve for vaccines.

Correct: A negative externality is a cost-side story: draw SMC above PMC (supply). A positive externality is a benefit-side story: draw SMB above PMB (demand).

Mistake: Drawing the deadweight loss triangle under the demand curve and above PMC, like consumer or producer surplus.

Correct: For a negative externality the triangle lies between Q* and Q1, below SMC and above demand, with its point at the social optimum. For a positive externality it lies between Q1 and Q*, below SMB and above supply.

Mistake: Thinking the market moves to SMC on its own once people learn about the pollution (on Problem Set 2 question 3(d), putting the equilibrium at Q = 2).

Correct: Without a tax or other policy, buyers and sellers still only see private costs, so the market stays at PMB = PMC: (4, $1). Q* is where society would like to be, not where the market goes.

Mistake: Reading "marginal social cost equals $2 per unit" on Problem Set 2 as SMC = 2.

Correct: The $2 is the external cost added on top of the $1 private cost, so SMC = 1 + 2 = 3. Always build SMC = PMC + EMC.

Mistake: Forgetting the external cost when computing total surplus.

Correct: Total surplus = CS + PS + tax revenue − external cost. On Problem Set 2 question 3(d) the market has CS 8 and PS 0, but total surplus is 8 − 8 = 0, not 8.

Mistake: Setting the Pigouvian tax equal to the external cost at the market quantity, or equal to the total external cost.

Correct: The tax is per unit and equals the external marginal cost at the efficient quantity Q*. When EMC is constant, that is just EMC.

Mistake: Concluding from Problem Set 2 question 3(f) that monopolies are good when there is pollution.

Correct: The monopoly was efficient only because its output cut happened to match the $2 external cost exactly, a lucky coincidence. With other numbers it is not efficient.

How it shows up on the exam

On the Fall 2025 midterm (F25 from here on), externalities appeared as one 2-point short answer, question 1(g): can there ever be an "optimal" level of polluting, or is it always zero? You had to refer to social marginal cost and social marginal benefit. The official answer says the socially optimal quantity is typically nonzero (and finite), found where social marginal cost crosses social marginal benefit (demand, or willingness to pay). It could be zero, but there is no reason it must be.

This year Edwards drilled the topic with numbers on Problem Set 2, question 3 ("Taxes, monopoly, and externalities in a nonstandard case"). Part (d) adds a $2-per-unit pollution cost to a market with demand P = 5 − Q and a flat marginal cost of $1, and asks for the equilibrium, consumer surplus, producer surplus, total external cost, deadweight loss, and the tax that restores efficiency. Part (f) combines that pollution with a monopolist. Both are fully worked below. Since he said on the Class 11 slides that topics covered more in class "will take more prominence," and the problem set is the closest thing to his exam, expect at least a short answer and quite possibly a graph or calculation.

Three things to know about scope:

  • The lecture ran out of time at Elinor Ostrom. He never said the Pigouvian tax, cap and trade, congestion pricing, or carbon tax slides out loud, but they are on the slides and the Pigouvian tax is tested on Problem Set 2 question 3(d), so learn them from this page.
  • Ostrom's trust idea is "not likely to be on the exam unless I flesh it out relatively well" (Class 11). If it shows up, the question will explain it.
  • Case studies on the exam are usually new and explained for you (Class 08). You do not need to memorize the London or New York numbers, but knowing what they show helps you answer a new case quickly.

On F25, graph parts were worth 4 points each. Full credit on an externality graph means: the external cost drawn as a gap between SMC and PMC (or the external benefit as a gap between SMB and demand), the market quantity and the efficient quantity both marked with values, and the deadweight loss triangle shaded between them, pointing toward the social optimum. For the short answer, use his words: social marginal cost, social marginal benefit, willingness to pay.

Practice questions

Externalities were only a 2-point short answer on the Fall 2025 midterm, but Problem Set 2 drilled them with numbers, so expect a short answer and possibly a graph or a calculation. Question 1 is a story question in his style; the rest are the 2-point and 4-point pieces. Do them on paper first.

12 points Waymo problems. Robotaxis are now a common sight in downtown San Francisco. Suppose daily demand for robotaxi rides downtown is P = 30 − Q and the companies' private marginal cost (supply) is P = Q, where Q is thousands of rides per day and P is dollars per ride. Each extra ride adds $6 of congestion costs to everyone else on the road: other drivers, buses, and cyclists who get stuck in more traffic.

(a) (2 points) Is this a positive or a negative externality? Which curve gets a social version that differs from the private one? Write down the social marginal cost.

(b) (4 points) I have drawn demand and private marginal cost for you below. Add the social marginal cost curve, and show the market outcome, the efficient outcome, and the deadweight loss, with values when you know them.

(c) (2 points) How big is the deadweight loss, and what is the total congestion cost imposed on others at the market outcome? Show your work.

(d) (2 points) What per-ride tax would make the market efficient? With the tax, what do riders pay, what do the companies keep, and how much revenue does the city collect each day?

(e) (2 points) A city supervisor says the only acceptable amount of robotaxi congestion downtown is zero, so robotaxis should be banned there. Is zero rides the efficient quantity? Briefly explain.

Show answer

(a) A negative externality: each ride imposes a cost on people outside the market (the riders and the companies do not pay it). It is a cost-side story, so the supply curve gets a social version: SMC = PMC + EMC = Q + 6. Demand stays as it is, since there is no external benefit, so D = PMB = SMB.

Full credit: negative; SMC lies above PMC (supply side); SMC = Q + 6.

(b) Market: buyers and sellers only count private costs, so demand = PMC: 30 − Q = Q, giving Q1 = 15 thousand rides at $15. Efficient: demand = SMC: 30 − Q = Q + 6, giving Q* = 12 thousand rides at $18. At the market quantity, the social marginal cost of the last ride is 15 + 6 = $21, far above its $15 marginal benefit.

Full credit: SMC drawn parallel to PMC and $6 above it; market (15, $15) and efficient (12, $18) marked with dotted lines to both axes; DWL triangle shaded between SMC and demand from 12 to 15, pointing at the social optimum.

(c) Deadweight loss = ½ × (15 − 12) × (21 − 15) = 9, or $9,000 per day. Total congestion cost at the market outcome = $6 × 15 thousand = $90,000 per day. Check with total surplus: at the market, consumer surplus ½ × 15 × 15 = 112.5 plus producer surplus 112.5 minus congestion cost 90 gives 135. At Q* the total is the area between demand and SMC from 0 to 12: ½ × 12 × (30 − 6) = 144. The gap is 144 − 135 = 9.

Full credit: DWL $9,000 per day and external cost $90,000 per day, with the work shown.

(d) A Pigouvian tax equal to the external marginal cost at Q*: $6 per ride. It lifts supply from PMC onto SMC, so the market lands on 12 thousand rides. Riders pay $18 (demand at Q = 12), the companies keep $18 − $6 = $12, and the city collects $6 × 12 thousand = $72,000 per day. The deadweight loss disappears.

Full credit: $6 per-ride tax; riders pay $18; companies keep $12; revenue $72,000 per day.

(e) No. The efficient quantity is where social marginal cost equals social marginal benefit, which is 12 thousand rides, not zero. The first rides are worth up to $30 to riders while their full social cost, congestion included, starts at only $6, so banning them throws away surplus: the whole area between demand and SMC up to 12, which is $144,000 per day. The optimal amount of congestion, like the optimal amount of pollution, is usually not zero.

Full credit: no; the optimum is where SMC = SMB (12 thousand rides), which is positive because the first rides are worth more than their full social cost.

2 points A campus group argues that the only acceptable level of carbon emissions from heating campus buildings is zero. From the perspective of ECON 2, can there be an optimal level of emissions above zero, or is the optimal level always zero? Refer to social marginal cost and social marginal benefit.

Show answer

There can be, and usually is. If the damage from emissions can be quantified, it raises social marginal cost above private marginal cost, and the socially optimal quantity of heating is where social marginal cost equals social marginal benefit (demand, or willingness to pay). That point is typically at a positive, finite quantity, because the first units (heat on the coldest days) are worth more than their full social cost, including the emissions. The optimum could be zero if SMC were above SMB even for the first unit, but there is no reason it must be.

Full credit: the optimum is where SMC = SMB, and that is typically nonzero and finite (it could be zero, but there is no reason it must be). This was Fall 2025 midterm question 1(g).

4 points Flu shots at a large university. Students' demand (private marginal benefit) is P = 30 − Q, where Q is thousands of shots, and the marginal cost of giving a shot is constant at $10. Each shot also gives $6 of benefit to other people by cutting the spread of flu. Draw the graph: demand, social marginal benefit, and marginal cost. Find the market quantity, the efficient quantity, and the deadweight loss, and name the per-unit policy that fixes it and its total cost.

Show answer

Market: demand = MC, 30 − Q = 10, so Q1 = 20 thousand shots. Each shot also protects other people, so SMB = PMB + EMB = 36 − Q. Efficient: SMB = MC, 36 − Q = 10, so Q* = 26 thousand shots. The market underproduces.

Deadweight loss: the triangle between Q = 20 and Q = 26, below SMB and above MC. At Q = 20 the gap is SMB − MC = 16 − 10 = 6, so DWL = ½ × 6 × 6 = 18, or $18,000.

Fix: a Pigouvian subsidy of $6 per shot, the external marginal benefit at Q*. It lifts the demand curve from PMB up to SMB. Supply is flat, so providers still get $10 and students pay $10 − $6 = $4; at $4, demand gives 30 − 4 = 26 thousand shots. Subsidy cost = $6 × 26 thousand = $156,000.

Full credit: SMB drawn $6 above demand (a benefit-side shift, not a supply shift); Q1 = 20 thousand and Q* = 26 thousand marked; DWL $18,000 shaded between SMB and MC from 20 to 26; a $6 per-shot subsidy costing $156,000.

2 points Same flu shot market. Instead of a $6 subsidy, the university makes flu shots completely free to students by paying providers the full $10. How many shots are given? Is free better than the $6 subsidy, in the efficient sense? Briefly explain.

Show answer

At a price of $0, students take shots until their private marginal benefit is zero: 30 − Q = 0, so Q = 30 thousand. That is more than the efficient 26 thousand. For shots 26 thousand to 30 thousand, the social marginal benefit (from $10 down to $6) is below the $10 marginal cost, so there is a new deadweight loss of ½ × (30 − 26) × (10 − 6) = 8, or $8,000. The cost to the university rises to $10 × 30 thousand = $300,000. In this model free overshoots; the subsidy should equal the external benefit, $6. (If you argue the external benefit is larger than $6, for example during a bad outbreak, a reasoned answer like that also earns credit.)

Full credit: 30 thousand shots, more than the efficient 26 thousand, so free overshoots and creates a deadweight loss ($8,000); the efficient subsidy equals the external marginal benefit.

2 points In the taxes and subsidies unit you learned that a subsidy creates deadweight loss. Why does the $6 flu shot subsidy reduce deadweight loss instead? Briefly explain.

Show answer

In the earlier unit, the market without the subsidy was already efficient: demand measured the full benefit, so pushing quantity past the equilibrium meant units whose marginal benefit was below marginal cost. With flu shots, demand leaves out the benefit to other people, so the unsubsidized market stops too early, at 20 thousand, while the social marginal benefit is still above marginal cost. A subsidy equal to the external marginal benefit moves the market to 26 thousand, where social marginal benefit equals marginal cost, which removes the deadweight loss instead of creating one.

Full credit: without an externality the market is already at MB = MC, so a subsidy overshoots; with a positive externality the market underproduces, and a subsidy equal to EMB moves it to SMB = MC.

4 points One company owns the only cement plant in the region. Weekly demand is P = 12 − Q, where Q is thousands of bags and P is dollars per bag. The private marginal cost is constant at $2 per bag, and each bag's production causes $4 of air pollution damage to nearby residents. Find the monopoly quantity and price, the efficient quantity, and the deadweight loss. Is the monopoly outcome efficient, as it was in Problem Set 2, question 3(f)? Show your work.

Show answer

Monopoly: MR = 12 − 2Q. The monopolist only counts its private cost, so it sets MR = PMC: 12 − 2Q = 2, giving Q = 5 thousand bags, and charges what demand will bear: P = 12 − 5 = $7.

Efficient: SMC = PMC + EMC = 2 + 4 = 6. Demand = SMC: 12 − Q = 6, so Q* = 6 thousand bags at $6. The monopoly now produces too little, even with the pollution counted.

Deadweight loss: the small triangle between demand and SMC from 5 to 6: ½ × (6 − 5) × (7 − 6) = 0.5, or $500 per week. Check: consumer surplus ½ × 5 × 5 = 12.5, plus profit (7 − 2) × 5 = 25, minus pollution 4 × 5 = 20, gives 17.5, compared with the maximum ½ × 6 × (12 − 6) = 18.

A competitive industry would produce where demand = PMC: Q = 10 thousand at $2, with deadweight loss ½ × (10 − 6) × (6 − 2) = 8, or $8,000 per week. So here the monopoly lands closer to the efficient quantity, but it is not efficient. In Problem Set 2, question 3(f), the monopoly was exactly efficient only because its markup ($3 − $1 = $2) happened to equal the $2 external cost, which Edwards called a lucky coincidence. Here the monopolist's markup is $7 − $2 = $5, more than the $4 external cost, so it cuts output a little too far.

Full credit: Q = 5 thousand and P = $7 from MR = private MC; SMC = $6 and Q* = 6 thousand; DWL $500 per week; not efficient (the monopoly underproduces), with the reason that Problem Set 2 only worked because the markup equaled the external cost.

2 points In the cement question, the city's economist proposes the standard Pigouvian tax of $4 per bag, equal to the external marginal cost. Would that make the monopoly outcome efficient? Briefly explain.

Show answer

No, it would make things worse. With the tax, the monopolist's private cost becomes $2 + $4 = $6, so it sets MR = 6: 12 − 2Q = 6, giving Q = 3 thousand bags at P = $9. The efficient quantity is still 6 thousand, so output moves further away from it, and the deadweight loss grows from $500 to ½ × (6 − 3) × (9 − 6) = 4.5, or $4,500 per week. The Pigouvian tax rule assumes a competitive market, where price equals private marginal cost. A monopolist already cuts output to raise its price, so taxing it by the full external cost cuts output too much.

Full credit: no; the tax cuts the monopoly quantity to 3 thousand at $9, further below Q* = 6 thousand, because the monopolist already restricts output; the full-EMC tax rule works for a competitive market.

2 points A factory's demand is P = 24 − Q and its private marginal cost is P = Q. The pollution damage from each extra unit grows as the air gets dirtier: the external marginal cost is EMC = Q. A regulator sets the Pigouvian tax equal to the external marginal cost at the current market quantity. Is that the right tax? Show your work.

Show answer

No. SMC = PMC + EMC = 2Q. The market quantity is where 24 − Q = Q, so Q1 = 12, where EMC = $12. The efficient quantity is where 24 − Q = 2Q, so Q* = 8, where EMC = $8. A $12 tax makes sellers need Q + 12, so 24 − Q = Q + 12 and Q = 6, below Q*: it overshoots and creates a new deadweight loss. The right tax is the external marginal cost at the efficient quantity, $8: then 24 − Q = Q + 8 gives Q = 8 = Q*.

Full credit: Q1 = 12, Q* = 8; the correct tax is EMC at Q*, $8, and the $12 tax overshoots to Q = 6.

2 points For each case, say whether it is a positive or negative externality and which curve (supply or demand) gets a social version: (i) a student parks an e-scooter in a dorm stairwell, where someone could trip over it during a fire evacuation; (ii) a neighbor's beehives pollinate the fruit trees in your backyard.

Show answer

(i) Negative externality. The scooter's price included no insurance for the trip hazard it creates for other people, so the cost lands on people outside the market. It is a cost-side story: social marginal cost lies above private marginal cost (the supply side). (ii) Positive externality. The pollination benefits you, but you are not part of the market for honey or hives. It is a benefit-side story: social marginal benefit lies above private marginal benefit (the demand side).

Full credit: (i) negative, SMC above PMC; (ii) positive, SMB above PMB.

2 points A fraternity's late-night parties keep the graduate student next door awake. Using the Coase theorem, explain how this externality could be solved without the government, and why it is easier here than for climate change.

Show answer

Coase says that if property rights are clearly defined and bargaining is cheap, the people involved can negotiate to the efficient outcome. If the neighbor has a right to quiet, the fraternity can pay her (or soundproof, or end parties earlier) to be allowed some noise; if the fraternity has the right to party, she can pay it to quiet down on nights before her exams. Either way they settle where the last hour of partying is worth as much to the fraternity as it costs the neighbor. It works here because only two parties need to bargain. With climate change, billions of people are affected and nobody owns the atmosphere, so bargaining is impossible in practice.

Full credit: clear property rights plus private negotiation or compensation reaches the efficient outcome; it needs few parties and low bargaining costs, which climate change lacks.

2 points Why can a cap-and-trade system cut emissions at lower total cost than a rule requiring every firm to cut its emissions by the same amount? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Cutting emissions is cheap for some firms and very expensive for others (electric cars exist, but electric planes do not). With tradable permits, firms that can cut cheaply cut more and sell their spare permits to firms for which cutting is expensive. Every firm faces the same price for emitting, so the total cut happens where it costs least. A uniform rule ignores those cost differences and forces expensive cuts on some firms. If the cap is set so that the permit price equals the social marginal cost of the pollution, cap and trade is efficient.

Full credit: everyone faces the same permit price, so cuts happen where they are cheapest; a uniform rule ignores different costs of cutting.

2 points Economists agree that a carbon tax should equal the marginal damage of CO2, yet their recommended tax levels differ a lot. Briefly explain one big reason why.

Show answer

Most of the damage from CO2 happens in the future, so its value today depends on how much the future is discounted. An economist who discounts the future heavily counts future damage as small today, so the estimated marginal damage, and the optimal tax, is small. One who discounts little counts future damage almost fully, so the optimal carbon tax is large (and unpopular).

Full credit: most damage is in the future; heavy discounting gives a small tax and little discounting gives a big one.

One-minute review

  • Externality: a cost or benefit that lands on people outside the market; the price leaves it out.
  • SMC = PMC + EMC; SMB = PMB + EMB. Market: PMB = PMC. Efficient Q*: SMB = SMC.
  • Negative externality: supply side, SMC above PMC, market overproduces. Positive: demand side, SMB above PMB, market underproduces.
  • DWL triangle lies between Q1 and Q*, between the social curve and the other curve, pointing at the social optimum. With constant EMC, DWL = ½ × EMC × (Q1 − Q*).
  • Total surplus = CS + PS + tax revenue − external cost. Max total surplus is the area between demand and SMC up to Q*.
  • Optimal pollution is usually not zero: it is where SMC = SMB (F25 question 1(g)).
  • Pigouvian tax = EMC at Q*; subsidy = EMB at Q*. The tax lifts supply onto SMC. Revenue = tax × Q*.
  • Problem Set 2 question 3(d): market (4, $1), CS 8, PS 0, external cost 8, Q* = 2, DWL 2, $2 tax. Question 3(f): monopoly Q = 2, P = $3, efficient by coincidence, profit 4, external cost 4, net surplus 2.
  • Coase: clear property rights plus cheap bargaining. Ostrom: trust and long-run cooperation (only if the exam explains it). Cap and trade: same permit price for everyone; set the cap so price = social MC.
  • Carbon tax = marginal damage of CO2; heavy discounting of the future means a small tax, little discounting means a big one.
  • Monopoly plus pollution: the monopolist sets MR = private MC. It is efficient only if its markup happens to equal the external cost (Problem Set 2, question 3(f)); a full Pigouvian tax on a monopolist can cut output too far.
