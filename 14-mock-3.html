Practice midterm C

Practice midterm C: tariffs, patents, and spillovers

A complete 60-point midterm in the same format as Edwards' Fall 2025 midterm, with new stories: an avocado tariff, a patented flu vaccine patch, and eight short answers. Every part has a full model answer, accurate graphs, and a "Full credit" line so you can grade yourself.

How to use this page

Take it like the real exam. Set a 60-minute timer, work on paper, and do not open any answer until time is up. The Fall 2025 midterm gave about one minute per point, so budget roughly 16 minutes for Question 1, 24 minutes for Question 2, and 18 minutes for Question 3. Every graphing part shows the axes you would get printed on the exam; copy them onto paper (or print this page) and draw on them.

This mock leans on things the other practice exams touch less: a tax wedge where you must compute both prices, revenue, and deadweight loss from equations; the reverse use of elasticity (predict the quantity change from a price change); profit when marginal cost slopes up; and the demand-side story of a positive externality.

Question 1: Short and Sather (16 points)

Eight parts, 2 points each. Name the answer, then give the reason in course vocabulary.

2 points 1(a). At the Cheese Board, your willingness to pay is $8 for your first slice of pizza, $5 for a second slice, and $2 for a third. If a slice costs $4, how many slices do you buy? Use this example to briefly explain why demand curves slope downward.

Show answer

You buy 2 slices. The first two slices are worth more to you than the $4 price ($8 and $5), and the third is worth only $2, less than it costs. If the price fell to $1.50 you would buy all 3, so a lower price means a larger quantity demanded.

That is the downward slope. The height of the demand curve at each quantity is the marginal benefit (willingness to pay) of that unit, and marginal benefit falls as you consume more because of diminishing marginal utility. More is better, but at a decreasing rate, so buyers only take more units when the price drops to the lower value of those extra units.

Full credit: 2 slices, plus "demand slopes down because marginal benefit (willingness to pay, marginal utility) falls with each additional unit." Saying only "people buy less when it costs more" restates the slope without the reason.

2 points 1(b). A strawberry grower at the Berkeley Farmers' Market can bring baskets to market at a marginal cost of $2 for the first basket, $4 for the second, and $6 for the third. Baskets sell for $6. Why is the area above the supply curve and below the price called producer surplus? Use the numbers.

Show answer

The supply curve is the marginal cost curve, so its height at each unit is what it costs the seller to produce that unit. The seller receives the market price for every unit, so the surplus on each unit is price minus marginal cost, and adding those gaps across all units gives the area between the price line and the supply curve.

Here the grower sells 3 baskets (the third has a marginal cost equal to the $6 price). Surplus is ($6 − $2) + ($6 − $4) + ($6 − $6) = $4 + $2 + $0 = $6. That $6 is the gain the grower gets from selling at the market price above what each basket costs to bring.

Full credit: "supply is marginal cost, the seller gets the price on every unit, so producer surplus is price minus marginal cost summed over the units sold," with $6 from the numbers.

2 points 1(c). You spend $60 a week on burritos and boba. Burritos cost $12 and boba drinks cost $6. Draw your budget line with burritos on the vertical axis and boba on the horizontal axis. Then the price of boba falls to $4. How does the budget line change? Label intercepts.

Show answer
  1. Burrito (vertical) intercept: $60 ÷ $12 = 5 burritos. Boba (horizontal) intercept: $60 ÷ $6 = 10 drinks. Slope = −(price of boba ÷ price of burritos) = −$6 ÷ $12 = −0.5, so each boba costs half a burrito.
  2. After the price cut, the boba intercept becomes $60 ÷ $4 = 15 drinks. The burrito intercept stays at 5, because if you spend everything on burritos the price of boba does not matter.
  3. The line pivots outward around the burrito intercept and gets flatter. The new slope is −$4 ÷ $12 = −1/3.

Full credit: burrito intercept 5 unchanged, boba intercept moves from 10 to 15, the line pivots out around the burrito intercept and becomes flatter. A parallel shift is the classic mistake; that only happens when income changes.

2 points 1(d). Let i be ice cream and o be everything else. You are optimizing, so mui ÷ pi = muo ÷ po. A heat wave hits Berkeley and makes every scoop of ice cream more enjoyable than before. Prices do not change. How do you change your purchases to restore the equality? (Hint: think about which curve shifts.)

Show answer

The heat wave raises mui at every quantity, so the left side is now bigger than the right side: a dollar spent on ice cream buys more marginal utility than a dollar spent on anything else. Buy more ice cream, which lowers mui through diminishing marginal utility, and buy less of other goods, which raises muo, until the two sides are equal again.

Because the price did not change, this is a shift of your demand curve for ice cream out to the right, not a movement along it. You want more ice cream at every price.

Full credit: "mui rises, so the left side is bigger; buy more ice cream (and less of other goods) until diminishing marginal utility brings mui back down." Mentioning that demand for ice cream shifts out is a strong extra.

2 points 1(e). The market price of milk is $4 a gallon. The government sets a minimum price for milk of $3 a gallon, and a classmate says this will cause a surplus of milk. Is she right? What would a minimum price of $5 do? Be careful.

Show answer

She is wrong about the $3 minimum. A price floor only matters if it is set above the equilibrium price. At $3 the market can still settle at $4, so nothing changes: no surplus and no deadweight loss.

A $5 minimum is binding. At $5, dairies want to sell more than buyers want to buy, so there is a surplus (quantity supplied minus quantity demanded). Only the quantity demanded at $5 actually gets bought, which is less than the equilibrium quantity. The units between that lower quantity and the equilibrium quantity, which buyers valued above their marginal cost, are no longer traded, so there is a deadweight loss.

Full credit: "$3 is below equilibrium, so it does not bind and nothing happens; $5 binds and creates a surplus, with quantity traded equal to quantity demanded at $5 and a deadweight loss."

2 points 1(f). Berkeley pays pizza sellers a subsidy of $2 for every pizza sold, and weekly sales rise from 10,000 to 12,000 pizzas. Assume pizza has no externalities. Buyers and sellers both gain, so why does the subsidy create a deadweight loss? How large is it?

Show answer

The subsidy pushes quantity above the competitive equilibrium. For the extra 2,000 pizzas, the marginal cost of making them is higher than buyers' marginal benefit, so society spends more producing them than they are worth. The city pays $2 × 12,000 = $24,000 a week, and the gains to buyers and sellers add up to less than that; the gap is the deadweight loss.

With straight-line curves, DWL = ½ × subsidy × (Q2 − Q1) = ½ × $2 × 2,000 = $2,000 per week. On a graph it is the triangle between supply (above) and demand (below) from 10,000 to 12,000, pointing back toward the original equilibrium.

Full credit: "the units from 10,000 to 12,000 have marginal cost above marginal benefit" (or "the subsidy costs more than buyers and sellers gain") and DWL = $2,000 per week.

2 points 1(g). Car exhaust in the Bay Area causes air pollution. Should the efficient amount of driving pollution be zero? Refer to social marginal cost and social marginal benefit.

Show answer

Usually not. The efficient quantity is where social marginal benefit equals social marginal cost, and that is typically a positive, finite amount. The first miles driven (getting to work, hauling groceries, ambulances) have a social marginal benefit well above their social marginal cost, including the pollution damage, so cutting them would make society worse off.

The market without intervention goes past the optimum, because drivers ignore the external cost, so driving should fall, but not to zero. Zero would only be efficient if the social marginal cost of even the first mile exceeded its benefit.

Full credit: "the optimum is where SMB = SMC, which is usually a positive quantity; it could be zero only if SMC exceeds SMB even for the first unit."

2 points 1(h). Roommates Ana and Ben split chores. In one hour Ana can cook 2 meals or do 4 loads of laundry. Ben can cook 1 meal or do 2 loads. Who should specialize in what? (Hint: this is tricky.)

Show answer

Neither has a comparative advantage. Ana's opportunity cost of one meal is 4 ÷ 2 = 2 loads of laundry, and Ben's is 2 ÷ 1 = 2 loads, exactly the same (and both give up ½ meal per load). Ana has an absolute advantage in both chores, but comparative advantage depends on opportunity costs, and theirs are equal.

So there are no gains from specialization: any division of chores produces the same total. Edwards' slide puts it this way: the size of the gains from specialization depends on the difference in opportunity costs, and here the difference is zero. This is the same idea as Bill and Robinson in Problem Set 1, who both gave up 1 fish per coconut.

Full credit: "equal opportunity costs (2 loads per meal for both), so neither has a comparative advantage and specializing gains nothing; any split works."

Question 2: Between a guac and a hard place (24 points)

Most avocados sold in the U.S. are imported, much of them from Mexico. For this question, treat every avocado sold in the U.S. as imported and ignore California growers. Guacamole and avocado toast have no close substitute, but avocados are a side ingredient that shoppers can skip.

2 points 2(a). What is the likely shape of U.S. demand for avocados: horizontal, vertical, or in between? Briefly explain.

Show answer

In between, and fairly steep (relatively inelastic). Demand is inelastic when there is no close substitute, and nothing really replaces avocado in guacamole, so many buyers keep buying when the price rises. It is not vertical, though, because avocado is something people can do without: some shoppers will skip the guacamole or buy fewer when the price goes up.

Full credit: a reasoned answer tied to substitutes. "Relatively inelastic, because there is no close substitute, but not perfectly inelastic, because people can cut back" is the cleanest version.

2 points 2(b). Foreign growers can sell their avocados in the U.S. or in other countries (Canada, Europe, Japan, or at home). What does that suggest about the shape of the supply of avocados to the U.S.? (Hint: think about the opportunity cost of shipping one more pound to the U.S.)

Show answer

Supply to the U.S. should be fairly flat (relatively elastic). The marginal cost of sending one more pound to the U.S. includes the price the grower gives up by not selling it elsewhere. If the price growers receive in the U.S. falls a little, they ship more avocados to other markets, so the quantity supplied to the U.S. responds strongly to price.

The total harvest is fixed in the short run (trees take years to grow), which by itself would make supply steep. The point is that supply to one country can still be elastic, because sellers can redirect the fixed harvest. Edwards made the same argument about gasoline: supply to a gas station reflects the world oil price, so it is very elastic.

Full credit: "relatively elastic (flat), because growers have other places to sell, so a lower U.S. price diverts avocados elsewhere."

For the rest of Question 2, suppose U.S. demand is P = 5 − Q and the supply of imported avocados is P = 0.5 + 0.5Q, where Q is billions of pounds per year and P is dollars per pound.

4 points 2(c). On the axes below, draw demand and supply. Label the equilibrium price and quantity, and shade and give values for consumer surplus and producer surplus. Show your work.

Show answer
  1. Set demand equal to supply: 5 − Q = 0.5 + 0.5Q, so 4.5 = 1.5Q and Q = 3 billion pounds. Price: P = 5 − 3 = $2 per pound. (Check with supply: 0.5 + 0.5 × 3 = $2.)
  2. Demand runs from $5 on the price axis to 5 billion pounds on the quantity axis. Supply starts at $0.50 and rises $0.50 per billion pounds, through (3, $2) and up to $3.50 at 6 billion pounds.
  3. Consumer surplus is the triangle below demand and above $2: ½ × 3 × ($5 − $2) = $4.5 billion.
  4. Producer surplus is the triangle above supply and below $2: ½ × 3 × ($2 − $0.50) = $2.25 billion.

Full credit: both curves through the right intercepts, equilibrium (3 billion pounds, $2) marked with dotted lines to both axes, CS = $4.5 billion and PS = $2.25 billion shaded and labeled. Demand should look steeper than supply, matching 2(a) and 2(b).

2 points 2(d). Use ε = (1 ÷ slope) × (P ÷ Q) to find the price elasticity of demand and the price elasticity of supply at the equilibrium. Are they consistent with your answers to 2(a) and 2(b)?

Show answer
  1. Demand slope is −1, so εD = (1 ÷ −1) × (2 ÷ 3) = −2/3 ≈ −0.67. The magnitude is below 1: relatively inelastic.
  2. Supply slope is 0.5, so εS = (1 ÷ 0.5) × (2 ÷ 3) = 4/3 ≈ 1.33. Above 1: relatively elastic.

Yes. Demand is inelastic, as 2(a) argued, and supply is elastic, as 2(b) argued. Supply is twice as elastic as demand at this point, which will matter for who pays the tariff.

Full credit: −0.67 (keep the negative sign) and 1.33, with "demand inelastic, supply elastic, consistent with (a) and (b)."

4 points 2(e). The U.S. puts a tariff of $1.50 per pound on imported avocados, collected from importers at the border. On the axes below, show the effect of the tariff with values when you know them: the new quantity, the price buyers pay, the price foreign growers keep, tariff revenue, and deadweight loss.

Show answer
  1. A tariff is a per-unit tax, so it drives a vertical wedge of $1.50 between the price buyers pay (on demand) and the price growers keep (on supply). Find the quantity where the gap between the curves is $1.50: (5 − Q) − (0.5 + 0.5Q) = 1.5, so 4.5 − 1.5Q = 1.5 and Q = 2 billion pounds.
  2. Buyers pay the demand price at Q = 2: 5 − 2 = $3.00. Growers keep the supply price: 0.5 + 0.5 × 2 = $1.50. Check: $3.00 − $1.50 = $1.50, the tariff.
  3. Tariff revenue is the rectangle from $1.50 to $3.00, out to Q = 2: $1.50 × 2 billion = $3 billion.
  4. Deadweight loss is the triangle between demand and supply from Q = 2 to Q = 3, pointing at the old equilibrium: ½ × $1.50 × (3 − 2) = $0.75 billion, or $750 million.

Full credit: the wedge drawn at Q = 2 between $3.00 on demand and $1.50 on supply, revenue rectangle labeled $3 billion, DWL triangle between Q = 2 and Q = 3 labeled $0.75 billion. Do not shift a curve and forget to show the two different prices; the two prices are the point of the graph.

4 points 2(f). A senator says, "Mexico will pay for this tariff." Using your numbers, who actually bears the burden of the tariff, and why? Would your answer change if the tariff were collected from U.S. grocery stores instead of importers? Explain fully.

Show answer

Mostly U.S. buyers, not foreign growers. The price buyers pay rises from $2.00 to $3.00, so they bear $1.00 of the $1.50 tariff (two thirds). The price growers keep falls from $2.00 to $1.50, so they bear $0.50 (one third). On the 2 billion pounds still sold, that is $1 × 2 billion = $2 billion paid by U.S. buyers and $0.50 × 2 billion = $1 billion paid by foreign growers, which adds up to the $3 billion of revenue.

The reason is elasticity: a tax falls more heavily on the more inelastic side of the market. U.S. demand (−0.67) is less elastic than supply to the U.S. (1.33), because buyers have no close substitute while growers can sell elsewhere. Growers can walk away; guacamole lovers mostly cannot. So the senator is only one-third right.

Collecting the tariff from grocery stores instead would change nothing. Who writes the check to the government does not matter: the same $1.50 wedge appears between what buyers pay and what sellers keep, so the equilibrium, the two prices, and the burden are identical.

Full credit: buyers pay $1 of the $1.50 (two thirds), growers $0.50 (one third), because demand is more inelastic than supply; and "no change, the side the tax is collected from does not matter."

2 points 2(g). After the tariff, a headline reads "Avocado prices up 50%." Using your demand elasticity from 2(d), predict the percent change in the quantity of avocados bought. Does it match your graph?

Show answer

Rearrange the elasticity formula: %ΔQ = ε × %ΔP = −2/3 × 50% ≈ −33%. Quantity should fall by about a third.

It matches. On the graph, the price rose from $2 to $3 (+50%) and quantity fell from 3 to 2 billion pounds, which is −1 ÷ 3 ≈ −33%. It lines up exactly here because demand is a straight line and both the elasticity and the percent changes are measured from the starting point, the way Edwards' key computes them.

Full credit: about −33% from −0.67 × 50%, with "yes, 3 to 2 billion pounds is a one-third drop."

4 points 2(h). The tariff raises $3 billion for the U.S. government. Show that U.S. buyers and foreign growers together lose more than $3 billion. How much do they lose, and what happened to the difference? Explain fully.

Show answer
  1. Consumer surplus after the tariff: ½ × 2 × ($5 − $3) = $2 billion, down from $4.5 billion. Buyers lose $2.5 billion.
  2. Producer surplus after the tariff: ½ × 2 × ($1.50 − $0.50) = $1 billion, down from $2.25 billion. Growers lose $1.25 billion.
  3. Total loss: $2.5 billion + $1.25 billion = $3.75 billion. The government collects $3 billion of it.
  4. The other $0.75 billion is the deadweight loss from 2(e). Nobody receives it.

The deadweight loss comes from the 1 billion pounds of avocados (from 2 to 3 billion) that are no longer sold. For each of those pounds, buyers valued the avocado more than it cost growers to supply it, so trading it would have created surplus. The tariff makes buyers pay more than that value, so the trades never happen, and that surplus disappears instead of being transferred to anyone.

Full credit: buyers lose $2.5 billion and growers lose $1.25 billion, $3.75 billion in total; $3 billion becomes revenue and the remaining $0.75 billion is deadweight loss from avocados that buyers valued above their marginal cost but no longer buy.

Question 3: Patch me if you can (18 points)

Golden Bear Bio, a made-up Berkeley startup, invents a needle-free flu vaccine patch and gets a patent on it. Many people who skip flu shots because they hate needles would use it, so treat the patch as its own market. Demand is P = 60 − Q and marginal cost is MC = Q, where Q is millions of patches per flu season and P is dollars per patch. Developing the patch cost $1.2 billion, which is already spent. Apart from that, fixed costs are zero.

2 points 3(a). Is the market for the patch perfectly competitive, or does Golden Bear Bio have market power? Briefly explain the role of the patent.

Show answer

Golden Bear Bio has market power: it is a monopoly. The patent is a legal barrier to entry, so no other firm can make the patch even if it could copy the technology, and the patch has no close substitute for people who refuse needles. As the only seller, the firm faces the whole downward-sloping market demand curve, so it chooses quantity knowing that selling more means lowering the price.

Full credit: "market power (monopoly), because the patent is a barrier to entry that keeps competitors out."

4 points 3(b). I have drawn the marginal cost curve for you. Add the curves you need to show the monopoly equilibrium, and label the quantity and price with values. Shade the deadweight loss and give its value.

Show answer
  1. Draw demand from $60 on the price axis to 60 million on the quantity axis.
  2. Draw marginal revenue from the same $60 intercept, twice as steep, hitting the quantity axis at 30 million: MR = 60 − 2Q.
  3. Set MR = MC: 60 − 2Q = Q, so Q = 20 million patches. (Marginal cost there is $20.)
  4. Read the price off demand, not off MC: P = 60 − 20 = $40 per patch.
  5. The efficient quantity is where demand meets MC: 60 − Q = Q, so Q = 30 million at $30.
  6. Deadweight loss is the triangle between demand and MC from 20 to 30 million, pointing at the efficient point: ½ × ($40 − $20) × (30 − 20) = $100 million.

Full credit: demand and MR (twice as steep, from $60 to 30 million) drawn, MR = MC at 20 million, price $40 read up on demand, DWL triangle from 20 to 30 million labeled $100 million. Reading the price at the MR = MC point ($20) is the most common way to lose points.

3 points 3(c). What is Golden Bear Bio's profit in one flu season, not counting the development cost? How many flu seasons of that profit does it take to earn back the $1.2 billion development cost? Show your work.

Show answer
  1. Total revenue: $40 × 20 million = $800 million.
  2. Total cost with zero fixed cost is the area under the MC curve from 0 to 20 million, a triangle: ½ × 20 million × $20 = $200 million.
  3. Profit: $800 million − $200 million = $600 million per season.
  4. Seasons to earn back development: $1.2 billion ÷ $600 million = 2 seasons.

Careful: the rectangle (P − MC at Q) × Q = ($40 − $20) × 20 million = $400 million is wrong here. It treats every patch as costing $20, but marginal cost rises from $0, so the earlier patches were cheaper. This is exactly the trap on Fall 2025 question 3(f), where Edwards wrote that he got it wrong at first.

Full credit: $600 million (TR $800 million minus the $200 million triangle under MC) and 2 seasons. Expect partial credit at most for $400 million.

2 points 3(d). When the patent expires, many generic makers enter and the market becomes perfectly competitive with the same marginal cost. What happens to the price and quantity of patches?

Show answer

Competitive supply is the marginal cost curve, so the market settles where demand meets MC: 60 − Q = Q, so Q = 30 million patches and P = $30. The price falls from $40 to $30 and the quantity rises from 20 to 30 million. The $100 million monopoly deadweight loss disappears, and consumer surplus grows from ½ × 20 × $20 = $200 million to ½ × 30 × $30 = $450 million.

Full credit: price falls to $30 and quantity rises to 30 million, where demand equals marginal cost.

Flu vaccines also protect people who do not buy them: every vaccinated person makes it less likely that roommates, classmates, and strangers catch the flu. Suppose this external benefit is worth $20 per patch.

2 points 3(e). Even after the patent expires, why does the competitive market from 3(d) sell too few patches? Which curve does the externality affect?

Show answer

Buyers decide based on their own private marginal benefit (the demand curve) and ignore the $20 benefit to other people. Social marginal benefit is private marginal benefit plus external marginal benefit, so it lies $20 above demand: SMB = 80 − Q. At the market quantity of 30 million, SMB is $50 while marginal cost is only $30, so more patches would be worth more to society than they cost to make. A positive externality is a story about the demand (benefit) side, so the market underproduces.

Full credit: "positive externality: SMB is above private MB (demand) by $20, buyers ignore the benefit to others, so at the market quantity SMB > MC and the market produces too little."

3 points 3(f). What Pigouvian subsidy per patch makes the competitive market efficient? Find the efficient quantity, the price buyers pay, the price sellers receive, and what the subsidy costs the government. How large was the deadweight loss without it? Show your work.

Show answer
  1. A Pigouvian subsidy equals the external marginal benefit at the efficient quantity: $20 per patch.
  2. Efficient quantity: SMB = MC, so 80 − Q = Q and Q* = 40 million patches.
  3. Sellers receive marginal cost at 40 million: $40. Buyers pay the demand price at 40 million: 60 − 40 = $20. The $20 gap is the subsidy.
  4. Subsidy cost: $20 × 40 million = $800 million.
  5. Deadweight loss without the subsidy: the triangle between SMB and MC from 30 to 40 million, ½ × ($50 − $30) × (40 − 30) = $100 million. The subsidy eliminates it.

Unlike the pizza subsidy in 1(f), this subsidy raises efficiency, because here the market started below the efficient quantity. It is the logic behind cheap or free flu shots; Edwards pointed out in class that flu shots are free for students on SHIP.

Full credit: $20 subsidy, Q* = 40 million, buyers pay $20, sellers get $40, cost $800 million, and the $100 million deadweight loss removed.

2 points 3(g). A classmate says patents are bad because they create monopoly deadweight loss, so the government should never grant them. Briefly explain the tradeoff.

Show answer

Without a patent, competitors would copy the patch as soon as it came out, the price would fall to marginal cost, and Golden Bear Bio could never earn back the $1.2 billion it spent developing it. Knowing that, it might never have invented the patch, and then society would lose all the surplus the patch creates, not just a deadweight loss triangle. Patents trade some deadweight loss for the incentive to innovate.

The cost of that trade is real, though. Counting the $20 external benefit, monopoly output of 20 million instead of the efficient 40 million loses ½ × ($60 − $20) × (40 − 20) = $400 million per season, and the firm earns back its development cost in just 2 seasons. That is why the length and strength of patents is debated, as Edwards' Humira example showed.

Full credit: "patents create deadweight loss, but they let inventors earn profits that cover high development (fixed) costs; without them, many products would never be invented."
