Market equilibrium and surplus

Label: Write D = MB = WTP, S = MC, P1, Q1, CS and PS. At a given price, Qd is what buyers want and Qs is what sellers offer.

  1. Draw D and S crossing at P1, Q1. Shade CS and PS.
  • CS is ½ × Q1 × (the price where D hits the P axis − P1).
  • PS is ½ × Q1 × (P1 − the price where S hits the P axis).
  • At Q1, CS + PS is the largest it can be, so DWL is 0.
  • Above P1, sellers offer more than buyers want, so the price falls. Below P1, buyers want more, so the price rises.

Shifting demand and supply

Label: Mark D1, D2 (or S1, S2), the arrow, and P1, Q1, P2, Q2.

  1. Buyers' income or tastes shift D; sellers' costs shift S.
  2. Draw the shift with an arrow and mark the new P2 and Q2.
  • If D shifts right, P and Q both rise; left does the opposite.
  • If S shifts right, P falls and Q rises; left does the opposite.
  • If both shift, only P or only Q is certain. For D right and S left, P rises; the change in Q depends on which shift is bigger.
  • A change in the good's own price is a move along the curves.

Elasticity: steep versus flat demand

Label: Mark steep D, flat D, S1, S2, P1, Q1, P2 and Q2.

  1. Draw the same shift from S1 to S2 on both graphs.
  2. Steep D makes P change a lot and Q a little; flat D does the reverse.
  • Elasticity ε = % change in Q ÷ % change in P. Use its size |ε|.
  • On a straight D, ε at a point = (1 ÷ slope of D) × (P ÷ Q).
  • If |ε| > 1, demand is elastic. If |ε| < 1, demand is inelastic.
  • Raising P raises spending P × Q if demand is inelastic.

Price ceiling and price floor

Label: Pceil is a legal top price; Pfloor is a legal bottom price.

  1. Draw D and S (neither curve shifts), then a flat line at the legal price. Read Qs on S and Qd on D at that price.
  • Only the smaller amount is sold: Qs with a ceiling (shortage = Qd − Qs), and Qd with a floor (surplus = Qs − Qd).
  • DWL = ½ × (Q1 − the amount sold) × (the gap between D and S at the amount sold).
  • With a ceiling, sellers lose. Buyers gain on price but lose on the units no longer sold.

Per-unit tax

Label: Mark D = MB, S = MC, S + t, P1, Q1, Q2, Pbuyers and Psellers.

  1. Draw D and S crossing at P1, Q1, then S + t (S moved up by t).
  2. S + t meets D at Q2. Read Pbuyers on D and Psellers on S at Q2.
  • Tax revenue = t × Q2, and DWL = ½ × t × (Q1 − Q2).
  • Whoever has the steeper curve pays more of the tax.
  • Buyers pay Pbuyers − P1 more; sellers get P1 − Psellers less.
  • If buyers legally pay the tax, shift D down by t instead. The result is the same.

Subsidy

Label: Mark D = MB, S = MC, S − s, P1, Q1, Q2, Pbuyers and Psellers.

  1. Draw D and S at P1, Q1, then S − s (S moved down by s).
  2. S − s meets D at Q2. Read Pbuyers on D; sellers get Pbuyers + s.
  3. Shade the cost box (0 to Q2) and the DWL from Q1 to Q2.
  • Government cost = s × Q2, and DWL = ½ × s × (Q2 − Q1).
  • Units past Q1 cost more than buyers value them, causing DWL.
  • Buyers' and sellers' gains = government cost − DWL.

PPC (production possibilities curve)

Label: Put C (consumption) and I (investment) on the axes.

  1. Draw a bowed-out PPC1; mark a on it, d inside, and c outside.
  2. More workers, machines, or tech push it out to PPC2.
  • The OC (opportunity cost) of one more C is the slope's size. The OC rises as you make more C, so the PPC bows out.
  • A point inside means unemployment or recession, not an inward shift. More I today pushes the PPC out later.

Comparative advantage: joint PPC

Label: Mark the intercepts, the kink, both slopes, and the gain.

  1. Each intercept is everyone's total: 60 Y or 30 X in 10 hours.
  2. Start at 60 Y. Ann (lower OC of X, slope −1) makes X first, up to the kink; then Ben (slope −4) takes it to 30 X.
  • OC of X = Y per hour ÷ X per hour, so Ann's is 1 and Ben's is 4.
  • Lower OC of X means comparative advantage in X.
  • With more people, add pieces from lowest OC of X to highest.

Budget line and spending rule

Label: I is income, Pf and Pc are prices, and qf and qc are amounts bought.

  1. Join I ÷ Pf on the food axis to I ÷ Pc on the clothing axis.
  2. More income (I') shifts it out in parallel; higher Pc pivots it in.
  • Rational Spending Rule: buy until MUa ÷ Pa = MUb ÷ Pb, where MU is the extra satisfaction from one more unit.
  • If Pc rises, MUc ÷ Pc falls, so buy less clothing.

Competitive firm and market

Label: Mark D, S, P1, Q1 and the firm's d = MR = P, MC, ATC and q*.

  1. Carry P1 from the market across as the firm's flat line d = MR = P.
  2. The firm makes q* where MC = P. MC cuts ATC at its lowest point.
  • The firm is too small to move P, so MR = P and its MC curve is its supply curve. Market S adds up all firms' MC.
  • Profit = (P − ATC) × q. In the long run, entry pushes P down to the lowest ATC, so profit = 0.
  • Profit also equals PS − fixed cost.

Monopoly

Label: Mark D = MB, MR, MC, Qm, Pm, Qc, Pc and the DWL.

  1. Draw D, MR (twice as steep, same P intercept), and MC.
  2. Find Qm where MR = MC, then go straight up to D to read Pm.
  3. D meets MC at Qc and Pc. Shade the DWL from Qm to Qc.
  • If D is P = a − bQ (a = intercept, b = slope), then MR = a − 2bQ.
  • The transfer is CS between Pc and Pm that becomes profit.
  • Profit = transfer + PS = revenue − the area under MC.
  • If MC is flat, a = 2 × Pm − MC and b = (Pm − MC) ÷ Qm.

Externalities: tax or subsidy

Label: Write SMC = PMC + EMC and SMB = PMB + EMB.

  1. The market makes Q1, where D meets S. The best amount Q* is where the social curve crosses the other curve.
  2. Shade the DWL between Q1 and Q*; its point is at Q*.
  • Harm: Q1 is too big. A tax t = EMC at Q* fixes it.
  • Benefit: Q1 is too small. A subsidy s = EMB at Q* fixes it.
  • If EMC or EMB is a constant amount, DWL = ½ × that amount × (the gap between Q1 and Q*).

Extreme slopes: vertical or flat

Label:

    • If one curve is vertical, that side pays all of a tax and DWL = 0. If one curve is flat, the other side pays all of it.

    How to answer (his grading)

    If the question asksDo this
    "Explain" or "Draw"Answer first, then give the reason in 2 to 6 sentences. On a graph, label both axes and every curve.
    Why does demand slope down, and supply up?Each extra unit adds less satisfaction, so MB falls as Q rises. Each extra unit costs more to make, so MC rises.
    Why is producer surplus a surplus?Each unit sells for P but costs only its own MC, so sellers keep P minus MC.
    • Demand = MB = WTP (willingness to pay). Supply = MC.

    Opportunity cost and the PPC

    • Terms: OC = opportunity cost, the value of the best option given up. Sunk cost = money already spent and gone. PPC = the most of two goods you can make.
    If the question asksDo this
    OC of 1 X? (you can make a X or b Y)Divide. The OC of 1 X is b ÷ a units of Y.
    What does going to an event cost you?Add hours × your wage (about $36 an hour) and the ticket's resale value. The price you paid is sunk, so leave it out.
    What does college cost?Tuition, fees, books, and the pay you give up by not working (about $60,000 a year) count. Rent and food do not, since you pay them anyway.
    Why is the PPC bowed out? Where is a recession?OC rises because the lowest-OC resources switch first. A recession is a point inside it, with no shift.

    Comparative advantage

    • Terms: absolute advantage = making more per hour. Comparative advantage = having the lower OC.
    If the question asksDo this
    Who should specialize in what, and why?Divide each person's Y per hour by their X per hour: that is their OC of 1 X. Whoever has the lower OC makes X.
    Everyone's output of one good doubles. What changes?Nothing changes. The OCs keep their order, so nobody switches.
    What trade price helps both people?Any price between the two OCs works.
    • The lower OC decides, not who makes more per hour.

    Supply and demand

    • Terms: normal good = bought more as income rises (inferior good: less). Substitute = used instead; complement = used with it.
    If the question asksDo this
    What shifts demand right? (The reverse shifts it left.)Higher income (normal good), lower income (inferior good), a pricier substitute, a cheaper complement, more buyers, a taste for it, or an expected price rise all shift it right.
    What shifts supply right? (The reverse shifts it left.)Cheaper inputs, better technology, more sellers, a tax or rule cut, or an expected price fall all shift it right.
    Was it a supply or a demand shock?If P and Q moved the same way, demand shifted. If they moved opposite ways, supply shifted.
    "The price rose, so demand fell." True?False. A change in the good's own price is a move along demand, not a shift.
    Both curves shift. What happens?If both shifts push P (or Q) the same way, that change is sure. The other depends on which shift is bigger.

    Households and the spending rule

    • Terms: MUa = the extra satisfaction from one more unit of good a. Pa = the price of good a.
    If the question asksDo this
    Is she spending rationally? (MU and prices given)Compute MU ÷ P for each good. Equal means yes. If not, she buys more of the good with the higher MU ÷ P, less of the other.
    Pa rises or falls. What should she do?If Pa rises, she buys less a and more b. If Pa falls, she buys more a and less b.
    A heatwave raises MU of a good?Its demand shifts right, so she buys more of it and less of the other good.
    • Rational Spending Rule: MUa ÷ Pa = MUb ÷ Pb, so the last dollar on each good adds the same satisfaction.
    • Trap Mistake: buying more of a raises MUa. Correct: it lowers it.

    Elasticity

    • Terms: ε = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP, where %Δ = (new − old) ÷ old. Elastic means |ε| > 1 and inelastic means |ε| < 1.
    If the question asksDo this
    Elasticity? Elastic or inelastic?Divide %ΔQ by %ΔP, using the starting values. Write "ε = −0.5, so inelastic."
    ε at a point on a straight line?Use ε = (1 ÷ slope) × (P ÷ Q). It is elastic above the midpoint and inelastic below.
    Price rises. Does spending rise or fall?It rises if demand is inelastic and falls if elastic. A price fall does the opposite. For big changes, compare P × Q before and after.
    Given ε and %ΔQ, find %ΔP.Use %ΔP = %ΔQ ÷ ε. If ε = −0.4 and Q falls 2%, price rises 5%.
    Steep or flat curve?Demand is steep (inelastic) with no close substitute or in the short run. It is flat with close substitutes or in the long run.

    Surplus, efficiency, DWL

    • Terms: WTP = the most a buyer would pay. CS = WTP − P and PS = P − MC, summed over units traded. DWL = surplus lost versus the efficient Q, where CS + PS is largest.
    If the question asksDo this
    Why is this area producer surplus?Each unit sells for P but costs only its MC. The area under MC is variable cost, so PS is what sellers keep.
    Is the outcome efficient?Yes, if nothing distorts it. MB = MC, the highest-WTP buyers buy, and the lowest-MC sellers sell. It can still be unfair.

    Price controls

    • Terms: A ceiling is a legal maximum price. It matters only if it is below P1. A floor is a legal minimum price, like a minimum wage. It matters only above P1. QS and QD are what sellers offer and buyers want at the legal price.
    If the question asksDo this
    Does either curve shift?No. Neither curve shifts. Draw a flat line at the legal price (graph 4).
    How much is actually sold?The smaller amount. With a ceiling, sellers offer only QS, so QS sells and the shortage is QD − QS. With a floor, buyers want only QD, so QD sells and QS − QD goes unsold.
    CS, PS, DWL under a ceiling?Only QS trades, so measure every area out to QS. DWL is the triangle from QS to Q1 (numbers: see Number problems).
    Do buyers gain from a ceiling?Maybe. Those who still buy pay less, but fewer units sell. Sellers always lose.
    Why is the real loss bigger than the DWL?The triangle assumes the highest-WTP buyers get the units. With a ceiling, luck or lines decide. With a floor, the lowest-MC sellers may not get to sell.
    Gouging ban, or a low price with a purchase limit?It acts like a ceiling: a shortage results, and the limit decides who buys. The model calls this wasteful. It ignores that price jumps feel unfair, and that a cap stops a seller with market power from overcharging.

    Taxes and subsidies

    • Terms: t and s are the tax and subsidy per unit, and Q2 is the new quantity. Pbuyers is what buyers pay, and Psellers = Pbuyers − t is what sellers keep.
    If the question asksDo this
    Find Q2 and both prices.Draw graph 5. Solve demand P = supply P + t to get Q2 and Pbuyers.
    Does it matter who pays it?No. Taxing buyers or sellers gives the same Q2 and prices.
    Who bears more of the tax, and why?The steeper (more inelastic) side, since it can't walk away. Flat supply or vertical demand puts it all on buyers. Vertical supply or flat demand puts it all on sellers.
    Revenue and DWL?Revenue = t × Q2. DWL = ½ × t × (Q1 − Q2), zero if a curve is vertical. There is no misallocation: price still decides who buys.
    Subsidy?Draw graph 6. Solve demand P + s = supply P. Cost = s × Q2. DWL = ½ × s × (Q2 − Q1): extra units cost more than they are worth.

    Competitive firms

    • Terms: A price taker is too small to change P, so its MR (extra revenue per unit) equals P. q is one firm's quantity. Fixed cost is paid even at zero output. Profit is PS minus fixed cost.
    If the question asksDo this
    How much does a firm make?It makes the q where P = MC. Set P equal to the MC formula and solve for q (graph 10).
    Should it enter? What about the long run?Enter if PS is bigger than fixed cost, since profit is then positive. Firms enter or leave until profit is zero.
    A cost rises for every firm. What happens to one firm?Its MC rises more than P does, so it makes less and its profit falls. Some firms then leave.

    Monopoly

    • Terms: Market power means a firm can raise P without losing every buyer. Qm and Pm are its quantity and price. Qc is the efficient quantity, where D meets MC.
    If the question asksDo this
    Why is MR below P?It must cut the price on every unit to sell one more, so it gains less than P.
    Find its Q and P.Draw MR starting where D hits the P axis, but twice as steep. Qm is where MR = MC. Pm is the height of D above Qm (graph 11).
    Is there DWL?Yes. It stops at Qm, short of Qc, to keep P above MC. DWL is the triangle between D and MC from Qm to Qc.
    Buyers get a subsidy s per unit.D and MR shift up by s. At the old Q, profit rises by s × Q and CS stays the same. But MR = MC now at a bigger Q, so Q rises and DWL shrinks.
    Does it have a supply curve?No. It picks one point on each demand curve.

    Externalities

    • Terms: An externality is a cost or benefit to outsiders that the price ignores. PMC is the seller's own cost (S). EMC is the harm to others per unit. SMC = PMC + EMC. EMB and SMB are the same for benefits (D = PMB). Q1 is the market quantity, Q* the efficient one.
    If the question asksDo this
    Compare the market Q and the best Q.Q1 is where D meets PMC. Q* is where SMB = SMC. A harmful externality makes Q1 too big; a helpful one, too small (graph 12).
    Is the best amount of pollution zero?Usually no. The first units are worth more than the harm they do, so Q* (where SMC = SMB) is above zero.
    How big is the DWL?It is the triangle between SMC and D from Q* to Q1: units that cost society more than they are worth.
    What tax fixes it?Tax each unit by EMC at Q*. For a helpful externality, subsidize each unit by EMB at Q*.
    What is total surplus?Add CS, PS and tax revenue, then subtract the total harm to others.

    Number problems with straight lines

    • Terms: Demand is P = a − bQ and supply is P = c + dQ. Q1 is the quantity before a policy, Q2 after.
    TaskHow
    EquilibriumSet demand price equal to supply price and solve for Q, then plug Q into either equation for P.
    CS and PSEach is a triangle: CS = ½ × Q × (a − P) and PS = ½ × Q × (P − c).
    Tax tBuyers pay t more than sellers get, so solve a − bQ = c + dQ + t for Q2. Revenue = t × Q2. DWL = ½ × t × (Q1 − Q2).
    Subsidy sSolve a − bQ + s = c + dQ for Q2. Cost = s × Q2. DWL = ½ × s × (Q2 − Q1).
    MonopolySet MR = a − 2bQ equal to MC to get Qm, then put Qm into demand for Pm. Given Pm, Qm and MC instead: a = 2 × Pm − MC, b = (Pm − MC) ÷ Qm.
    ExternalityQ* solves demand = supply + EMC; Q1 solves demand = supply. DWL = ½ × EMC × (Q1 − Q*) if EMC is constant.
    • Check: CS + PS + revenue + DWL must equal the old total.
