Practice midterm B: bubble trouble and bottled-up markets

A complete 60-point midterm built the way Edwards built the Fall 2025 midterm: two free points, eight 2-point short answers, a 24-point market story, and an 18-point question on taxes, externalities, and monopoly. Every part has a full model answer, the graph his key would draw, and a line on what earns full credit.

How to use this page

Take it as a real exam. Set a timer for 60 minutes, put your cheat sheet and a calculator next to you, and write every answer on paper before you open anything. For the graph parts, copy the pre-drawn axes onto your paper (or sketch on a printout) and draw your full answer, with labels and values, before you look at the key. Edwards grades what you write inside the box, so practice saying the idea in two to four sentences.

A good pace is one minute per point: about 16 minutes on Question 1, 24 on Question 2, and 18 on Question 3, which leaves 2 minutes for your name on every page. If a part stalls you, write the rule you would use and move on; partial credit is real on his exams.

Question 1: Sather up, short questions, short answers (16 points)

Eight parts, 2 points each. Each one tests a single idea. Give the answer, then the economic reason in one to three sentences.

2 points 1(a). Maya has 6 free hours on Sunday. In each hour she can either read 20 pages of her econ textbook or solve 4 math problems. By the end of the day she has solved 12 problems and read 50 pages. What is her opportunity cost of one math problem? Is she on, inside, or outside her production possibilities curve (PPC)? Briefly explain.

Show answer
  1. One hour produces either 4 problems or 20 pages, so one problem costs 20 ÷ 4 = 5 pages. That is her opportunity cost of a problem, and minus 5 is the slope of her PPC with problems on the horizontal axis.
  2. Her PPC runs from 120 pages (6 hours × 20, all reading) to 24 problems (6 hours × 4, all math). It is a straight line because her rates never change.
  3. Twelve problems take 12 ÷ 4 = 3 hours. The other 3 hours could have produced 3 × 20 = 60 pages.
  4. She read only 50 pages, so the point (12 problems, 50 pages) is inside her PPC.

She could have read 10 more pages without giving up a single problem, so some of her time was not used productively. Points inside the PPC are attainable but inefficient.

Full credit: 5 pages per problem, and "inside" with the reason (12 problems leave time for 60 pages, and she read only 50).

2 points 1(b). When the e-scooter companies in Berkeley raise their price per ride, students take fewer scooter rides and more bike-share rides. Which of these is a change in demand and which is a change in quantity demanded? Briefly explain.

Show answer

The drop in scooter rides is a decrease in quantity demanded of scooter rides. The scooter's own price changed, so riders move up along the same scooter demand curve; nothing about their willingness to pay for a scooter ride changed.

The rise in bike-share rides is an increase in demand for bike share. Bike share is a substitute, and a higher price for a substitute makes people willing to pay more for bike rides at every bike-share price, so the whole bike-share demand curve shifts right.

Full credit: both labeled correctly, with the reason: a change in a good's own price is a movement along its demand curve, while a change in the price of a substitute shifts the other good's demand curve.

2 points 1(c). Suppose a drought in Mexico cuts the number of avocados reaching Bay Area stores this month by 10%, and the price elasticity of demand for avocados is −0.4. By about what percent will the price of avocados rise? Will shoppers spend more or less on avocados in total? Show your work.

Show answer
  1. Start from the definition: ε = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP. Here the quantity change is known and the price change is the unknown, so turn it around: %ΔP = %ΔQ ÷ ε.
  2. %ΔP = (−10%) ÷ (−0.4) = +25%. The price must rise until shoppers are willing to buy 10% fewer avocados, and because demand is inelastic (|ε| = 0.4 is below 1) that takes a big price increase.
  3. Spending changes by roughly the sum of the percent changes: +25% − 10% ≈ +15%. (Exactly: 1.25 × 0.90 = 1.125, so spending rises 12.5%.)

Price rises about 25%, and shoppers spend more in total, because with inelastic demand the price increase outweighs the drop in quantity.

Full credit: the 25% price increase from %ΔP = %ΔQ ÷ ε, and "more" with the inelastic-demand reason. Either the 15% rule of thumb or the exact 12.5% is fine.

2 points 1(d). During graduation weekend, all 1,200 hotel rooms in Berkeley are rented, and no new rooms can be added on short notice. Suppose rooms rent for $300 a night and the city adds a $30 per night tax that guests pay at checkout. Who bears the burden of the tax? Briefly explain.

Show answer

The hotels bear all of it. The supply of rooms that weekend is perfectly inelastic (a vertical supply curve at 1,200 rooms): hotels cannot add rooms, and an empty room earns nothing, so they rent all 1,200 at whatever price the market will pay. Guests' willingness to pay for a room, tax included, has not changed, so the most they will now pay the hotel is $30 less. Demand, measured in what hotels receive, shifts down by $30, and the price hotels keep falls from $300 to $270 while guests still pay $300 in total.

It does not matter that guests hand over the tax at checkout. Incidence falls on the more inelastic side of the market, and here supply is as inelastic as it gets. This is the mirror image of Edwards' Berkeley soda tax poll, where supply was perfectly elastic and consumers paid the whole tax.

Full credit: "hotels" (sellers) bear the whole tax, with the reason that supply is perfectly inelastic, and a note that who pays at checkout does not matter.

2 points 1(e). A strawberry stand at the Saturday farmers' market is one of many sellers and takes the market price of $4 per basket as given. Its marginal cost of the q-th basket in an hour is $0.50 × q. How many baskets per hour should it sell? Why would selling a 9th basket lower its profit?

Show answer

A price taker faces a flat demand curve at the market price, so each extra basket brings in exactly $4: P = MR = $4. It should sell up to the point where P = MC: 0.50 × q = 4, so q = 8 baskets per hour.

The 9th basket has a marginal cost of 0.50 × 9 = $4.50 but sells for only $4, so making it lowers profit by $0.50. Every basket up to the 8th brings in at least as much as it costs.

Full credit: 8 baskets from P = MC, and the 9th basket's marginal cost of $4.50 is above the $4 price.

2 points 1(f). The only taco truck allowed inside the Greek Theatre on concert night sells 100 burritos at $10 each. To sell a 101st burrito, it would have to cut the price to $9.95 for everyone. What is the marginal revenue of the 101st burrito? If a burrito's marginal cost is $6, should the truck sell it? Briefly explain.

Show answer
  1. Revenue at 100 burritos: 100 × $10 = $1,000.
  2. Revenue at 101 burritos: 101 × $9.95 = $1,004.95.
  3. Marginal revenue of the 101st: $1,004.95 − $1,000 = $4.95.

Marginal revenue is far below the $9.95 price because the truck gains $9.95 from the new buyer but loses $0.05 on each of the 100 burritos it was already selling, which is $5.00. Since MR of $4.95 is below the $6 marginal cost, the truck should not sell the 101st burrito, even though that buyer would pay $9.95, more than it costs to make. This is why a monopoly sells less than the efficient quantity.

Full credit: MR = $4.95 with the work (or the $9.95 gain minus the $5 loss on the other 100), and "no" because MR is below MC.

2 points 1(g). A house on Channing Way throws a party every Saturday. Each hour of the party after midnight costs the neighbors $80 in lost sleep. The house's marginal benefit is $200 for the first hour after midnight, $100 for the second, and $40 for the third. What is the efficient number of party hours after midnight? Describe how a Coase solution and a Pigou solution could each get there.

Show answer

The efficient amount is where marginal benefit equals social marginal cost, so the party should continue as long as the house's marginal benefit is above the $80 external cost. Hours 1 and 2 pass ($200 and $100 are above $80) and hour 3 fails ($40 is below $80), so the efficient amount is 2 hours, which is not zero.

Coase: if property rights are clear and bargaining is cheap, the two sides negotiate to 2 hours. If the neighbors have the right to quiet, the house will pay them at least $80 an hour for hours 1 and 2 but not for hour 3. If the house has the right to party, the neighbors will pay it something between $40 and $80 to stop the third hour. Pigou: the city charges a fine of $80 per hour after midnight, equal to the external marginal cost, so the house faces the full social cost and stops after 2 hours.

Full credit: 2 hours from comparing marginal benefit with the $80 cost, plus one correct sentence each: Coase uses clear property rights and bargaining; Pigou uses a per-unit tax equal to the external marginal cost.

2 points 1(h). Priya has a scholarship that pays all of her tuition and fees at Berkeley. Books and supplies cost her $1,200 a year. Rent and food cost $20,000 a year, the same as they would if she worked instead. If she did not go to college, she could work full time for $50,000 a year. What is her yearly opportunity cost of attending college? Is college free for her?

Show answer

Her opportunity cost is books and supplies plus foregone earnings: $1,200 + $50,000 = $51,200 a year. Tuition and fees cost her nothing. Rent and food are left out because she pays them whether she studies or works, so they cancel. College is not free: the biggest cost is the $50,000 in foregone earnings, and the scholarship does not touch it.

Full credit: the answer must include foregone earnings ($50,000) and must leave out rent and food. His Fall 2025 key said full-credit answers on the cost of college need to state foregone earnings.

Question 2: Bubble, bubble, toil and tapioca (24 points)

Boba shops crowd the blocks south of campus along Durant and Telegraph, a dozen of them within a few minutes of Sather Gate. Students who skip boba can switch to coffee, smoothies, or plain iced tea. Suppose that in a typical fall week, all the shops near campus together sell about 20 thousand cups at about $5 per cup. (All numbers in this question are made up for practice.)

2 points 2(a). Think about the demand curve facing one boba shop on Durant, and the demand curve for boba near campus as a whole. Which is more elastic? Briefly explain.

Show answer

The demand facing one shop is far more elastic (close to flat). The other shops on the same block sell nearly identical drinks, so if one shop raises its price, most of its customers walk next door. Demand for boba near campus as a whole is relatively inelastic (steep): when every shop's price rises, the only substitutes are different drinks like coffee or smoothies, which are not the same thing to a boba habit, so total quantity falls only a little.

Full credit: the single shop is more elastic because of close substitutes next door, and the market is less elastic because the substitutes for boba itself are imperfect.

2 points 2(b). Once a shop has signed its lease and bought its equipment, what determines the marginal cost of one more cup? Is it high or low compared with the $5 price? Why might marginal cost rise if all the shops try to make many more cups in a week?

Show answer

The marginal cost of a cup is the tea, milk, sugar, tapioca pearls, cup, sealing film, and straw, plus a minute or two of an employee's time. Rent and machines are fixed costs, paid no matter how many cups are made, so they are not part of marginal cost. Marginal cost is low compared with the $5 price.

It rises as output grows because each shop has a fixed counter, a few sealing machines, and limited staff: making many more cups means paying overtime, hiring extra workers, and cooking pearls in more batches. Rising marginal cost is why supply slopes upward.

Full credit: the variable inputs named, "low," and a capacity reason that marginal cost rises, so supply slopes up.

4 points 2(c). On the axes below, draw supply and demand for boba near campus in a typical fall week, with the equilibrium at $5 and 20 thousand cups. Make the slopes match your answers to 2(a) and 2(b), and label the equilibrium with values.

Show answer

Draw a relatively steep demand curve (inelastic, from 2(a)) and an upward-sloping supply curve (rising marginal cost, from 2(b)) crossing at (20 thousand cups, $5), with dotted lines from the intersection to both axes.

The key uses demand P = 15 − 0.5Q and supply P = 0.25Q (Q in thousands), which pass through (20, $5) and match the data used later. You do not need these exact lines: any steep demand and upward supply crossing at (20, $5) gets full credit, as on his Fall 2025 egg graph.

Full credit: both curves labeled, demand steeper than you would draw for a single shop, supply sloping up, and the intersection marked at 20 thousand cups and $5.

2 points 2(d). Compared with September, was finals week mainly a supply shock or a demand shock? What about the April pearl shortage? Briefly explain using the data.

Show answer

Finals week was a demand shock. Price and quantity both rose ($5.00 to $5.50 and 20 to 22 thousand cups). Only a rightward shift of demand moves price and quantity in the same direction; students cramming for exams want more sugar and caffeine at every price.

The April shortage was a supply shock. Price rose ($5.00 to $6.00) while quantity fell (20 to 18 thousand). Price and quantity moving in opposite directions is the sign of a supply shift; the pearl shortage raised the shops' cost of each cup, shifting supply up (to the left).

Full credit: demand for finals week and supply for April, each justified by whether price and quantity moved together or in opposite directions.

2 points 2(e). Your roommate computes the price elasticity of demand from the September and finals-week rows. What number does she get, and why is it not the price elasticity of demand? Use the right pair of rows to compute it (use simple percent changes from the September values). Is demand elastic or inelastic?

Show answer
  1. Roommate's number: %ΔP = (5.50 − 5.00) ÷ 5.00 = +10% and %ΔQ = (22 − 20) ÷ 20 = +10%, so she gets +1.
  2. That is not a demand elasticity. Demand shifted during finals week, so the two points are on different demand curves; they trace out the supply curve instead. A positive sign is the giveaway, since demand elasticity is negative. Her +1 is really the elasticity of supply.
  3. To measure demand elasticity you need a movement along demand, which comes from a supply shift. Use September and April: %ΔP = (6 − 5) ÷ 5 = +20% and %ΔQ = (18 − 20) ÷ 20 = −10%.
  4. ε = −10% ÷ 20% = −0.5.

The price elasticity of demand is −0.5, so demand is relatively inelastic (magnitude below 1), which matches the steep demand curve from 2(a).

Full credit: +1 for the roommate, the reason (demand shifted, so those points lie on supply, not demand), and −0.5 from the September and April rows, called inelastic.

4 points 2(f). On new axes, redraw the September supply and demand curves and depict the April pearl shortage, with values when you know them. The shortage raised the shops' cost by $1.50 per cup. How much of that $1.50 did customers end up paying? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Supply shifts up by $1.50 at every quantity (S' = S + $1.50), because each cup now costs $1.50 more to make. Demand does not move. The new equilibrium is at (18 thousand cups, $6), read from the April row: price up a lot, quantity down a little, because demand is steep.

Customers pay $1.00 of the $1.50 (the price went from $5 to $6). Shops absorb the other $0.50: after covering the extra $1.50 of cost, they have $6 − $1.50 = $4.50 per cup, compared with $5 before. Customers bear more because demand is less elastic than supply here (demand −0.5 and supply +1 at the September point), the same rule as tax incidence.

Full credit: the original curves, S' shifted up (left) and labeled, the new equilibrium marked at 18 thousand and $6, and the split: customers $1.00, shops $0.50, because demand is the more inelastic side.

2 points 2(g). What happened to total weekly spending on boba near campus between September and April? Is that what you would expect, given your answer to 2(e)? Show your work.

Show answer
  1. September: $5 × 20,000 cups = $100,000 per week.
  2. April: $6 × 18,000 cups = $108,000 per week.

Spending rose by $8,000 (8%). That is what inelastic demand predicts: price rose 20% while quantity fell only 10%, and spending changes by roughly the sum, +20% − 10% ≈ +10% (exactly 8%). With elastic demand, the same price increase would have lowered spending.

Full credit: $100,000 to $108,000 (or "up about 10%" from the sum rule), linked to demand being inelastic.

4 points 2(h). Students complain, and suppose the city council caps the price of boba at $5 per cup during the shortage. Suppose demand is P = 15 − 0.5Q and April supply is P = 1.5 + 0.25Q, with Q in thousands of cups per week. Under the cap, how many cups are sold, how many do students want to buy, and how big is the shortage? What is the deadweight loss compared with the uncapped April market? Show your work.

Show answer
  1. Neither curve shifts. The cap only stops the price from rising to $6.
  2. Quantity supplied at $5: 5 = 1.5 + 0.25Q, so QS = 3.5 ÷ 0.25 = 14 thousand cups. Shops sell only what they are willing to supply, so 14 thousand cups are sold.
  3. Quantity demanded at $5: 5 = 15 − 0.5Q, so QD = 10 ÷ 0.5 = 20 thousand cups.
  4. Shortage = QD − QS = 20 − 14 = 6 thousand cups per week.
  5. Check the uncapped April market: 15 − 0.5Q = 1.5 + 0.25Q gives Q = 18 and P = $6, matching the table.
  6. At 14 thousand cups, willingness to pay is 15 − 0.5(14) = $8 and marginal cost is $5. The deadweight loss is the triangle between demand and supply from 14 to 18: ½ × (18 − 14) × (8 − 5) = 6, which is $6,000 per week.

The cap lowers the price for students who get a cup, but 4 thousand cups that were worth more to students than they cost to make are no longer made, and nothing guarantees the remaining cups go to the students who value them most. Expect long lines: a student who would pay $8 for the last cup will wait in a line worth up to $3 of her time.

Full credit: 14 sold, 20 demanded, shortage of 6 thousand, deadweight loss of $6 thousand per week, and no curve shifted. Quantity sold is read off supply, not demand.

2 points 2(i). With no cap at all, one popular shop kept its price at $5 in April, limited each customer to one large cup per visit, and sold out of pearls most afternoons. Why might a profit-seeking shop do this rather than raise its price to $6? What does the textbook model say is lost?

Show answer

Customers care about fairness. A shortage is temporary, but a shop near campus sells to the same students for four years, and a price hike during a shortage can look like price gouging and cost it loyal customers long after the pearls come back. Holding the price and limiting cups trades some profit now for goodwill later.

The textbook model says the below-market price creates a shortage at that shop: cups are rationed by limits, lines, and luck instead of price, so they do not necessarily go to the customers with the highest willingness to pay, and time spent in line is wasted. This is the same tradeoff as Trader Joe's egg limits in the Fall 2025 midterm.

Full credit: a fairness or reputation reason (repeat customers, fear of looking like price gouging) and the cost the model points to (shortage and rationing that does not send cups to the highest willingness to pay).

Question 3: Water you going to do about it? (18 points)

Suppose that at a Cal home football game at Memorial Stadium, fans' demand for bottled water is P = 12 − Q, where Q is thousands of bottles per game and P is dollars per bottle. To begin, any vendor may sell water inside the stadium, and every vendor can get bottles at a constant cost of $2 each, so marginal cost is flat at $2. Measure all surplus areas in thousands of dollars per game.

3 points 3(a). Find the competitive equilibrium price and quantity. Compute consumer surplus and producer surplus. Why is producer surplus equal to what you found? Show your work.

Show answer
  1. With many vendors, supply is the flat marginal cost line, so the price is $2.
  2. Quantity from demand: 2 = 12 − Q, so Q = 10 thousand bottles.
  3. Consumer surplus is the triangle under demand and above $2: ½ × 10 × (12 − 2) = 50 ($50,000 per game).
  4. Producer surplus = 0.

Producer surplus is the area above the supply (marginal cost) curve and below the price. Supply is flat at exactly the price, so every bottle sells for exactly what it costs and there is no area between them.

Full credit: P = $2, Q = 10 thousand, CS = 50 (thousand dollars), PS = 0 because price equals marginal cost on every unit.

2 points 3(b). Using ε = (1/slope) × (P/Q), find the price elasticity of demand at the competitive equilibrium. What is the elasticity of supply? Is demand elastic or inelastic?

Show answer

Demand P = 12 − Q has slope −1. At (Q, P) = (10, 2): ε = (1 ÷ −1) × (2 ÷ 10) = −0.2, so demand is inelastic at this point.

Supply is flat, with slope 0, so its elasticity is 1 ÷ 0, which is infinite (perfectly elastic). Vendors will supply any number of bottles at $2 and none below it.

Full credit: −0.2, inelastic, and supply perfectly (infinitely) elastic because it is flat.

3 points 3(c). The city imposes a $4 per-bottle tax collected from the vendors. Find the price fans pay, the price vendors keep, and the new quantity. Compute consumer surplus, tax revenue, and deadweight loss. Who bears the tax?

Show answer
  1. The tax raises vendors' cost per bottle to $2 + $4 = $6, so the supply line moves up to $6. Who sends the check to the city does not matter.
  2. Fans pay $6; vendors keep 6 − 4 = $2, the same as before.
  3. Quantity: 6 = 12 − Q, so Q = 6 thousand.
  4. Consumer surplus: ½ × 6 × (12 − 6) = 18.
  5. Tax revenue: $4 × 6 = 24.
  6. Deadweight loss: ½ × (10 − 6) × 4 = 8. Check: consumer surplus fell from 50 to 18, a loss of 32, which equals revenue 24 plus deadweight loss 8. Producer surplus stays 0.

Fans bear the entire tax. Supply is perfectly elastic, so vendors cannot be pushed below $2, and the whole $4 lands on the price fans pay, even though vendors are the ones who remit it.

Full credit: fans pay $6, vendors keep $2, Q = 6, CS = 18, revenue = 24, DWL = 8, and consumers bear all of it because supply is flat.

3 points 3(d). Forget the tax for now. Empty bottles end up under the bleachers and in Strawberry Creek, and the cleanup and pollution cost people outside this market $4 per bottle. Find social marginal cost, the efficient quantity, the total external cost and the deadweight loss of the unregulated market. Now look back at 3(c): was the $4 tax good or bad for society?

Show answer
  1. Social marginal cost = private marginal cost + external marginal cost: SMC = 2 + 4 = 6.
  2. Efficient quantity where SMC meets demand (social marginal benefit): 6 = 12 − Q, so Q* = 6 thousand.
  3. The unregulated market still sells 10 thousand at $2. External cost: $4 × 10 = 40.
  4. Deadweight loss is the triangle between SMC and demand from 6 to 10, pointing toward Q*: ½ × (10 − 6) × (6 − 2) = 8.

The $4 tax in 3(c) was good for society. It equals the external marginal cost, so it is exactly the Pigouvian tax: it moves the market from 10 to the efficient 6 thousand bottles. The "deadweight loss" of 8 in 3(c) counted only fans and vendors; once the litter is counted, those 4 thousand bottles cost society more than they were worth, so cutting them gains 8.

Full credit: SMC = $6, Q* = 6 thousand, external cost 40, DWL 8, and the $4 tax recognized as the Pigouvian tax that fixes it.

4 points 3(e). Now suppose the university sells one company the exclusive right to sell water in the stadium, and fans may not bring their own. Ignore the tax and the litter. On the axes below, draw everything you need to find the monopoly outcome, with values when you know them. Find the monopoly price, quantity, profit, and deadweight loss. I have drawn the MC curve for you.

Show answer
  1. Draw demand from $12 on the price axis to 12 thousand on the quantity axis.
  2. Marginal revenue has the same intercept and is twice as steep: MR = 12 − 2Q, from $12 down to 6 thousand.
  3. Quantity where MR = MC: 12 − 2Q = 2, so Q = 5 thousand.
  4. Price read off demand at Q = 5 (not off MR or MC): P = 12 − 5 = $7.
  5. Profit = (P − MC) × Q = (7 − 2) × 5 = 25 ($25,000 per game). With flat marginal cost and no fixed cost, this rectangle is the whole profit.
  6. Deadweight loss: the triangle between demand and MC from 5 to the competitive 10: ½ × (10 − 5) × (7 − 2) = 12.5. Consumer surplus is ½ × 5 × 5 = 12.5.

The monopoly cuts sales in half, from 10 to 5 thousand bottles, and raises the price from $2 to $7.

Full credit: demand and MR drawn and labeled (MR twice as steep), Q = 5 where MR = MC, P = $7 read off demand, profit 25, and DWL 12.5. Reading the price off MR or MC ($2) loses most of the points.

3 points 3(f). Bring the litter back: the external cost is again $4 per bottle, and the monopoly from 3(e) is the only seller. Is the monopoly's output too high, too low, or just right from society's point of view? What is the deadweight loss? Should the city also charge the monopoly the $4 per-bottle tax? Briefly explain.

Show answer

The efficient quantity is still Q* = 6 thousand (where demand meets SMC = $6). The monopoly sells 5 thousand, so output is slightly too low, by 1 thousand bottles. Deadweight loss is the small triangle between demand and SMC from 5 to 6: ½ × (6 − 5) × (7 − 6) = 0.5 ($500 per game), much smaller than the 8 under unregulated competition. By restricting output to raise its price, the monopoly happens to undo most of the overproduction caused by the litter.

The city should not add the $4 tax here. With the tax, the monopoly's marginal cost becomes $6, and MR = MC gives 12 − 2Q = 6, so Q = 3 and P = $9. That moves output further from the efficient 6 thousand, and the deadweight loss grows to ½ × (6 − 3) × (9 − 6) = 4.5. A Pigouvian tax equal to the external cost is the right fix for a competitive market, but a monopoly already produces too little.

Full credit: "too low" (5 versus 6), DWL of 0.5, and "no tax" because it would cut output to 3 and make the outcome worse (DWL 4.5).

Three habits from this exam that carry straight to Thursday: when price and quantity move together, demand shifted, and when they move in opposite directions, supply shifted. To get the percent price change from a quantity shock, divide: %ΔP = %ΔQ ÷ ε. The burden of any tax or cost increase falls mostly on the more inelastic side, and entirely on it when that side is flat or vertical.
