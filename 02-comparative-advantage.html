Classes 2 and 3 · Comparative advantage

Comparative advantage and specialization

This unit covers why two people (or countries) who have different opportunity costs can both end up with more by specializing and trading, and how to show that on a production possibilities curve (PPC). Edwards tests it with the same Bill and Chris island every time: who should specialize in what, what a technology change does, and how to draw the island PPC with intercepts, slopes, and kinks.

The ideas, explained

Opportunity cost and the slope of a PPC

A production possibilities curve shows every combination of two goods that a person or economy can produce using all of its available time and resources. Points on the curve are productively efficient: you cannot get more of one good without giving up some of the other. Points inside the curve leave resources unused. Points outside are unattainable.

Moving along the curve means giving up some of one good to get more of the other. That trade-off is the opportunity cost, and it shows up as the slope.

The slope of the PPC is minus the opportunity cost of the good on the horizontal axis. A flatter PPC means a lower opportunity cost of the horizontal good; a steeper PPC means a higher one.

For one person with fixed hourly rates, the PPC is a straight line, so the opportunity cost is the same at every point. You compute it from the hourly rates:

OC of 1 unit of X = (units of Y per hour) ÷ (units of X per hour)

The reason this works: one hour can make either the Y amount or the X amount. Giving up an hour of Y to make X costs you the Y amount, and dividing by the X amount gives the cost per single unit of X. The opportunity cost of Y is just the reciprocal.

Absolute advantage and comparative advantage

Absolute advantage means you can produce more of a good per hour than someone else. Comparative advantage means you can produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than someone else. Comparative advantage, not absolute advantage, decides who should specialize in what.

The surprising part, which Edwards stressed with Elon Musk, is that someone who is better at everything should still specialize. Even if Musk were a better car mechanic than any mechanic, he should spend his time running his companies and pay someone else to fix his car, because an hour of his time spent fixing cars gives up far more value than it does for the mechanic. "Even if you do something better than everybody else, you still should farm it out." The same logic says a corporate lawyer who is better at taxes than her accountant should still hire the accountant.

Every person has a comparative advantage in something, even someone with no absolute advantage at all. If a coconut costs you more fish than it costs me, then a fish must cost you fewer coconuts than it costs me. So with two people and two goods, as long as their opportunity costs differ, each one has the comparative advantage in exactly one good.

Bill and Chris: the individual PPCs

In one hour, Bill can catch 1 fish or gather 1 coconut. Chris can catch 8 fish or gather 2 coconuts. Each works 10 hours a day. Coconuts go on the horizontal axis and fish on the vertical axis, as on Edwards' slides.

Chris has the absolute advantage in both goods: he makes more fish and more coconuts per hour than Bill. ("Bill looks rather puny," says the slide.) But a coconut costs Chris 4 fish and costs Bill only 1 fish, so Bill has the comparative advantage in coconuts. Going the other direction, a fish costs Chris only ¼ coconut and costs Bill 1 coconut, so Chris has the comparative advantage in fish. Chris should fish and Bill should gather coconuts.

Read the graph the way Edwards did on the Class 3 slide: Bill's PPC is flatter, meaning its slope is smaller in absolute value (−1 against −4). Since the slope is minus the opportunity cost of coconuts, Bill's opportunity cost of coconuts is smaller. Be careful to say "flatter" or "smaller in absolute value," because −1 is technically a larger number than −4.

The island PPC without specialization

Now combine the two into one island economy. "No specialization" means Bill and Chris never agree on who does what; they both shift their hours between fish and coconuts in the same way at the same time. Each hour that both of them fish yields 1 + 8 = 9 fish. Each hour that both gather yields 1 + 2 = 3 coconuts. So every hour they both switch from fish to coconuts trades 9 fish for 3 coconuts: one coconut costs 3 fish everywhere along the line.

  • Vertical intercept: everyone fishes all day, 10 × 1 + 10 × 8 = 90 fish.
  • Horizontal intercept: everyone gathers all day, 10 × 1 + 10 × 2 = 30 coconuts.
  • Slope: −90 ÷ 30 = −3, a straight line.

The island PPC with specialization

Now they cooperate. Bill's pitch on the slide: Chris, fish full time, and I will too, so we will have 90 fish. If we need coconuts, I will switch over and gather some. If we need more than 10, I will be tapped out and you will have to help.

  1. Start at 90 fish and 0 coconuts, the same top point as before.
  2. The first coconuts come from Bill, the low opportunity cost coconut producer. Each coconut he gathers costs only 1 fish, so this segment has slope −1. When Bill has moved all 10 hours to coconuts, the island has 10 coconuts (all Bill's) and 80 fish (all Chris's). That is the kink at (10 coconuts, 80 fish).
  3. Any more coconuts must come from Chris, at 4 fish each, so the slope becomes −4. Chris's 20 coconuts cost his 80 fish, which brings the island to 30 coconuts and 0 fish.

So the specialized PPC runs from (0, 90) to (10, 80) to (30, 0). It has the same two intercepts as the unspecialized line (at the endpoints everyone is doing the same task, so it does not matter how they would have split it), but it bows out above that line everywhere in between.

The shaded area between the two curves is labeled "gains from specialization" on Edwards' slide. A clean way to measure the gain at a point: at 10 coconuts, the unspecialized line gives 90 − 3 × 10 = 60 fish, while the specialized curve gives 80 fish. Specialization adds 20 fish a day with the same 20 hours of work, just by putting each person on the task where his opportunity cost is lower. Edwards said he was not sure the area itself has a direct meaning here, so measure gains as extra output at a given amount of the other good.

Why gains exist and why a country's PPC is bowed out

There are gains from specialization when opportunity costs differ across producers and production is organized according to comparative advantage. The bigger the difference in opportunity costs, the bigger the gains.

If two people had identical opportunity costs ("big Chris and little Chris," in Edwards' words), there would be no reason to specialize: the specialized PPC would be the same straight line as the unspecialized one, and there would be no gains at all. It is the difference that creates the gain.

The kinked shape also explains why a whole country's PPC is bowed out (concave). When the country wants more of a good, it first moves the people with the lowest opportunity cost of that good, then people with higher and higher opportunity costs. Each new producer adds a steeper segment. Two people give one kink. Edwards said ten people would give ten straight pieces that "would look pretty curved," and a country with millions of people gives a smooth bowed-out curve where the opportunity cost rises as you produce more.

The Bill and Chris kink looks abrupt because Bill is bad at everything; his segment is short. If Bill could make 20 fish or 20 coconuts, the kink would move further out.

The fishhook: a proportional change does not change who specializes

On the Fall 2025 midterm, a new fishhook doubled both people's fish per hour. Recompute: Bill now catches 2 fish or gathers 1 coconut, so a coconut costs him 2 fish. Chris now catches 16 fish or gathers 2 coconuts, so a coconut costs him 8 fish. Both opportunity costs doubled, so the ranking is unchanged: Bill still has the lower opportunity cost of coconuts and still gathers them, and Chris still fishes.

The general rule: comparative advantage depends on ratios. Multiplying everyone's output of one good by the same factor leaves every ratio's ranking the same. Only an uneven change can flip it. For example, if only Bill's fishing improved to 5 fish an hour, a coconut would cost him 5 fish, more than Chris's 4, and they would swap roles. The official answer also noted that they might get sick of fish and have Chris fish a bit less, but Bill would still always gather coconuts.

Robinson: a third islander with the same opportunity cost as Bill (Problem Set 1)

Robinson can catch 2 fish or gather 2 coconuts per hour, for 10 hours. Robinson is twice as productive as Bill at both tasks, but his ratio is the same: a coconut costs Robinson 2 ÷ 2 = 1 fish, exactly like Bill. Chris's coconut still costs 4 fish.

  • PPC1, no specialization. Vertical intercept 10 × 1 + 10 × 8 + 10 × 2 = 110 fish. Horizontal intercept 10 × 1 + 10 × 2 + 10 × 2 = 50 coconuts. Straight line with slope −110 ÷ 50 = −2.2 (11 fish given up per 5 coconuts).
  • Who gathers coconuts? Bill and Robinson tie for the lowest opportunity cost of coconuts, so they do the same thing: both gather coconuts only, and Chris fishes only.
  • PPC2, with specialization. Start at 110 fish. Bill and Robinson switch first, each coconut costing 1 fish, so the slope is −1. Together they can gather 10 + 20 = 30 coconuts, giving up 10 + 20 = 30 fish. The kink is at (30 coconuts, 80 fish), where the 80 fish are all Chris's. After that, Chris switches at slope −4, and his 20 coconuts cost his 80 fish, ending at (50, 0).

The trap is drawing two kinks. Three people usually give two kinks, but Bill and Robinson have the same slope, so their two segments line up into one straight segment of slope −1. Equal opportunity costs mean one kink.

Tom and Krisha: comparative advantage with no absolute advantage

The textbook example (Figure 2.5): per day, Tom can produce 24 pounds of nuts or 12 pounds of coffee beans; Krisha can produce 12 pounds of nuts or 24 pounds of coffee. Nuts go on the horizontal axis.

Per dayNutsCoffeeOC of 1 lb nutsOC of 1 lb coffee
Tom2412½ lb coffee2 lb nuts
Krisha12242 lb coffee½ lb nuts

Here neither has an absolute advantage in both goods: Tom is better at nuts and Krisha is better at coffee. Tom has the comparative advantage in nuts (½ lb of coffee per pound, against Krisha's 2) and Krisha has it in coffee.

Alone. If each splits the day to produce equal amounts of both goods, each lands at point B = (8 nuts, 8 coffee). Check Tom: one third of his day on nuts gives 24 × ⅓ = 8, and two thirds on coffee gives 12 × ⅔ = 8. Krisha does the reverse: 12 × ⅔ = 8 nuts and 24 × ⅓ = 8 coffee. Together they have 16 pounds of each.

Specialized. Tom makes 24 pounds of nuts, Krisha makes 24 pounds of coffee, and they split 50-50. Each gets point E = (12 nuts, 12 coffee), which is outside both individual PPCs. Neither could reach E alone; trade gets them there.

The joint PPC. Edwards built it the same way as Bill and Chris. Start with both on coffee: 12 + 24 = 36 pounds. If they want nuts, Tom switches first, since a pound of nuts costs him only ½ pound of coffee (slope −½). When Tom is all on nuts, they have 24 nuts and 24 coffee: the kink at (24, 24). More nuts must come from Krisha at 2 pounds of coffee each (slope −2), ending at 36 nuts and 0 coffee.

Edwards pointed out two ways to see the same gain. In the per-person picture, E sits off both individual PPCs "precisely because of the cooperation." In the economy-wide picture, the pair's total output moves out to the kinked joint PPC. "I could see it both ways, and I think you should try to as well." Their kink looks milder than Bill and Chris's because Tom and Krisha are on a level footing; Edwards compared the shape to a Cybertruck.

Gains from trade and the terms of trade

Specializing means each person produces mostly one good, so they must trade to eat both. The price they trade at (the terms of trade) decides how the gain is split. Without trade, each person's own opportunity cost acts as his price; Edwards called these "autarkic prices." A trade helps someone only if it gets him a good more cheaply than he could make it himself.

For both sides to gain, the trading price must lie between the two opportunity costs. For Bill and Chris, a coconut must sell for more than 1 fish (Bill's cost) and less than 4 fish (Chris's cost).

Why: Bill sells coconuts. If he gets fewer than 1 fish per coconut, he would do better catching the fish himself. Chris buys coconuts. If he pays more than 4 fish per coconut, he would do better gathering them himself. Any price in between leaves both better off. At exactly 1 fish, all the gain goes to Chris; at exactly 4 fish, all of it goes to Bill.

A numerical check. Suppose the island wants 15 coconuts. Without specialization (each splits his day in half), Bill has 5 fish and 5 coconuts and Chris has 40 fish and 10 coconuts: 45 fish in total. With specialization, Bill gathers 10 coconuts and Chris spends 7.5 hours fishing (60 fish) and 2.5 hours gathering (5 coconuts): 60 fish and 15 coconuts in total, a gain of 15 fish. Now Bill trades 5 coconuts to Chris.

For Tom and Krisha, a pound of nuts must trade for between ½ and 2 pounds of coffee. Edwards said they have to agree on a price, and since they produce equal amounts, "probably a price of one to one" makes sense. At 1 to 1, Tom trades 12 pounds of nuts for 12 pounds of coffee and both reach E.

Edwards' two answers to "will both benefit?" from the slides: the simple answer is that as long as there is no coercion, if two parties choose to specialize and trade, both must be benefiting. The more complicated answer is that in a market system, prices tend to adjust so that both parties gain.

What specialization needs, and its limits

Trust and institutions. Bill only gives up fishing if he trusts Chris to hand over fish later. Edwards stressed that specialization needs laws, police, courts, and a constitution, which he called social infrastructure and the rule of law. Without them, Chris might "just go and beat up Bill and take Bill's stuff," and the "no coercion" condition fails.

Where comparative advantage comes from. Climate and natural resources (coffee in the tropics, oil in Saudi Arabia) and history (cars around Detroit). It can change over time: many cars are now built in China. His Class 3 case asked whether California should grow rice. Rice uses the most water per acre of the listed crops (4.5 acre-feet), and irrigation is about 60.7% of California's water use. California's alternative uses of that water (nuts, vegetables, urban growth) are high value, while in Arkansas the alternatives are low value. His conclusion: California is almost surely not the low opportunity cost rice producer in the U.S.; history (the Gold Rush and Chinese immigrant demand) explains why it grows rice anyway.

Can there be too much specialization? From his slides:

  • For individuals, doing one narrow task all day (an assembly line) can be psychologically numbing.
  • For countries, depending on trade raises concerns: national security (supply chain breakdowns, trade wars, enough food, military production), infant industries that may need protection at first, and environmental and labor protection laws that differ across countries.

He also gave a spoken limit: a construction worker should probably work on his own house (unlike filing his own taxes, which he should hire out). Doing it himself removes the problem of aligning the incentives of worker and client, and he knows the work was done right. Similarly, parents who are themselves housekeepers should clean their own house, since hiring someone "basically no more efficient" gains nothing and you cannot see what a hired housekeeper does. He warned against answering these "who should do it" questions from budgets ("they cannot afford it"); answer from opportunity cost.

Finally, which point on the PPC is best is not something the PPC tells you. It depends on preferences: fish and coconuts go together, so a mix may beat 80 fish and 10 coconuts. That analysis waits for later courses.

Worked examples

Example: who specializes, Bill and Chris

Bill: 1 fish or 1 coconut per hour. Chris: 8 fish or 2 coconuts per hour. Who should do what?

  1. Opportunity cost of a coconut: Bill gives up 1 fish ÷ 1 coconut = 1 fish. Chris gives up 8 fish ÷ 2 coconuts = 4 fish.
  2. Bill has the lower opportunity cost of coconuts, so he has the comparative advantage in coconuts.
  3. Check the other direction: a fish costs Bill 1 coconut and costs Chris ¼ coconut, so Chris has the comparative advantage in fish.
  4. Absolute advantage does not matter: Chris is better at both, but that does not decide who specializes.

Answer: Chris fishes and Bill gathers coconuts, because a coconut costs Chris 4 fish but costs Bill only 1 fish.

Example: island PPCs and the gain at 10 coconuts

Same people, 10 hours each. Find both island PPCs and the gain from specialization at 10 coconuts.

  1. No specialization: most fish = 10 × 1 + 10 × 8 = 90; most coconuts = 10 × 1 + 10 × 2 = 30; straight line, slope −90 ÷ 30 = −3.
  2. With specialization: from 90 fish, Bill switches first at 1 fish per coconut (slope −1) until he is all on coconuts at (10, 80). Then Chris switches at 4 fish per coconut (slope −4) down to (30, 0).
  3. At 10 coconuts without specialization: 90 − 3 × 10 = 60 fish.
  4. At 10 coconuts with specialization: Bill makes all 10 coconuts and Chris catches 80 fish.

Answer: The gain is 80 − 60 = 20 fish per day, at the same 10 coconuts.

Example: the fishhook (Fall 2025 midterm)

A fishhook doubles both people's fish per hour. Does specialization change?

  1. New rates: Bill 2 fish or 1 coconut; Chris 16 fish or 2 coconuts.
  2. New opportunity cost of a coconut: Bill 2 ÷ 1 = 2 fish; Chris 16 ÷ 2 = 8 fish.
  3. Both doubled, so Bill still has the lower opportunity cost of coconuts.

Answer: Nothing changes: Bill still gathers coconuts and Chris still fishes, because a proportional change leaves the ranking of opportunity costs the same.

Example: Robinson's PPC2 (Problem Set 1)

Add Robinson, 2 fish or 2 coconuts per hour, 10 hours. Draw PPC2 with specialization.

  1. Opportunity cost of a coconut: Bill 1 fish, Robinson 2 ÷ 2 = 1 fish, Chris 4 fish.
  2. Intercepts (same as PPC1): 10 + 80 + 20 = 110 fish, and 10 + 20 + 20 = 50 coconuts.
  3. Bill and Robinson switch first at slope −1. Their 30 coconuts cost 30 fish: kink at (30, 80).
  4. Chris switches next at slope −4: 20 coconuts cost 80 fish, ending at (50, 0).

Answer: PPC2 runs (0, 110) to (30, 80) to (50, 0), slopes −1 and −4, one kink, bowed out above the straight PPC1 (slope −2.2).

Example: Tom and Krisha and the trading price

Tom: 24 nuts or 12 coffee per day. Krisha: 12 nuts or 24 coffee. Who specializes, and what prices work?

  1. A pound of nuts costs Tom 12 ÷ 24 = ½ pound of coffee and costs Krisha 24 ÷ 12 = 2 pounds. Tom has the comparative advantage in nuts, Krisha in coffee.
  2. Specialized output: 24 nuts and 24 coffee. Split 50-50, each gets (12, 12), compared with (8, 8) alone.
  3. Price range: a pound of nuts must trade for between ½ and 2 pounds of coffee. Edwards suggested 1 to 1.

Answer: Tom grows nuts and Krisha grows coffee; at 1 pound of nuts for 1 pound of coffee, both reach (12, 12), outside their own PPCs.

Common mistakes

Mistake: "Chris is better at both, so Chris should do both" or "Chris should fish because he catches more fish."

Correct: That is absolute advantage. Specialization follows comparative advantage: compare opportunity costs. Chris fishes because a coconut costs him 4 fish, against 1 fish for Bill.

Mistake: Flipping the opportunity cost, for example writing that a coconut costs Chris ¼ fish.

Correct: The opportunity cost of a coconut is the fish given up to get it: (fish per hour) ÷ (coconuts per hour) = 8 ÷ 2 = 4 fish. Write the units every time ("4 fish per coconut") and the flip becomes obvious.

Mistake: Giving the specialized PPC different intercepts from the unspecialized one (Edwards himself told a student this in class, then the slides and the Problem Set 1 key contradict him).

Correct: Both PPCs share the intercepts (90 fish and 30 coconuts; 110 and 50 with Robinson). The specialized one is higher only between the intercepts. What is unattainable is having 90 fish and 30 coconuts at the same time.

Mistake: Drawing two kinks in Robinson's PPC2, or having Robinson fish.

Correct: Robinson's opportunity cost of a coconut (1 fish) equals Bill's, so both gather coconuts, their segments share slope −1, and there is one kink at (30, 80).

Mistake: Saying Bill's PPC has a "smaller slope" or "larger slope" without care.

Correct: Say Bill's PPC is flatter, with a slope smaller in absolute value (−1 against −4). That means a lower opportunity cost of the horizontal-axis good, coconuts.

Mistake: Concluding that the fishhook makes Bill switch to fishing because he is now better at it.

Correct: Both opportunity costs double (Bill 2 fish per coconut, Chris 8), so the ranking stays the same and nothing changes.

Mistake: Saying a trade price outside the two opportunity costs (for example 5 fish per coconut) helps both.

Correct: At 5 fish per coconut, Chris would rather gather coconuts himself at a cost of 4 fish. The price must be strictly between 1 and 4 fish per coconut for both to gain.

Mistake: Answering "should she hire someone?" with budgets ("she cannot afford it").

Correct: Edwards said that reasoning "might lead us a bit astray." Answer from opportunity cost, and mention the information or incentive problem only as a separate limit.

How it shows up on the exam

Comparative advantage was worth 4 of the 60 points on the Fall 2025 midterm, split into two 2-point short answers. Both used the Bill and Chris numbers from class (Bill catches 1 fish or gathers 1 coconut per hour; Chris catches 8 fish or gathers 2 coconuts per hour; each works 10 hours).

  • Fall 2025 midterm 1(d), 2 points. Who should specialize in what, and why? He added a hint that it is a simple question with a simple answer. The official answer: Chris fishes and Bill gathers coconuts, because Chris's opportunity cost of a coconut (4 fish) is higher than Bill's (1 fish).
  • Fall 2025 midterm 1(e), 2 points. A new fishhook doubles both people's fish per hour. Does the specialization change? The official answer: nothing changes. Bill now gets 2 fish or 1 coconut and Chris gets 16 fish or 2 coconuts, so Chris is still comparatively better at fishing and Bill still gathers coconuts.
  • Problem Set 1, question 2, 8 points. A third islander, Robinson (2 fish or 2 coconuts per hour), joins. Part (a), 2 points: draw the island PPC with no specialization. Part (b), 2 points: who gathers coconuts? (The key calls this "tricky.") Part (c), 4 points: draw the PPC with specialization, redraw the first one, and label intercepts, slopes, kinks, and the shape.
  • Fall 2025 final, multiple choice. The principle of comparative advantage implies that I should specialize in the task I do best. The wrong choices included "all the tasks I do better than others," which is absolute advantage. "Do best" here means best relative to your other tasks, which is the same as lowest opportunity cost.

What earns full credit: name the opportunity cost for each person in units of the other good ("a coconut costs Chris 4 fish but costs Bill only 1 fish"), then state who does what. The official answers always include that comparison, so never stop at "Chris fishes." On PPC graphs, label both intercepts with numbers, every slope, and the kink as a coordinate pair, and say why the curve is bowed out.

Edwards also said that in two-dimensional graph questions it is easy to say "the thing exactly opposite to the way it really is" (which good's opportunity cost, flat versus steep). His advice: slow down and write your reasoning in words, which earns at least partial credit.

Practice questions

2 points On an island, Bill can catch 1 fish or gather 1 coconut in an hour, and Chris can catch 8 fish or gather 2 coconuts. Who should specialize in what? Briefly explain.

Show answer

A coconut costs Bill 1 fish but costs Chris 8 ÷ 2 = 4 fish, so Bill is the low opportunity cost producer of coconuts and has the comparative advantage in them. Equivalently, a fish costs Chris only ¼ coconut against 1 coconut for Bill. Chris should fish and Bill should gather coconuts, even though Chris has the absolute advantage in both goods.

Full credit: Chris fishes, Bill gathers coconuts, with the opportunity cost comparison (4 fish against 1 fish, or the reverse comparison for fish).

2 points Bill and Chris discover a fishhook that lets each of them catch twice as many fish per hour as before. What, if anything, does this change about how they should specialize? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Bill now catches 2 fish or gathers 1 coconut, so a coconut costs him 2 fish. Chris catches 16 fish or gathers 2 coconuts, so a coconut costs him 8 fish. Both opportunity costs doubled, so Bill still has the lower opportunity cost of coconuts. Nothing changes: Bill gathers coconuts and Chris fishes. (They might want fewer fish and have Chris fish a bit less, but Bill still specializes in coconuts.)

Full credit: "Nothing changes," with the reason that a proportional change keeps Chris comparatively better at fishing (new numbers help).

2 points Suppose instead that only Bill gets better at fishing, to 5 fish per hour (still 1 coconut per hour). Chris is unchanged. Now who specializes in what?

Show answer

A coconut now costs Bill 5 fish and still costs Chris 4 fish. Chris now has the lower opportunity cost of coconuts, so the roles flip: Chris gathers coconuts and Bill fishes. Note that Chris still has the absolute advantage in fishing (8 against 5 per hour) and yet he should stop fishing.

Full credit: roles flip, with 5 fish against 4 fish per coconut. An uneven change can flip comparative advantage; a proportional one cannot.

2 points With Bill and Chris working 10 hours each, how many fish can the island have along with 10 coconuts if they do not specialize? If they do? What explains the difference?

Show answer

Without specialization the PPC is a straight line from 90 fish to 30 coconuts with slope −3, so 10 coconuts leaves 90 − 3 × 10 = 60 fish. With specialization, Bill gathers all 10 coconuts (his whole day) and Chris catches 80 fish. Specializing by comparative advantage gains 20 fish, because each coconut is made by the producer who gives up only 1 fish for it instead of 4.

Full credit: 60 fish and 80 fish, a 20-fish gain, explained by producing coconuts at the lower opportunity cost.

2 points Robinson joins the island; he can catch 2 fish or gather 2 coconuts per hour, 10 hours a day. Draw the island PPC with no specialization (each person splits time the same way), with fish on the vertical axis. Identify and explain both intercepts and the slope.

Show answer

Vertical intercept: everyone fishes, 10 × 1 + 10 × 8 + 10 × 2 = 110 fish. Horizontal intercept: everyone gathers, 10 × 1 + 10 × 2 + 10 × 2 = 50 coconuts. Because all three shift their hours together, every hour switched costs the same mix, so the PPC is a straight line with slope −110 ÷ 50 = −2.2 (11 fish given up for every 5 coconuts).

Full credit: straight line, 110 and 50 labeled, slope −2.2 with its meaning.

2 points With Robinson on the island, who should gather coconuts if they follow comparative advantage? Briefly explain.

Show answer

A coconut costs Bill 1 fish, Robinson 2 ÷ 2 = 1 fish, and Chris 4 fish. Bill and Robinson have the same, lowest opportunity cost of coconuts, so both of them gather coconuts only, and Chris fishes only. Robinson being better than Bill at both tasks does not matter; only the ratio does.

Full credit: Bill and Robinson gather coconuts, Chris fishes, because Bill and Robinson have the same opportunity cost (1 fish per coconut), lower than Chris's.

4 points Now draw the island PPC2 with specialization, and redraw PPC1 for comparison. Identify the intercepts, slopes, and kink(s), and briefly explain the shape.

Show answer

PPC2 has the same intercepts as PPC1: 110 fish and 50 coconuts. Starting from 110 fish, the first coconuts come from Bill and Robinson at 1 fish each, so the first segment has slope −1. When both are gathering full time they have 30 coconuts and have given up 30 fish, so there is one kink at (30 coconuts, 80 fish). After that only Chris can switch, at 4 fish per coconut, so the slope is −4 until (50, 0). PPC2 bows out above PPC1 because the lowest opportunity cost producers switch first, and the opportunity cost of coconuts rises as more are produced.

Full credit: same intercepts, one kink at (30, 80), slopes −1 and −4, PPC2 above PPC1, and a sentence on why it is bowed out.

2 points Tom can produce 24 pounds of nuts or 12 pounds of coffee per day; Krisha can produce 12 pounds of nuts or 24 pounds of coffee. Does either have an absolute advantage in both goods? Should they specialize? Briefly explain.

Show answer

No: Tom is better at nuts and Krisha is better at coffee, so neither has an absolute advantage in both. A pound of nuts costs Tom ½ pound of coffee and costs Krisha 2 pounds, so Tom has the comparative advantage in nuts and Krisha in coffee. They should specialize: together they make 24 pounds of each, and splitting it 50-50 gives each (12, 12), more of both goods than the (8, 8) each gets alone.

Full credit: no absolute advantage in both; comparative advantage Tom nuts, Krisha coffee, with opportunity costs; specializing gives (12, 12) against (8, 8).

2 points Bill and Chris specialize and trade coconuts for fish. Between what prices must a coconut trade for both to gain? Would Chris agree to pay 5 fish per coconut?

Show answer

The price must be between the two opportunity costs: more than 1 fish per coconut (otherwise Bill would rather catch his own fish) and less than 4 fish per coconut (otherwise Chris would rather gather his own coconuts). At 5 fish per coconut, Chris would refuse, because gathering a coconut himself costs him only 4 fish.

Full credit: between 1 and 4 fish per coconut, with the reason; Chris says no to 5 because his own cost is 4.

2 points Two islanders are identical in their opportunity costs: each can catch 4 fish or gather 2 coconuts per hour. Are there gains from specialization? What does their joint PPC look like?

Show answer

No. A coconut costs each of them 2 fish, so neither has a comparative advantage. Moving either person from fish to coconuts costs the same 2 fish per coconut, so the joint PPC is a straight line with slope −2, whether or not they specialize. Gains from specialization require differences in opportunity cost.

Full credit: no gains, because the opportunity costs are equal; joint PPC straight (no kink).

2 points True or false: patterns of international trade are based solely on comparative advantage. Briefly explain.

Show answer

False. Trade also reflects history (California rice, Detroit cars), policy (tariffs, national security concerns), and other factors. Still, underneath most trade there is probably some element of comparative advantage, such as climate or resources making some places low opportunity cost producers.

Full credit: false, with at least one other factor, plus the nuance that comparative advantage still underlies much of trade.

2 points Give two reasons a country might not want to specialize completely according to comparative advantage.

Show answer

Any two of: national security (supply chains can break down in a trade war or a crisis, and a country may want its own food or military production); infant industries that may need protection at first before they can compete; differences in environmental protection laws across countries; differences in labor protection laws. At the individual level, very narrow specialized work can be psychologically numbing.

Full credit: two distinct limits from his list, each with a brief reason.

One-minute review

  • Opportunity cost of 1 X = (Y per hour) ÷ (X per hour). The slope of the PPC is minus the opportunity cost of the horizontal-axis good.
  • Absolute advantage is more output per hour; comparative advantage is lower opportunity cost. Specialize by comparative advantage.
  • Bill: a coconut costs 1 fish. Chris: a coconut costs 4 fish (a fish costs ¼ coconut). Bill gathers coconuts, Chris fishes.
  • Island PPC without specialization: 90 fish to 30 coconuts, straight, slope −3.
  • With specialization: (0, 90) to kink (10, 80) to (30, 0), slopes −1 then −4, same intercepts. At 10 coconuts, 80 fish instead of 60: a gain of 20 fish.
  • Fishhook doubling everyone's fish: opportunity costs become 2 and 8 fish per coconut, so nothing changes.
  • Robinson (2 and 2): PPC1 110 to 50, slope −2.2. Same opportunity cost as Bill, so Bill and Robinson gather coconuts; PPC2 has one kink at (30, 80), slopes −1 and −4.
  • Tom and Krisha: no absolute advantage in both, still specialize. Alone (8, 8) each; specialized and split, (12, 12) each, outside both PPCs. Joint PPC: 36 coffee, kink (24, 24), 36 nuts.
  • The trade price must be between the two opportunity costs (1 to 4 fish per coconut; ½ to 2 pounds of coffee per pound of nuts).
  • Gains need different opportunity costs, trust, and the rule of law. Limits: national security, infant industries, different environmental and labor laws, numbing narrow work.
