Game plan

What the exam looks like

Last year's midterm (Fall 2025) was 60 points in 60 minutes with no multiple choice: 2 free points, eight 2-point short answers, one long market story (24 points), and one long monopoly story (18 points). Three graphs were worth 4 points each. This year Edwards said on the class 11 slides that monopoly "will be on the midterm" as "a shorter question than the F25 Q3 about monopolist Taylor Swift," that topics covered more in class get more weight, and that the class 9 material on average cost curves and how profits evolve is "not what you should study." Externalities were drilled with numbers on Problem Set 2, question 3, so expect them.

How he grades (from the Fall 2025 key)

  • Answers go inside the box. "Don't fill the space with words. Less is more." Two to four sentences with the right vocabulary (marginal benefit, marginal cost, opportunity cost, willingness to pay) beat a paragraph.
  • Required phrases get full credit. College cost needed "foregone earnings." Pollution needed "social marginal cost" and "social marginal benefit."
  • Graphs: label axes and curves, put the equilibrium at the numbers he gives, draw dotted lines to both axes, and write "values when you know them."
  • Elasticity: the class 6 slide defines it in absolute value, but the class 7 slides and the Fall 2025 key keep the minus sign (−0.5). Write both: "−0.5, so |ε| = 0.5 < 1, inelastic."
  • Show your arithmetic. He gives partial credit in quarter points (the lowest Fall 2025 score was 11.75).

What he said out loud in lecture (Fall 2026 recordings plus the Fall 2025 monopoly lecture)

  • The Fall 2025 midterm is the template. Classes 8, 9, and 11: "last year's midterm will be pretty indicative," and look at the problem sets too. Class 1: the midterm will be "a new version of what's in here."
  • Guaranteed: standard supply and demand, and elasticity ("Those are gonna be on there"), including the reverse use where a quantity shock tells you the percent change in price (question 1(g) below). He called the flat versus steep shape of demand "really important."
  • The monopoly trap he hammered in class 11: get Q from MR = MC, then read the price off demand. In his example Q = 8 and P = 4, "not 2." That is drill D13(a).
  • Rational Spending Rule: "for the exam... write this down." The most-missed idea: when a good's price goes up, you buy less of it, and its marginal utility rises.
  • "Explain" means explain. A bare number does not get full credit. If you draw a different curve than he did, argue for its shape and you get partial credit. If wording is unclear, write your interpretation and answer it.
  • Not on the exam: indifference curves, income and substitution effects, average total cost curves and the profit rectangles drawn with them, the zero-profit monopolist, calculus, and coding. Ostrom only if the exam explains it. Logs: you will not need them from memory; if a question uses them, the exam will explain how.
  • Cheat sheet: one page, front and back, typed or handwritten, and you can build it with friends. He told you to put two things on it: "inelastic means steep and elastic means flat," and MUa/Pa = MUb/Pb. He also suggested the monopoly graph.
  • True-or-false statements with "always" or "solely" are usually false.

1. (16 points) Marginal questions, marginal answers

(a) (2 points) An economy produces consumption goods (horizontal axis) and investment goods (vertical axis). Moving along its PPC from point A to point B adds 10 units of consumption and gives up 30 units of investment. What is the opportunity cost of one unit of consumption along this stretch? Where on the graph is an economy that is in a recession?
(b) (2 points) Boba near campus goes from $6.00 to $6.60 a cup, and weekly quantity demanded falls from 20,000 to 16,000 cups. Using simple percentage changes from the starting values, what is the price elasticity of demand? Is demand relatively elastic or inelastic? Does total spending on boba rise or fall?
(c) (2 points) You buy coffee ($4 each, marginal utility 12) and burritos ($10 each, marginal utility 30). Are you following the Rational Spending Rule? Then the price of coffee rises to $6. How should you change what you buy, and why?
(d) (2 points) In one day Oski can write 4 essays or finish 8 problem sets. The Stanford Tree can write 1 essay or finish 4 problem sets. Who has the absolute advantage in each task? If they split the work, who should specialize in essays, and why?
(e) (2 points) You win a free ticket to a Warriors game. You could resell it for $150. The game plus the BART ride takes 4 hours (ignore the fare), and you could otherwise spend those hours working your campus job at $20 an hour. What is your opportunity cost of going? Be specific.
(f) (2 points) The city caps rents below the market-clearing level. Does the demand curve or the supply curve shift? What happens to the number of apartments actually rented? On a graph, how would you measure the shortage? (Be careful.)
(g) (2 points) The price elasticity of demand for gasoline in California is about −0.4. A refinery fire cuts the quantity of gas sold by 2%. By about how much does the price of gas rise? Does total spending on gas rise or fall? Show your work.
(h) (2 points) A small almond farm sells at the market price of $5 per pound. Its marginal cost is MC = q/2, where q is thousands of pounds per year, and its fixed costs are $20,000 a year. How much should it grow, and why? Should it enter the market?

2. (24 points) Grounds for concern

Coffee beans are grown in a handful of tropical countries and shipped around the world. A newly planted coffee tree takes several years before it produces beans. For this question, suppose the U.S. market for coffee beans starts at about $4 per pound and about 3 billion pounds per year.
(a) (2 points) What is the likely shape of the demand curve for coffee beans in the short run: horizontal, vertical, or somewhere in between? Briefly explain.
(b) (2 points) What is the likely shape of the supply curve in the short run? How about over 5 to 10 years? Briefly explain.
(c) (4 points) Draw short-run supply and demand in the U.S. coffee bean market. Put the starting equilibrium at $4 per pound and 3 billion pounds, and make the slopes match your answers to (a) and (b). Do not worry about other specifics.
(d) (2 points) Suppose coffee bean prices jump twice in five years, each time right after a drought in Brazil, while the number of coffee drinkers grows slowly and steadily. Is this more likely a supply shock or a demand shock, and which way does the curve move? When the price rises, does the demand curve shift?
(e) (4 points) Suppose that after the second drought the price is $8 per pound and quantity is 2.5 billion pounds. Redraw supply and demand and depict the drought, with values when you know them.
(f) (4 points) Using the numbers in (e), compute the price elasticity of demand (simple percentage changes from the starting values). Did total spending on coffee beans rise or fall, and by how much? Why? Show your work.
(g) (2 points) A neighborhood coffee shop keeps selling bags of beans at its old price but posts a sign: "Limit one bag per customer." What does the basic competitive model predict will happen on its shelves? Why might the shop do this anyway?
(h) (4 points) Congress adds a $1 per pound tax on coffee beans, collected from importers. In the long run, supply is far more elastic than demand. Who bears most of the tax? Does it matter that importers write the check? Will the deadweight loss be large or small? Briefly explain.

3. (10 points) Patently obvious

BioBear holds a patent on Sneezgone, the only drug for a rare allergy. Monthly demand is P = 100 − Q, where P is dollars per dose and Q is thousands of doses. Marginal cost is constant at $20 per dose, and fixed costs are zero.
(a) (2 points) Why can BioBear charge more than marginal cost? What keeps rivals out?
(b) (4 points) I have drawn the marginal cost curve for you. Add any curves you need to explain the market equilibrium, and label the monopoly quantity and price with values.
(c) (2 points) What is BioBear's monthly profit, in dollars? What is the deadweight loss? Show your work.
(d) (2 points) The patent expires, generic makers enter, and competition drives the price down to marginal cost. What happens to price, quantity, and consumer surplus? Where did BioBear's old profit go?

4. (8 points) Blowing leaves, blowing smoke

Gas leaf blowers are loud and pollute. In the market for yard cleanups, demand is P = 10 − Q (P in dollars, Q in thousands of cleanups per month), and private marginal cost is constant at $2. Each cleanup imposes $4 of noise and pollution costs on neighbors.
(a) (4 points) I have drawn the private marginal cost for you. Show the market outcome, the social optimum, and the deadweight loss, with values.
(b) (2 points) At the market outcome, what are consumer surplus, producer surplus, and the total cost imposed on neighbors?
(c) (2 points) What per-unit tax gets the market to the efficient quantity? Some neighbors want gas-blower yard cleanups banned outright, which would take this market to zero. Is zero the efficient quantity here? Briefly explain.

Part 1 answer key

Each answer shows the steps, then what earns full credit. Score yourself honestly: a part is full credit only if your answer has the idea in the grading line.

1. Short answers

1(a)

Slope of the PPC = −30/10 = −3. The slope of the PPC is minus the opportunity cost of the good on the horizontal axis, so one unit of consumption costs 3 units of investment.

A recession is a point inside the PPC: the economy is not producing all it is capable of. The curve itself does not shift.

Full credit: 3 units of investment, and "inside the PPC." A recession is not an inward shift of the PPC (class 2 slide).
1(b)%ΔP = (6.60 − 6.00) / 6.00 = +10% %ΔQ = (16,000 − 20,000) / 20,000 = −20% ε = −20% / 10% = −2, so |ε| = 2 > 1: elastic Spending before: $6.00 × 20,000 = $120,000 Spending after: $6.60 × 16,000 = $105,600 (falls 12%)

Demand is elastic, so quantity falls by a bigger percentage than price rises, and total spending falls, from $120,000 to $105,600.

Full credit: −2 (or 2 in absolute value), elastic, spending falls.
1(c)Before: MUcoffee/Pcoffee = 12/4 = 3 MUburrito/Pburrito = 30/10 = 3 Equal, so yes. After: MUcoffee/Pcoffee = 12/6 = 2, which is less than 3

Yes, at first the marginal utility per dollar is the same (3 utils per dollar). After the price rise, coffee gives less utility per dollar. Buy less coffee (its marginal utility rises as you drink less) and more burritos (their marginal utility falls) until the two ratios are equal again.

Full credit: say which side of the equation fell and which change in quantities restores equality, using diminishing marginal utility.
1(d)Opportunity cost of 1 essay: Oski 8/4 = 2 problem sets Tree 4/1 = 4 problem sets Opportunity cost of 1 problem set: Oski 1/2 essay Tree 1/4 essay

Oski has the absolute advantage in both tasks (4 essays beats 1, and 8 problem sets beats 4). Oski specializes in essays because his opportunity cost of an essay (2 problem sets) is lower than the Tree's (4). The Tree does problem sets, where its opportunity cost is lower.

Full credit: compares opportunity costs. "Oski is better at everything" alone is the absolute advantage trap.
1(e)$150 resale value + 4 hours × $20 = $150 + $80 = $230

The ticket was free to you, but going means giving up the $150 you could sell it for, plus the wages you could earn in those hours.

Full credit: $230 with both pieces. Writing $0 or only $80 misses the resale value (Edwards' theater-ticket example).
1(f)

Neither curve shifts. The ceiling holds the price below equilibrium, so we move along both curves. Landlords supply only QS, which is less than the old Q1, so fewer apartments are rented. Renters want QD, which is more than Q1. On a graph the shortage is the horizontal gap at the ceiling price: QD − QS. There is deadweight loss between QS and Q1, and apartments may go to people who do not value them most (misallocation).

Full credit: no shift, quantity rented falls to QS, shortage = QD − QS. This is the Problem Set 1, question 3(c) trap he flagged as "tricky."
1(g)%ΔP = %ΔQ / ε = −2% / −0.4 = +5% Spending: %ΔP + %ΔQ = 5% − 2% = about +3%

Price rises about 5%. Demand is inelastic (|ε| = 0.4 < 1), so it takes a big price rise to cut quantity demanded by 2%. Spending rises, by about 3%. (Adding the two percent changes is a shortcut that works for small changes like these.)

Full credit: about +5% with the division shown, and spending rises.
1(h)P = MC: 5 = q/2, so q = 10 thousand pounds Area between price and MC from 0 to 10 = ½ × 10 × 5 = 25, which is $25,000 (q is in thousands) $25,000 > $20,000 fixed cost, so it enters (profit = $5,000)

The farm is a price taker, so its marginal revenue is the $5 price, and it grows until MC = $5. It enters because revenue minus variable cost (the area between price and MC, its producer surplus) covers the fixed cost.

Full credit: 10 thousand pounds from P = MC, and the entry comparison against the $20,000 fixed cost.

2. Coffee

2(a)

Steep (inelastic), close to vertical in the short run. Coffee is a daily habit with caffeine, and there is no close one-for-one substitute (tea and energy drinks are partial). Willingness to pay for the first cups is very high.

2(b)

Short run: steep (inelastic). Farmers can only harvest the trees already in the ground, so producing more means sharply rising marginal cost. Over 5 to 10 years: much flatter (more elastic), because farmers can plant new trees and new regions can start growing.

2(c)

Both curves are steep in the short run (see (a) and (b)), with demand the steeper of the two. They cross at (3 billion pounds, $4), with dotted lines to both axes.

Full credit: equilibrium at (3, $4), demand clearly steep, supply upward sloping and steep, axes and curves labeled.
2(d)

Supply shock. The jumps line up with droughts, which destroy part of the harvest, while slow, steady growth in coffee drinkers cannot explain sudden jumps. Supply shifts left (up): at every price, fewer beans are available. The demand curve does not shift; the higher price moves buyers along demand to a lower quantity demanded.

2(e)

Same demand. Supply shifts up to S' and crosses demand at (2.5 billion pounds, $8). The price jumps a lot and quantity drops a little, because demand is steep.

Full credit: supply shifts left, demand unchanged, new point labeled (2.5, $8), old point labeled (3, $4).
2(f)%ΔP = (8 − 4) / 4 = +100% %ΔQ = (2.5 − 3) / 3 = −16.7% ε = −16.7% / 100% = −0.17, so |ε| < 1: inelastic Spending before: $4 × 3 billion = $12 billion Spending after: $8 × 2.5 billion = $20 billion Change: +$8 billion, or +67%

Spending rises because demand is inelastic: the percentage rise in price is much bigger than the percentage drop in quantity. (The shortcut %ΔP + %ΔQ only works for small changes. With a 100% price jump, multiply P × Q directly, as above.)

Full credit: ε of about −0.17, inelastic, spending up $8 billion, with the inelastic reason.
2(g)

Selling below the market price works like a price ceiling on its own shelves: quantity demanded exceeds the beans it has, so there is a shortage, and the limit (plus who shows up first) rations the bags instead of willingness to pay. Why do it anyway: customers care about fairness, and by not looking like it is price gouging, the shop keeps goodwill and its repeat drink customers.

Mirrors the Trader Joe's egg answer in the Fall 2025 key. Full credit for shortage and rationing plus a fairness or goodwill reason.
2(h)

Coffee drinkers bear most of it. The less elastic side of the market bears more of a tax, and the more elastic side avoids it: with very elastic supply, the price importers keep barely falls, so the consumer price rises by nearly the full $1. It does not matter who writes the check; a tax collected from either side gives the same equilibrium. Deadweight loss is small, because inelastic demand means quantity barely falls.

Full credit: consumers, who writes the check does not matter, small DWL with the elasticity reason.

3. Monopoly

3(a)

The patent is a legal barrier to entry: no one else may make the drug, and it has no close substitutes. So BioBear faces the whole downward-sloping market demand and chooses the quantity, which lets it set price above marginal cost.

3(b)Demand P = 100 − Q, so MR = 100 − 2Q (same intercept, twice as steep) MR = MC: 100 − 2Q = 20, so Q = 40 thousand doses Price off demand: P = 100 − 40 = $60
Full credit: demand from (0, 100) to (100, 0), MR from (0, 100) to (50, 0), Q = 40 where MR crosses MC, P = $60 read up on demand. Edwards: "(1) Set Q where MR = MC (2) Set P off demand."
3(c)Profit = (P − MC) × Q = (60 − 20) × 40 = 1,600 thousand dollars = $1.6 million per month Efficient Q: demand = MC, 100 − Q = 20, so Q = 80 DWL = ½ × (80 − 40) × (60 − 20) = 800 thousand dollars = $0.8 million per month

With flat MC and zero fixed cost, total cost is 20 × 40 = 800, so profit = total revenue − total cost = 2,400 − 800 = 1,600, the same as (P − MC) × Q. Q is in thousands, so write $1.6 million.

Full credit: $1.6 million profit and $0.8 million DWL, with the work. "1,600" and "800" are right only if labeled as thousands of dollars.
3(d)Competitive: P = MC = $20, Q = 80 thousand CS before = ½ × 40 × (100 − 60) = 800 ($0.8 million) CS after = ½ × 80 × (100 − 20) = 3,200 ($3.2 million)

Price falls to $20, quantity doubles to 80 thousand doses, and consumer surplus rises by $2.4 million. BioBear's old $1.6 million profit goes back to consumers (it was a transfer, not a loss), and the $0.8 million deadweight loss is recovered: 800 + 1,600 + 800 = 3,200.

4. Externality

4(a)Market: demand = PMC, 10 − Q = 2, so Q1 = 8 and P = $2 SMC = PMC + EMC = 2 + 4 = 6 Optimum: demand = SMC, 10 − Q = 6, so Q* = 4 DWL = ½ × (8 − 4) × (6 − 2) = 8, which is $8,000 per month
Full credit: SMC drawn $4 above PMC, Q1 = 8 and Q* = 4 marked, DWL triangle between SMC and demand from 4 to 8, pointing toward the social optimum.
4(b)CS = ½ × 8 × (10 − 2) = 32, which is $32,000 PS = 0 (flat MC: price equals MC on every unit) Cost to neighbors = $4 × 8 = 32, which is $32,000

Social surplus at the market outcome is 32 + 0 − 32 = 0. At Q* = 4 it would be the area between demand and SMC from 0 to 4: ½ × 4 × (10 − 6) = 8. The gap, 8, is the deadweight loss from (a).

4(c)

A $4 per-unit (Pigouvian) tax, equal to the external marginal cost at Q*. It moves the private cost curve up to SMC, so the market lands on Q* = 4. A ban is not efficient: the first 4 thousand cleanups are worth more to buyers than their full social cost. Banning them throws away ½ × 4 × (10 − 6) = 8, or $8,000 a month, of surplus. The optimal amount of pollution is usually not zero (Fall 2025 question 1(g)).
