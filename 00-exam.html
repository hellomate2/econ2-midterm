How the midterm works

This unit covers the format, scope, grading habits, cheat sheet rules, and logistics of Thursday's midterm, using the Fall 2025 midterm and its official answer key as the model. Nothing here is economics content; it is about turning what you already know into points on paper in 60 minutes.

Scope, grading, and how to answer

What Edwards said about this midterm's scope

In Class 11 (October 1) he put a slide up called the plan for the scope of the midterm and talked through it. There are four points to take from it.

First, monopoly will be on the exam, but as a shorter question than Fall 2025's Taylor Swift question (which was 18 points). He explained why: the monopoly class on September 29 was canceled because he was sick, so it was taught only through the recording and Problem Set 2. He still said "you do still need to understand what's going on." A shorter question still needs the full logic: quantity where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, then the price read off the demand curve.

Second, "other topics will take more prominence, because we covered them more." The points that monopoly loses go to topics that got more class time: supply and demand, elasticity, welfare, taxes and price controls, households, firms, and externalities.

Third, he named what is sure to be there. "There's gonna be demand and supply curves, right, the standard thing." Price elasticity of demand "will be on there," including the reverse use (a shock that changes quantity by some percent tells you the percent change in price). And the shape of demand: "Is it flat, is it steep? That kind of thing is really important."

Fourth, he said what not to study. Here is the full list from the lectures.

How he grades

Graduate student instructors (GSIs) score the exams, using his answer key. These are the habits that matter, each with where it comes from.

Less is more. Rule 6 on the Fall 2025 cover page says to write legibly, keep your answer inside the box, and "Don't fill the space with words. Less is more." If the grader cannot read it, you get no credit. His own official answers are two to six sentences long.

Graders look for the key's ideas, not its exact words. In Class 1 he read the demand-slope answer aloud and said: "You don't have to say exactly that, but you have to kind of get in that general sandbox of answer." A student asked whether answers must match the key verbatim; he said no, and shorthand is fine "if it's clear and readable." What you write has to touch the key's points.

Some phrases are required, though. The key for the costs-of-college question says full credit answers "need to state foregone earnings." Some questions name the terms you must use, like the pollution question that said to refer to social marginal cost and social marginal benefit. When a question names a concept, use those exact words. The table below lists the vocabulary his keys reward.

Explain means explain. In Class 4 a student asked how much work to show on a calculation. He said to write "something more than just the number," and that on exams "Explain. That's usually what I write," so "you need to have some kind of explanation." A bare number is not a full answer. Write the number and one sentence on how you got it and what it means.

Partial credit is real. The lowest Fall 2025 score was 11.75, which means graders give quarter points. His post-exam slide said "Awarding partial credit is the right thing and a hard thing to do." So never leave a box blank. A correct idea with a wrong number still earns something.

If a question is unclear, write down how you read it. In Class 9 a poll said "demand" when it meant "quantity demanded." He said that if this confuses you on an exam, write "I think this is what you mean" and answer that, and "that's at least partial if not full credit." In Class 2 he gave the same advice for graphs that get confusing: slow down and explain in words what you think is going on.

Answer exactly what is asked, with the simplest story that fits. In Class 5 he said that on an exam you have to "make sure you answer exactly the question as stated." He also said an elaborate side story can earn partial credit, but the clearest approach is to use what the case study tells you. On opinion questions (Fall 2025 asked whether a $200 ticket is high or low), the key says "Full credit for reasoned answers of any kind." Pick a side and give a reason.

Label your graphs. In the Class 10 recording he said to label the upward-sloping curve as supply because "students get that screwed up all the time." His keys label every curve, mark the equilibrium values on both axes with dotted lines, and name shifted curves with a prime (Supply').

The grading formula in one line: answer the question in the first few words, give the reason in his vocabulary, show the number and how you got it, label everything on a graph, and stop.

How to write a 2-point answer

Most of the exam is 2-point boxes. His official answers follow the same three steps, and you can copy the pattern.

  1. Lead with the answer itself: "Demand." "Supply shock." "Chris fishes, Bill gathers coconuts." "Increase purchases of clothing." "Only one." A grader should find your answer in the first line.
  2. Give one or two sentences of why, using the economic reason and the right term (marginal benefit, opportunity cost, marginal cost, elasticity magnitude below 1).
  3. If numbers are involved, show them: the two percent changes and the division, or the opportunity cost of each person. Then stop writing.

That usually comes to two to four sentences. A full box is a warning sign that you are repeating yourself or wandering away from the question.

How to draw a 4-point graph

The three Fall 2025 graphs were each worth 4 points, as much as two short questions. His keys are clean and simple. Here is the checklist that matches them.

  1. Use the axes he gives you. If you sketch your own, label the vertical axis price and the horizontal axis quantity, with units.
  2. Make the slopes match the story. He told the class in Class 6 to put this on the cheat sheet: inelastic means steep, elastic means flat. In the egg question, no single substitute meant steep demand, and low marginal cost meant flat, low supply.
  3. Put the equilibrium at the numbers he gives. If he says $2 and 10 billion, the curves must cross at exactly that point, with dotted lines over to $2 on the price axis and down to 10 on the quantity axis.
  4. Label every curve: Demand, Supply (or MC), MR, SMC. Label a shifted curve with a prime, like Supply'.
  5. For a shift, keep the original curves, draw the new one, and mark the new equilibrium "with values when you know them" (his phrase from the egg question).
  6. For monopoly, draw marginal revenue starting at the same vertical intercept as demand and twice as steep. Find the quantity where marginal revenue meets marginal cost, then go straight up to demand for the price.
  7. If the question asks about surplus or deadweight loss, shade and label each area.

Before drawing, a quick sketch of the "X" in the margin helps. In Class 6 he said he saw students on last year's midterm doing exactly that to see which way things move.

The cheat sheet rules

He has said the same rule in Class 7, Class 8, and Class 11: you get one page of notes, front and back. It can be typed or handwritten ("typed as tiny as you want or written," in the Class 10 recording). You can make it on an iPad, but it must be printed on paper, because no screens are allowed. You can build it with friends and each bring a copy, though his advice is to make your own, because "the act of writing it typically means that you'll remember it more."

Two more rules. The exam is open book, but he said not to bother bringing the textbook, because the exam is written new and "the answers aren't gonna be in the textbook." And do not copy old answer keys onto your sheet expecting to reuse them; he said copied answers from past exams were "never the right answers" for a new exam and only created more work for the graders.

He suggested specific things to put on it:

  • The Rational Spending Rule, MUa ÷ Pa = MUb ÷ Pb (Class 7 and Class 8: "that's the thing you write on your cheat sheet").
  • "Inelastic means steep and elastic means flat" (Class 6).
  • Marginal revenue equals marginal cost and the monopoly diagram (Class 10 recording: "write this down on your cheat sheet, this graph").
  • A supply and demand cross with the upward-sloping curve labeled supply (Class 10 recording).

Using the 60 minutes

The exam has 60 points and 60 minutes, so a useful pace is about one point per minute: roughly 2 minutes for a 2-point box and 4 minutes for a 4-point graph. Grab the 2 free points first by writing your name and student ID on every page before you start. If a part stalls you, write what you know, move on, and come back. Because long questions start easy, the first parts of every long question are the cheapest points on the exam, so make sure you reach all of them.

A study plan for the last day

  1. Take the Fall 2025 midterm on paper under a 60-minute timer, without the key. Then grade it against the official answers, part by part, looking for the required words you left out.
  2. Finish the cheat sheet. Put on the items he suggested above, then fill the gaps your practice exam revealed.
  3. Drill the sure things: elasticity in both directions (percent change in price to percent change in quantity, and a quantity shock to the percent change in price), supply and demand shifts with slopes that match a story, and flat versus steep demand.
  4. Redo the Problem Set 2 Question 3 graphs (tax, externality, and monopoly on the same linear demand). They cover welfare areas, externalities, and monopoly at once.
  5. Do one short monopoly drill: marginal revenue from the same intercept and twice as steep, quantity at marginal revenue equals marginal cost, price off demand.
  6. Pack your Cal ID, calculator, cheat sheet, pencils, eraser, and a ruler the night before. Check the seating chart for your seat.

Good answer versus weak answer

Each example below is a real Fall 2025 question (paraphrased), a weak answer of the kind students write, what is wrong with it, and an answer that matches his official key.

Example: Why does demand slope down? (Fall 2025, Question 1(a), 2 points)

The question asked which curve slopes downward, demand or supply, and why.

  1. Weak answer: "Demand slopes down because when the price goes up, people buy less." This names the right curve, but the reason is just a description of the downward slope. It does not say why people buy less.
  2. What the key wants: the economic reason, which is that each extra unit is worth less to you than the one before. His key puts it as marginal benefit, marginal utility, or willingness to pay falling as quantity increases.
  3. Full-credit answer: "Demand. Each extra unit adds less to buyers than the last one, so marginal benefit (willingness to pay) falls as quantity rises. More is better, but at a decreasing rate."

Answer: Name the curve, then give the marginal benefit reason in one sentence. That is the whole 2 points.

Example: The costs of attending Berkeley (Fall 2025, Question 1(f), 2 points)

The question asked for the most important costs of attending UC Berkeley, with a hint that some costs you literally pay and some you do not.

  1. Weak answer: "Tuition, rent, food, and books." This lists only money you pay. The hint pointed at a cost you do not pay with a check, and this answer misses it.
  2. What the key wants: tuition and fees plus foregone earnings, the wages you give up by not working full time. The key says full credit answers "need to state foregone earnings." It adds that with average earnings around $60,000 a year, foregone earnings are probably larger than tuition except at private colleges.
  3. Bonus point of logic from Problem Set 1: rent and food are not really costs of college, because you pay for living expenses whether or not you enroll, so they cancel out.
  4. Full-credit answer: "Tuition and fees, which you pay directly, and foregone earnings, the wages you could have earned working full time instead. With average earnings around $60,000 a year, the foregone earnings are probably the bigger cost."

Answer: Whenever a question asks about the cost of college or of your time, the phrase "foregone earnings" (or opportunity cost of time) has to appear.

Example: Elasticity of lunches around Berkeley (Fall 2025, Question 1(b), 2 points)

The price of lunch rose from $10 to $11, and quantity demanded fell from 50,000 to 47,500. What is the price elasticity of demand, and is it relatively elastic or inelastic? Briefly explain.

  1. Weak answer: "0.5, elastic." The number is missing its sign and its work, and "elastic" is wrong.
  2. Find the percent change in price from the starting value: $1 ÷ $10 = 10%.
  3. Find the percent change in quantity from the starting value: −2,500 ÷ 50,000 = −5%.
  4. Divide: ε = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP = −5% ÷ 10% = −0.5.
  5. Compare the magnitude to 1: 0.5 is less than 1, so demand is relatively inelastic.

Answer: "Price rose 10% and quantity demanded fell 5%, so the elasticity is −5% ÷ 10% = −0.5. This is relatively inelastic because the magnitude is below 1." His key uses simple percent changes from the starting values and keeps the negative sign.

Example: Drawing a supply shock (Fall 2025, Question 2(g), 4 points)

Eggs normally sell for about $2 a dozen, with about 10 billion dozen sold per year. Earlier parts established that eggs have no single substitute (so demand is steep) and that the marginal cost of an egg is low and rising (so supply is flat, low, and upward sloping). The question asked you to redraw the market and show the early 2025 avian flu shock, with values when you know them.

  1. Weak answer: a generic X with unlabeled curves, the supply curve shifted the wrong way, and no numbers on the axes. Even if the idea is right, it would likely earn little of the 4 points, because the grader cannot see the slopes, the equilibrium, or which curve moved.
  2. Draw demand steep, because there is no obvious substitute for eggs.
  3. Draw supply flat and low, because the marginal cost of an egg is small and rises slowly.
  4. Make them cross at exactly ($2, 10 billion), with dotted lines to both axes.
  5. Avian flu kills hens, which raises the marginal cost of every egg, so supply shifts up and to the left. Label it Supply'.
  6. Mark the new equilibrium: the price rises to about $6, while quantity falls only a little, to roughly 7.5 billion dozen. Steep demand is why the price jumps a lot and quantity barely moves.

Answer: Slopes that match the story, the equilibrium at his numbers, every curve labeled, and the new values marked. That is what the 4 points pay for.

Example: The monopoly graph (Fall 2025, Question 3(e), 4 points)

The Eras Tour sold about 10 million tickets at an average of $200. He drew the marginal cost curve for you (a straight line from the origin through $100 at 10 million tickets) and asked you to add whatever curves explain the market outcome.

  1. Weak answer: draws a demand curve crossing marginal cost and calls that the equilibrium. That is the competitive outcome (15 million tickets at $150), and it ignores market power, which the earlier parts set up (there is only one Taylor Swift).
  2. Draw demand so that it passes through the outcome he told you: $200 at 10 million tickets. His key runs demand from $300 at zero tickets to zero at 30 million.
  3. Draw marginal revenue from the same $300 intercept and twice as steep, so it hits zero at 15 million.
  4. Marginal revenue meets marginal cost at 10 million tickets, where marginal cost is $100. That sets the quantity.
  5. Go straight up from 10 million to the demand curve to get the price, $200. Price comes from demand, never from the point where marginal revenue meets marginal cost.

Answer: Demand, marginal revenue twice as steep from the same intercept, quantity at marginal revenue equals marginal cost, price off demand, with dotted lines and values. His key printed that logic right next to the graph.

Common mistakes

Mistake: Filling the whole box with a paragraph that restates the question before answering it.

Correct: Put the answer in the first few words, give the reason, and stop. The cover page says "Less is more," and a grader skimming hundreds of exams needs to find your answer fast.

Mistake: Writing only a number for a calculation, like "−0.5" or "$200."

Correct: Show the pieces (the two percent changes, or $2 billion ÷ 10 million) and add a sentence on what the number means. He said exams ask you to explain, and a bare number is not an explanation.

Mistake: Leaving out the concept the question points at, such as listing tuition, rent, and books as the costs of college.

Correct: Use the term the key requires. For college that is foregone earnings. If a question says "refer to social marginal cost and social marginal benefit," those words must appear in your answer.

Mistake: Drawing curves without labels, or swapping supply and demand.

Correct: Label every curve. Supply (marginal cost) slopes up and demand slopes down. He said students "get that screwed up all the time."

Mistake: Drawing a textbook X that ignores the numbers and the story, such as an equilibrium nowhere near $2 and 10 billion, or flat demand for eggs.

Correct: Cross the curves at the values he gives, with dotted lines to both axes, and choose slopes that match what earlier parts said (no substitutes means steep demand).

Mistake: Reading the monopoly price off the point where marginal revenue meets marginal cost ($100 on Fall 2025, or $2 in his class example where the right answer is $4).

Correct: Marginal revenue equals marginal cost gives the quantity only. The price is the height of the demand curve at that quantity. He called this "the subtle thing to remember about the monopoly model" in Class 11.

Mistake: Leaving a confusing part blank.

Correct: Write how you read the question and answer that version, or describe the graph in words. He said both earn at least partial credit, and graders give quarter points.

Mistake: Forgetting to write your name and student ID on every page, or still writing when time is called.

Correct: Do the name and ID first; it was a free point on Fall 2025. When time is called, put the pen down and hand in the exam with your Cal ID. The Fall 2025 penalty for continuing was 10 points at a time.

Practice questions

Each question below is written the way Edwards writes exam questions, on topics from across the midterm. The skill being practiced is turning what you know into the answer his key rewards. Write what you would put in the box, or sketch the graph, before you open the model answer. Each model answer is the full-credit version, followed by a note on what the grader looks for.

2 points Which way does the supply curve slope, and why? Briefly explain.

Show answer

"Supply slopes upward. The supply curve is the marginal cost curve, and marginal cost rises as output increases: to produce more, the market has to pull in producers and resources with higher opportunity costs, like moving Chris from fishing, where he is great, into gathering coconuts."

This is the mirror image of his official answer for demand: name the curve in the first word, then give the marginal reason in one sentence.

Full credit: names supply as the upward-sloping curve and says marginal cost (the opportunity cost of producing one more unit) rises with quantity.

2 points A boba shop on Durant raises the price of a milk tea from $6.00 to $6.60, and daily sales fall from 2,000 cups to 1,700. What is the price elasticity of demand? Is demand relatively elastic or relatively inelastic? Show your work. A student wrote only "1.5, elastic." What would that earn, and what is the full-credit answer?

Show answer

The student has the right conclusion but no work and no negative sign, so expect partial credit at best. He said a bare number is not an explanation.

Full-credit answer: "The price rose $0.60 ÷ $6.00 = 10%. Quantity demanded fell 300 ÷ 2,000 = 15%, a change of −15%. So ε = −15% ÷ 10% = −1.5. The magnitude is above 1, so demand is relatively elastic: quantity responds more than proportionally to price, which makes sense with several other boba shops on the same block."

Use plain percent changes from the starting values. That is what his key did on the Fall 2025 midterm lunch question.

Full credit: both percent changes shown, −1.5 with its sign, and "relatively elastic because the magnitude is above 1."

2 points Suppose the price elasticity of demand for gasoline in California is −0.25. A refinery fire cuts the quantity of gasoline sold by 5%. About how much does the price of gasoline rise? Briefly explain.

Show answer

"Elasticity is the percent change in quantity divided by the percent change in price, so the percent change in price is the percent change in quantity divided by the elasticity: %ΔP = %ΔQ ÷ ε = −5% ÷ −0.25 = +20%. The price rises about 20%. Demand is very inelastic, since drivers have few substitutes in the short run, so it takes a large price increase to get them to cut back by 5%."

This is the "reverse use" of elasticity that Edwards said in Class 11 would be on the midterm: a supply shock moves quantity, and the demand elasticity tells you how far price must move.

Full credit: about a 20% price increase, with the rearranged formula shown and a sentence tying the large price change to inelastic demand.

2 points You split your food budget between coffee and burritos so that the Rational Spending Rule holds: MUc ÷ Pc = MUb ÷ Pb. The price of a burrito at your usual place on Bancroft goes up. How should you change your burrito purchases to restore the equality? Briefly explain.

Show answer

"Buy fewer burritos. The higher price lowers MUb ÷ Pb, so the last dollar spent on burritos now buys less marginal utility than the last dollar spent on coffee. Buying fewer burritos raises the marginal utility of the last burrito, because marginal utility falls as you consume more and rises as you consume less. That pushes the right side back up until the two sides are equal again."

His key for this type of question (Fall 2025, Question 1(c)) says which side of the equation moved, then which quantity change moves marginal utility back. Spending some of the money on more coffee, which lowers MUc, is also correct as an addition.

Full credit: fewer burritos, with the reason that the right side fell and that buying fewer raises the marginal utility of burritos.

2 points From the perspective of Econ 2, is the optimal amount of carbon pollution from flights out of SFO zero? Refer to social marginal cost and social marginal benefit. A student wrote: "Zero, because pollution hurts people and the planet." Grade it and write the full-credit answer.

Show answer

The student's answer uses neither of the two terms the question required and reaches the wrong conclusion, so it would earn little or nothing.

Full-credit answer: "Not zero. Pollution from flights is a negative externality, so the social marginal cost of a flight is above the private cost airlines pay. The socially optimal number of flights is where social marginal cost equals social marginal benefit (travelers' willingness to pay, the demand curve). That is fewer flights than the unregulated market chooses, but usually still a positive number, so the optimal amount of pollution is usually positive. It could be zero, but there is no reason it must be."

Full credit: says the optimum is typically not zero and puts it where social marginal cost equals social marginal benefit, using those words.

2 points A question says: "The price of pizza at Sliver rises. What happens to the demand for pizza?" You think the question means quantity demanded, but you are not sure. What do you write?

Show answer

Write your interpretation and answer it: "I read this as asking about quantity demanded. A rise in the price of pizza is a movement up along the demand curve, so quantity demanded falls. The demand curve itself does not shift, because a change in a good's own price does not shift its demand."

In Class 9 Edwards said that writing "I think this is what you mean" and answering it earns "at least partial if not full credit." Here it also shows you know the difference between a shift and a movement along the curve.

Full credit: states the interpretation and says quantity demanded falls along an unchanged demand curve.

2 points Why is the area below the demand curve and above the market price called consumer surplus? Briefly explain.

Show answer

"Each point on the demand curve is some buyer's willingness to pay (marginal benefit) for that unit. Every buyer pays only the market price, so on each unit bought, the buyer keeps the difference between willingness to pay and the price. Adding that gap over every unit from zero to the equilibrium quantity gives the area below demand and above the price."

This is the demand-side twin of Fall 2025 Question 1(h), which asked the same thing about producer surplus (price minus marginal cost on each unit). In class he described consumer surplus as what buyers "take away in the bag."

Full credit: willingness to pay (marginal benefit) minus the price on each unit, added up over all units bought.

2 points Suppose cans of soda in campus vending machines are supplied at a constant marginal cost of $1.00, so supply is flat. The city adds a tax of $0.50 per can, collected from the vending company. What happens to the price students pay, and who bears the tax? Briefly explain.

Show answer

"The price students pay rises by the full $0.50, to $1.50, so students bear the whole tax. Supply is perfectly elastic: the company needs $1.00 per can after tax to cover its marginal cost, so the flat supply curve shifts up by the full $0.50 and the price rises with it. It does not matter that the company sends the money to the city; who bears a tax depends on the slopes of the curves, not on who writes the check."

This is the nonstandard case from Problem Set 2, Question 3, where flat supply meant consumers bore the entire $2 tax.

Full credit: price up by the full $0.50, consumers bear all of it because supply is flat, and who remits the tax does not matter.

4 points Suppose gasoline in a small town sells for $5 a gallon, with 40,000 gallons sold per week. Drivers have few substitutes in the short run, and stations can bring in more fuel at only slightly higher cost. Draw supply and demand. (Make sure to model the equilibrium and the general story about the slopes, but do not worry about other specifics.)

Show answer

Few substitutes means demand is inelastic, so draw it steep. More fuel at only slightly higher cost means marginal cost rises slowly, so draw supply flat. Cross them at exactly $5 and 40,000 gallons, with dotted lines to both axes, and label both curves.

Full credit: labeled curves, steep demand, flat upward-sloping supply, and the equilibrium marked at exactly $5 and 40,000 with dotted lines to both axes.

4 points Suppose the rental market for apartments near campus clears at $3,000 a month with 20,000 apartments rented. The city sets a rent ceiling of $2,000. At $2,000, landlords would offer 15,000 apartments and renters would want 30,000. Draw the market with the ceiling, with values when you know them. Which curve shifts? How big is the shortage? Be careful.

Show answer

"Neither curve shifts. A price ceiling is a rule about the price; it does not change renters' willingness to pay or landlords' marginal cost. At $2,000, quantity supplied is 15,000 and quantity demanded is 30,000. Only 15,000 apartments get rented, because nobody can rent an apartment that no landlord offers, so the quantity traded is read off the supply curve. The shortage is 30,000 − 15,000 = 15,000 apartments."

This is the trap from Problem Set 1, Question 3(c), where the key said "This one is tricky": students draw a shifted curve when nothing shifts.

Full credit: says neither curve shifts; draws the ceiling below the equilibrium at $2,000; marks quantity supplied at 15,000 and quantity demanded at 30,000; labels the shortage of 15,000; shows the original equilibrium at (20,000, $3,000).

4 points Suppose Pop Mart is the only maker of Labubu dolls, and demand is P = 60 − Q, with Q in millions of dolls and P in dollars. The axes come with the marginal cost curve already drawn: MC is flat at $20. Draw what you need to explain the market outcome, and mark the quantity and price Pop Mart chooses, with values. Also mark the efficient quantity.

Show answer

"Marginal revenue starts at the same intercept as demand ($60) and is twice as steep, so MR = 60 − 2Q, which hits zero at 30 million. Pop Mart sets MR = MC: 60 − 2Q = 20, so Q = 20 million dolls. The price is read off demand at 20 million: P = 60 − 20 = $40. The efficient quantity is where demand meets marginal cost: 60 − Q = 20, so Q = 40 million at $20. The monopolist sells half the efficient quantity."

If a follow-up asked: profit with no fixed cost is ($40 − $20) × 20 million = $400 million, and the deadweight loss is ½ × 20 million × $20 = $200 million. Edwards said the monopoly question will be shorter than the Taylor Swift one, so this graph is about the size of what to expect.

Full credit: demand and MR labeled with the same $60 intercept and MR reaching zero at 30 million; quantity of 20 million from MR = MC; price of $40 read off demand (not $20); efficient point at 40 million and $20.

Story question: "Matcha do about nothing" (14 points)

This one is built like the Fall 2025 egg question. Matcha is a powdered green tea made from shade-grown tea leaves, mostly in Japan. Suppose that new tea fields take several years before they can be harvested, and that grinding the leaves into powder is slow. Suppose also that before the recent matcha boom, the world market sold about 4 thousand tons a year at about $100 per kilogram, and that tea drinkers have plenty of substitutes (other green teas, coffee, and so on).

2 points (a) What is the likely shape of the short-run supply curve for matcha: flat, steep, or in between? Briefly explain.

Show answer

"Steep (relatively inelastic). Output cannot grow quickly, because new fields take years and grinding is slow, so producing more in the short run means a sharply rising marginal cost. The supply curve is the marginal cost curve, so it is steep."

Full credit: steep or close to vertical, because marginal cost rises fast when output cannot expand quickly.

4 points (b) Draw supply and demand for matcha before the boom. Make sure to model the equilibrium and the general story about the slopes.

Show answer

Draw supply steep (from part a) and demand flatter, because buyers have many substitutes. Cross them at exactly 4 thousand tons and $100, with dotted lines to both axes.

Full credit: steep supply, flatter demand, both labeled, and the equilibrium at exactly (4 thousand tons, $100) with dotted lines.

2 points (c) Matcha lattes then went viral on social media. Is this a supply shock or a demand shock? Which way does the curve shift, and what happens to the price and quantity of matcha?

Show answer

"A demand shock. Tastes changed, so at every price buyers now want more matcha: demand shifts out (to the right). Moving up along the steep supply curve, the price rises and the quantity rises."

Full credit: demand, shifts out or right, price up and quantity up.

4 points (d) Redraw the market and show the boom, with values when you know them. Suppose the price rose to about $200 per kilogram.

Show answer

Keep the original curves, draw Demand' to the right of Demand, and mark where it crosses supply at $200. Read the new quantity off the steep supply curve: only about 4.4 thousand tons. The price doubles while quantity rises only about 10%, because supply is so steep.

Full credit: original curves kept; Demand' labeled to the right; new equilibrium at $200 with a small quantity increase; a sentence that steep supply is why the price, not the quantity, does most of the moving.

2 points (e) Some cafes and tea shops kept the price at $100 per kilogram and limited customers to one tin each. Suppose sellers across the market did this. What happens, and why might sellers do it anyway?

Show answer

"Holding the price at $100, below the new equilibrium, works like a price ceiling. At $100, buyers on the new demand curve want about 6.4 thousand tons, but sellers offer only 4 thousand, so there is a shortage of about 2.4 thousand tons, and the one-tin limit rations it. Sellers may do it because customers care about fairness: doubling the price overnight could look like price gouging and cost them goodwill and repeat business."

This mirrors Trader Joe's selling eggs below the market price with a one-dozen limit on the Fall 2025 midterm.

Full credit: shortage at the below-market price (quantity demanded above quantity supplied), the limit as a way to ration it, and a fairness or price-gouging reason.

One-minute review

  • The Fall 2025 midterm is the template: 2 free points, eight 2-point short questions, then two long case studies. No multiple choice. Graphs are 4 points each.
  • Monopoly will be a shorter question than the 18-point Taylor Swift question. Supply and demand, elasticity (both directions), and flat versus steep demand are certain.
  • Not tested: average total cost and profit rectangles, the zero-profit monopolist, monopsony, indifference curves, logs, calculus, coding, and why marginal revenue is twice as steep.
  • Two-point answer: the answer first, one or two sentences of why in his vocabulary, the numbers if any, then stop. Less is more.
  • Required words: foregone earnings for the cost of college; social marginal cost and social marginal benefit when the question names them; marginal benefit or willingness to pay for why demand slopes down.
  • Graph: slopes that match the story, the equilibrium at his numbers with dotted lines, every curve labeled, shifted curves marked with a prime, new values shown.
  • Monopoly: quantity where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, price off demand.
  • If a question is unclear, write your interpretation and answer it. Never leave a box blank; graders give quarter points.
  • Cheat sheet: one page, front and back, typed or handwritten, on paper.
