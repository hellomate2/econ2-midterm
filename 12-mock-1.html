Practice midterm A

Practice midterm A: a full 60-point exam

This is a complete midterm in the exact format of Edwards' Fall 2025 midterm, with new stories and new numbers: 2 free points, eight 2-point short answers, a 24-point market story, and an 18-point monopoly and externality question. Set a timer for 60 minutes, use only your one-page cheat sheet and a calculator, and write every answer on paper before you open a single key.

How to use this exam

Take it like the real thing. Clear your desk except for your cheat sheet, a calculator, a pen, and blank paper. For each graph part, copy the printed axes onto your paper with the same numbers, because on Thursday the axes will already be printed and your job is only to draw on them. Start a 60-minute timer and do not stop it to look anything up. When time is called, stop.

Then grade yourself one part at a time. Open "Show answer," compare your work to the model answer and the "Full credit" line, and give yourself full, half, or zero credit honestly. The button above opens every answer at once if you want to grade quickly. Last year's class averaged 44.9 out of 60, so anything above 50 is a strong result.

Question 1 (16 points): The Bear necessities

Short questions, short answers. Each part is worth 2 points. Briefly explain each answer.

2 points 1(a). A friend gives you a free ticket to Saturday's Cal football game. Going means skipping a 3-hour shift at your campus job, which pays $25 per hour. What is the opportunity cost of going to the game? (Hint: free is not the same as costless.)

Show answer

The opportunity cost of going is the value of the next best alternative you give up, which here is the shift: 3 hours × $25 = $75 of foregone earnings. The ticket itself costs you nothing out of pocket, but the time does. You should go only if the game is worth more than $75 to you.

Full credit: $75, identified as foregone earnings (the value of the next best use of your time). Saying the cost is $0 because the ticket is free earns nothing.

2 points 1(b). Maya and Theo are finishing a group project. In one hour, Maya can write 4 pages of the report or make 8 slides. In one hour, Theo can write 1 page or make 4 slides. Maya is faster at both tasks. Who should make the slides, and why?

Show answer
  1. Maya's opportunity cost of one slide: 4 pages ÷ 8 slides = ½ page of report.
  2. Theo's opportunity cost of one slide: 1 page ÷ 4 slides = ¼ page of report.
  3. Theo gives up less report to make a slide, so Theo has the comparative advantage in slides. Maya's opportunity cost of a page (2 slides) is lower than Theo's (4 slides), so Maya has the comparative advantage in writing.

Theo makes the slides and Maya writes, even though Maya has the absolute advantage in both. Specialization follows the lower opportunity cost, not who is faster.

Full credit: Theo makes slides, Maya writes, with the reason that Theo's opportunity cost of a slide (¼ page) is lower than Maya's (½ page). Saying Maya should do both because she is faster gets no credit.

2 points 1(c). A taqueria on Southside raises the price of a burrito from $11 to $13, and it sells fewer burritos. A friend says, "Demand for burritos fell." Is your friend using the right words? Briefly explain.

Show answer

No. A change in the good's own price does not shift its demand curve. The higher price moves buyers along the same demand curve to a lower quantity demanded. "Demand fell" would mean the whole curve shifted left, so that buyers wanted fewer burritos at every price, which would take a change in something other than the burrito's price, such as tastes, incomes, or the price of a substitute.

Full credit: "quantity demanded fell," a movement along the demand curve, not a shift of demand. Explain that only a change in something other than the good's own price shifts the curve.

2 points 1(d). A late spring frost destroys 12% of the California almond crop, and the crop cannot be replaced this year. Suppose the price elasticity of demand for almonds is −0.4. By about what percent will the price of almonds rise? Does growers' total revenue rise or fall? Show your work.

Show answer
  1. This is the reverse use of elasticity. Start from ε = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP and solve for the price change: %ΔP = %ΔQ ÷ ε.
  2. Quantity falls 12%, so %ΔP = −12% ÷ −0.4 = +30%.
  3. Revenue: demand is inelastic (0.4 is less than 1), so the price rises by more, in percent, than the quantity falls. New revenue is about 1.30 × 0.88 = 1.144 times the old revenue.

The price rises about 30%, and growers' total revenue rises, by about 14%, because demand is inelastic.

Full credit: +30% from −12% ÷ −0.4, and revenue rises because demand is inelastic. Dividing the wrong way (−0.4 ÷ −12%, or −12% × −0.4 = about 5%) gets no credit for the number.

2 points 1(e). You spend your weekly food budget on boba ($6 a cup) and burritos ($12 each). At your current purchases, the last cup of boba gives you 30 units of marginal utility and the last burrito gives you 48. Recall the Rational Spending Rule. Are you spending your budget the best way? If not, what should you change?

Show answer
  1. Marginal utility per dollar for boba: 30 ÷ $6 = 5.
  2. Marginal utility per dollar for burritos: 48 ÷ $12 = 4.

The Rational Spending Rule says the last dollar spent on each good should bring the same marginal utility. Here a dollar on boba brings 5 and a dollar on burritos brings 4, so you are not optimizing. Buy more boba and fewer burritos. As you buy more boba, its marginal utility falls; as you buy fewer burritos, theirs rises, until the two ratios are equal.

Full credit: compute 5 and 4, say they are not equal, and shift spending toward boba, with diminishing marginal utility as the reason the ratios come together. Comparing 30 and 48 without dividing by price gets the direction wrong.

2 points 1(f). A tax on a good collects revenue for the government, but it also creates a deadweight loss. Why is the deadweight loss called a "loss" when nobody writes a check for it? Which units of the good does the deadweight loss triangle represent?

Show answer

The tax revenue is a transfer from buyers and sellers to the government, so it is still counted in total surplus. The deadweight loss is different: it is the surplus from the units that are no longer traded because of the tax, the units between the after-tax quantity and the original equilibrium quantity. On each of those units, buyers' marginal benefit (willingness to pay) was above sellers' marginal cost, so the trade would have made both sides better off. Because the trades do not happen, that surplus goes to nobody: not buyers, not sellers, not the government.

Full credit: the units between the taxed quantity and the original equilibrium, each with marginal benefit above marginal cost, so surplus is lost that no one receives (revenue is a transfer, the triangle is not).

2 points 1(g). On the weekend of the Big Game, suppose the City of Berkeley caps the price of a ride-share trip across town at $15. Without the cap, the market price would be $25. What happens to the number of rides, and do the rides go to the people who value them most? Be careful.

Show answer

The cap is a binding price ceiling, because it sits below the $25 equilibrium. Neither curve shifts. At $15, drivers move down the supply curve and offer fewer rides, while riders move down the demand curve and want more. The number of rides actually taken equals the lower quantity supplied, so there are fewer rides than without the cap, and there is a shortage equal to quantity demanded minus quantity supplied. The scarce rides go to whoever is luckiest or waits longest, not necessarily to the riders with the highest willingness to pay, and the lost rides create a deadweight loss.

Full credit: binding ceiling, no curve shifts, rides fall to quantity supplied, shortage = quantity demanded − quantity supplied, and rides are not guaranteed to go to the highest willingness to pay. Saying "supply shifts left" loses the point he warns about.

2 points 1(h). Flu shots are free for students with SHIP. Getting a flu shot also makes it less likely that your roommates and classmates catch the flu. Without any subsidy, would a free market produce too many flu shots, too few, or the efficient number? Name the curve that is different and the policy that would fix it.

Show answer

Too few. This is a positive externality: each shot benefits people outside the transaction, so social marginal benefit is above private marginal benefit. Positive externalities are a story about the demand side. The market stops where private marginal benefit (demand) equals marginal cost, below the efficient quantity Q* where social marginal benefit equals marginal cost. A per-unit subsidy equal to the external marginal benefit raises demand up to the social marginal benefit curve and moves the market to Q*.

Full credit: too few, because social marginal benefit is above private marginal benefit (the demand side), and a per-unit subsidy equal to the external benefit fixes it.

Question 2 (24 points): Matcha ado about something

Matcha is a green tea powder made by grinding tea leaves that were shaded for weeks before harvest, and most of it comes from Japan. Over 2024 and 2025, matcha lattes became a social-media trend, and cafes around Berkeley added them to their menus. Use the round numbers below, which are made up for this question: in 2023, the U.S. market for matcha powder cleared at about $40 per kilogram and about 4 million kilograms per year.

2 points 2(a). Tea bushes take several years to grow before they can be harvested, and matcha leaves must be shaded before picking and then ground slowly between stones. Over a year or two, what is the likely shape of the supply curve for matcha: close to horizontal, close to vertical, or in between? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Supply is steep, close to vertical (inelastic), in the short run. Growers can only harvest the bushes they already have, and new bushes take years to produce leaves, so output cannot expand quickly. Once existing fields and grinding stones are at capacity, getting more matcha means pulling leaves away from other teas or running mills longer at higher cost, so marginal cost rises steeply as quantity rises. Over many years, as new fields mature, supply would become flatter.

Full credit: steep or inelastic, because output cannot expand quickly (bushes take years, capacity is fixed), so marginal cost rises steeply with quantity.

2 points 2(b). At a cafe, a matcha latte sits on the menu next to coffee, chai, and other teas. What is the likely shape of the market demand curve for matcha? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Demand is relatively flat (elastic), and in any case much flatter than supply. Buyers have close substitutes in coffee, chai, and other green teas, so if the price of matcha rises, many of them switch, and quantity demanded falls a lot. This is the opposite of the egg question on the Fall 2025 midterm, where there was no single substitute and demand was steep.

Full credit: relatively flat or elastic, because there are close substitutes. Edwards gives credit for reasoned answers here, so "in between" with the substitutes reason also works, as long as your demand is drawn flatter than supply in 2(c).

4 points 2(c). On the axes below, draw typical supply and demand for the U.S. matcha market in 2023, at about $40 per kilogram and 4 million kilograms per year. Make sure to model that equilibrium and the slopes from your answers to 2(a) and 2(b), but do not worry about other specifics.

Show answer
  1. Mark the equilibrium first: 4 million on the quantity axis and $40 on the price axis, with dotted lines to both axes.
  2. Draw supply steep through that point (from 2(a)).
  3. Draw demand flat through the same point (from 2(b)).
  4. Label both curves and the equilibrium values.

Full credit: both curves labeled, crossing at (4 million, $40) with dotted lines to the axes, supply clearly steeper than demand.

2 points 2(d). Suppose that between 2023 and 2025 the price of matcha powder rose from $40 to $120 per kilogram, and the quantity sold rose from 4 million to 5 million kilograms per year. One headline blamed a hot summer that hurt the harvest in Japan. Was the main shock to this market a supply shock or a demand shock? Briefly explain.

Show answer

A demand shock: demand shifted right as matcha went viral. Price and quantity both rose, and only a rightward shift of demand moves the equilibrium up and to the right along an upward-sloping supply curve. A bad harvest is a supply shock: supply would shift left, which raises the price but lowers the quantity sold. Quantity rose here, so even if the hot summer hurt supply a little, the demand increase was the main story.

Full credit: demand shock, with the reason that price and quantity rose together; a supply decrease would have lowered quantity.

4 points 2(e). Redraw supply and demand from 2(c), and also depict the events of 2025, with values when you know them. Assume only the curve you picked in 2(d) shifted.

Show answer
  1. Redraw the 2023 curves: steep supply and flat demand crossing at (4 million, $40).
  2. Shift demand to the right and label it Demand'. Supply does not move.
  3. The new equilibrium is where Demand' crosses the same supply curve, at (5 million, $120). Mark it with dotted lines to both axes.
  4. Because supply is steep, the big increase in demand shows up mostly as a higher price (it tripled) and only a little as more quantity (up 25%).

Full credit: original curves plus a labeled rightward shift of demand, supply unchanged, new equilibrium at (5 million, $120) with dotted lines, old equilibrium at (4 million, $40) still marked. Shifting supply instead loses most of the points.

2 points 2(f). Assume supply did not shift between 2023 and 2025, so both points from 2(d) lie on the same supply curve. Calculate the price elasticity of supply. Is supply elastic or inelastic? Show your work.

Show answer
  1. Percent change in quantity, from the starting value: (5 − 4) ÷ 4 = +25%.
  2. Percent change in price, from the starting value: ($120 − $40) ÷ $40 = +200%.
  3. εS = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP = 25% ÷ 200% = 0.125.

The elasticity of supply is 0.125, far below 1, so supply is very inelastic, which matches the steep supply curve from 2(a). Supply elasticity is positive because price and quantity move in the same direction along supply.

Full credit: 0.125 (or 1/8), shown as 25% ÷ 200%, and "inelastic" because it is less than 1. Using 120 − 40 = 80 as if it were a percent, or dividing price by quantity, gets no credit for the number.

4 points 2(g). A cafe on Telegraph Avenue raised the price of its matcha latte from $6.00 to $7.20, and its weekly latte sales fell from 500 to 350. Calculate the price elasticity of demand for this cafe's lattes. Is it elastic or inelastic? Did the cafe's revenue from matcha lattes rise or fall? Why might one cafe's demand be more elastic than demand in the whole market? Show your work.

Show answer
  1. Percent change in price: ($7.20 − $6.00) ÷ $6.00 = +20%.
  2. Percent change in quantity: (350 − 500) ÷ 500 = −30%.
  3. ε = −30% ÷ 20% = −1.5. The size, 1.5, is above 1, so demand is elastic.
  4. Revenue before: $6.00 × 500 = $3,000 per week. Revenue after: $7.20 × 350 = $2,520 per week. Revenue fell by $480, as it must when demand is elastic and the price rises.

One cafe's demand is more elastic than the market's because a single cafe has many close substitutes that the whole market does not: the same matcha latte at the other cafes a block away. If one cafe raises its price, its customers can walk down the street. If every seller's price rises, buyers can only switch to other drinks.

Full credit: ε = −1.5 with the work shown, "elastic," revenue fell from $3,000 to $2,520, and the reason: other cafes selling the same drink are close substitutes for one cafe.

4 points 2(h). Suppose some long-established tea sellers in Kyoto kept their prices well below the new market price and limited each customer to one tin. Within days, tins from those sellers appeared online at three times the shop price. Use what you know about prices held below equilibrium to explain what happened. Why might a seller choose to do this instead of charging the market price?

Show answer

Holding the price below equilibrium works like a price ceiling. At the low price, quantity demanded is far above the quantity the sellers can supply, so there is a shortage. Instead of the price rationing the matcha, the one-tin limit and luck (who shows up first) do. That means the tins do not necessarily go to the buyers with the highest willingness to pay, so some buyers resell to them online at prices near what those buyers are actually willing to pay, which is why resale prices were so far above the shop price.

A seller might accept this because customers value fairness: raising prices sharply in a boom can look like price gouging and damage a reputation built over generations. A long-lived seller also cares about keeping loyal regular customers for years after the trend fades, which is worth more than one year of high prices. This is the same logic as Trader Joe's selling eggs below the market price with a limit in 2025.

Full credit: shortage because quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the held-down price, rationing by limits instead of price (so not to the highest willingness to pay, which explains resale), plus one reason such as fairness, reputation, or long-run customers.

Question 3 (18 points): Lime and punishment

Suppose the City of Berkeley gives a single company, Oski Scoots, the only permit to rent electric scooters in the city. Its scooters are painted lime green. Each ride costs the company $2 in electricity, repairs, and staff time to collect and recharge scooters, no matter how many rides it sells, and the company has no fixed costs (assume the permit is free). At a price of $10 per ride, nobody rides; each $1 cut in the price adds 1,000 rides per day.

2 points 3(a). Is Oski Scoots a price taker, like a perfectly competitive firm? Where does its market power come from?

Show answer

No, it is not a price taker. Oski Scoots is the only seller of scooter rides in Berkeley, so it faces the whole downward-sloping market demand curve and chooses its price. Its market power comes from a barrier to entry: the city's exclusive permit is a legal barrier, so no rival can enter even if prices are high. Riders have imperfect substitutes (walking, bikes, the bus), but no other scooter.

Full credit: market power, not a price taker, with the exclusive permit named as the barrier to entry.

4 points 3(b). I have drawn the marginal cost (MC) curve for you. Draw the demand curve and any other curves you need to find the market outcome under monopoly. Label the monopoly quantity Qm and price Pm, with values.

Show answer
  1. Demand: from the story, demand starts at $10 with 0 rides and falls $1 for every 1,000 rides, so it runs from $10 at zero rides down to $0 at 10,000 rides: P = 10 − Q, with Q in thousands.
  2. Marginal revenue has the same intercept and is twice as steep: MR = 10 − 2Q, from $10 down to 5,000 rides, halfway to demand's quantity intercept.
  3. Step 1: the monopolist picks the quantity where MR = MC: 10 − 2Q = 2, so Qm = 4,000 rides per day.
  4. Step 2: go straight up to demand for the price: P = 10 − 4 = Pm = $6 per ride.

Full credit: demand from $10 to 10,000 rides, MR from $10 to 5,000 rides (twice as steep), Qm = 4,000 where MR = MC, and Pm = $6 read off demand, with dotted lines. Reading the price off MR or MC ($2) is the classic mistake.

2 points 3(c). What is Oski Scoots' profit per day? Show your work.

Show answer

With zero fixed costs and a constant marginal cost, total cost is just $2 × 4,000 = $8,000 per day (the area under MC). Total revenue is $6 × 4,000 = $24,000 per day. Profit = $24,000 − $8,000 = $16,000 per day, the same as (P − MC) × Q = ($6 − $2) × 4,000.

Full credit: $16,000 per day, with revenue minus cost (or (P − MC) × Q) shown.

3 points 3(d). Ignoring anything else for now, how many rides per day would be efficient? Calculate the deadweight loss of monopoly per day, and briefly explain why Oski Scoots chooses not to sell the efficient number.

Show answer

The efficient quantity is where demand (marginal benefit) meets marginal cost: 10 − Q = 2, so 8,000 rides per day at a price of $2. The monopoly sells only 4,000.

Deadweight loss is the triangle between demand and MC from 4,000 to 8,000 rides: ½ × 4,000 × ($6 − $2) = $8,000 per day.

Why it stops short: to sell one more ride, Oski Scoots must cut the price on every ride, not just the new one, so its marginal revenue is below the price. Past 4,000 rides, the extra revenue from a ride is less than its $2 cost, even though riders' willingness to pay for rides 4,000 to 8,000 is above $2. Those rides would create surplus, but they would lower profit, so they do not happen.

Full credit: 8,000 rides, deadweight loss of $8,000 per day, and the reason: MR is below price because a price cut applies to all rides, so the monopolist stops where MR = MC even though willingness to pay is above MC.

3 points 3(e). Riders often leave scooters lying on sidewalks, where they block wheelchair users and trip pedestrians. Suppose each ride imposes $2 of costs on people who are not part of the transaction. What is the socially efficient number of rides now? Does Oski Scoots sell too many rides, too few, or the efficient number? What is the deadweight loss at the monopoly outcome?

Show answer
  1. This is a negative externality, so it moves the cost side: social marginal cost = MC + external marginal cost = $2 + $2 = $4 per ride.
  2. Efficient quantity: demand equals social marginal cost, 10 − Q = 4, so Q* = 6,000 rides per day.
  3. The monopolist ignores the external cost and still sells 4,000, which is too few even now: its markup cuts output by more than the externality calls for.
  4. Deadweight loss: the triangle between demand and social marginal cost from 4,000 to 6,000 rides: ½ × 2,000 × ($6 − $4) = $2,000 per day.

Full credit: SMC = $4, Q* = 6,000, "too few," and deadweight loss of $2,000 per day. Saying the monopoly sells too many because of the pollution misses that the markup already cut output below Q*.

4 points 3(f). The city council proposes a fee of $2 per ride, equal to the external cost, to be paid by Oski Scoots. What quantity and price will the monopolist choose with the fee? Does the fee move the market closer to the efficient number of rides or further away? Explain. (Hint: a per-ride fee raises the monopolist's marginal cost.)

Show answer
  1. The fee raises Oski Scoots' marginal cost from $2 to $4 per ride.
  2. Step 1: MR = new MC: 10 − 2Q = 4, so Q = 3,000 rides per day.
  3. Step 2: price off demand: P = 10 − 3 = $7 per ride.
  4. The efficient quantity is still 6,000. The fee moves the market further away, from 4,000 rides to 3,000. Deadweight loss grows from $2,000 to ½ × 3,000 × ($7 − $4) = $4,500 per day.

A fee equal to the external cost is the right corrective (Pigouvian) tax for a competitive market, which overproduces when there is a negative externality. A monopolist already underproduces because of its markup, so taxing it cuts output further below the efficient level. A better policy here would target the problem directly (fines for blocking sidewalks, required parking spots) or open the market to more scooter companies.

Full credit: Q = 3,000 and P = $7 from MR = MC + fee, "further away" from Q* = 6,000, and the reason that the monopoly already sold too few rides, so a full Pigouvian fee overcorrects.
