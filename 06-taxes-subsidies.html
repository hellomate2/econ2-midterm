Classes 6 and 7 · Elasticity applied

Taxes and subsidies

A per-unit tax or subsidy drives a vertical wedge between demand and supply, and elasticity decides who really pays, how much revenue comes in, and how big the deadweight loss is. Expect a graph with labeled areas, a "who bears the tax and why" short answer, or a quick calculation with linear curves like Problem Set 2.

How it shows up on the exam

The Fall 2025 midterm had no tax question, but this year taxes got two full lectures (Classes 6 and 7) and both problem sets drilled them. Edwards said in Class 11 that topics "we covered more" will take more prominence on this midterm. Here is what he has asked so far.

Format to expect: a 2-point "Briefly explain" box (who bears this tax, and why), or a 4-point graph on pre-printed axes. On the Fall 2025 midterm every graphing part was worth 4 points. He often draws one curve for you and asks you to add the rest, "with values when you know them."

For incidence, the grade comes from the argument for the curve's shape. Say who the sellers or buyers are, whether they can walk away (sell elsewhere, buy something else, wait), and conclude which side is more inelastic and so bears more. In Class 7 he said a drawing different from his still earns partial credit if the argument holds together.

For a graph, label: the wedge (tax amount) at the new quantity Q2, PD (price buyers pay), PS (price sellers keep), P*, Q1, Q2, CS, PS, tax revenue, and the DWL triangle pointing at the old equilibrium. Give the numbers whenever the curves have equations.

The ideas, explained

1. A tax is a vertical wedge

A per-unit tax (Edwards sometimes writes it as τ, "tau") is a fixed number of dollars charged on every unit sold, like his 50 cents per gallon of gasoline or Berkeley's 1 cent per ounce of soda. Once the tax exists, the buyer and the seller no longer face the same price. The buyer pays PD. The government takes the tax. The seller keeps what is left, PS = PD − tax.

On the graph, this means you look for the quantity where the vertical distance between demand (above) and supply (below) is exactly the tax. That quantity is the new quantity Q2. Read PD off the demand curve at Q2, because demand tells you what buyers are willing to pay for that many units. Read PS off the supply curve at Q2, because supply is marginal cost and tells you the lowest price sellers will accept for that many units.

Why does quantity fall? Before the tax, the last unit traded at Q1 was worth exactly its cost (MB = MC). With a tax, a unit only gets traded if the buyer's willingness to pay exceeds the seller's marginal cost by at least the tax. Units near Q1 no longer clear that bar, so they stop being traded.

A tax is a vertical wedge equal to the tax, placed at the new quantity Q2. Buyers pay PD (on demand), sellers keep PS = PD − tax (on supply). Quantity falls from Q1 to Q2. The buyers' price typically rises by less than the tax.

A note on symbols: Edwards wrote PD and PS for the two prices in Class 7. Do not mix up PS, the price sellers keep, with PS, producer surplus. On an exam, writing "price sellers receive" in words avoids the problem.

2. It does not matter who writes the check

The slides work through a 50 cents per gallon gas tax collected from producers. If the tax is collected from producers, it acts like an extra marginal cost, so the supply curve shifts up by exactly 50 cents. If the same tax is collected from consumers, it acts like a lower marginal benefit (buyers will now pay the store 50 cents less than before for each gallon, since they also owe the tax), so demand shifts down by 50 cents. Either way you end up with the same Q2, the same price for buyers, and the same price for sellers. The wedge is the same size in the same place.

This is the difference between statutory incidence (who the law says pays) and economic incidence (whose price actually changes). Only economic incidence matters, and it depends on elasticities. The payroll tax is his favorite example: the law splits the 12.4% Social Security tax half from the employer and half from the worker, but that split says nothing about who really bears it.

A tax on sellers shifts supply up by the tax. A tax on buyers shifts demand down by the tax. Both give the same equilibrium: same quantity, same buyer price, same seller price.

3. The more inelastic side bears more

The burden on buyers is how much their price rises (PD − P*). The burden on sellers is how much the price they keep falls (P* − PS). The two pieces add up to the tax. Which piece is bigger depends on who can get away.

Recall from the elasticity unit: inelastic means steep and elastic means flat. A side of the market that is inelastic cannot easily change its quantity when its price changes. It has no good substitute, no other place to sell, or it already committed to its plans. When the wedge appears, the side that can walk away does so, and the price it faces barely moves. The side that cannot walk away is stuck absorbing the price change.

"Inelastic factors bear the tax while elastic factors avoid the tax" (Class 6 slides). Buyers bear more when demand is less elastic; sellers bear more when supply is less elastic. If both sides are about equally elastic, they split it about evenly.

Edwards drilled this with four stories. Learn the reason for each curve's shape, since that reason is what earns the points.

You can answer "both" only if you argue supply is not flat; for example, he said a statewide soda tax might put some of the burden on sellers, since moving sales out of a whole state is harder than moving them out of one city.

4. The four limit cases

When a curve is perfectly flat or perfectly vertical, the incidence is total, and you should be able to draw each case quickly.

CaseWho bears the taxQuantityDWL
Flat (perfectly elastic) supplyBuyers, all of it: PD = P* + tax, PS = P*FallsYes
Vertical (perfectly inelastic) supplySellers, all of it: PD = P*, PS = P* − taxUnchangedZero
Vertical (perfectly inelastic) demandBuyers, all of itUnchangedZero
Flat (perfectly elastic) demandSellers, all of itFallsYes

The pattern: the flat side never bears anything, because it would rather leave the market than accept a different price. The vertical side bears everything, because its quantity is fixed no matter what. Edwards' examples of nearly vertical supply were Cal football tickets (a fixed number of seats) and H-1B visas under a quota. The Fall 2025 final used soybeans that are already harvested and cannot be stored: supply is vertical, so a shock moves price all the way along demand and quantity stays put.

5. Welfare: revenue, surplus, and deadweight loss

The slide title says it all: a tax "extracts surplus from consumers and producers and creates deadweight loss." Here is the standard picture, using the curves from his practice problems: demand P = 10 − Q, supply P = Q, and a $2 tax.

  • Consumer surplus is the triangle under demand and above PD, out to Q2.
  • Producer surplus is the triangle above supply and below PS, out to Q2.
  • Tax revenue is the rectangle between PD and PS from 0 to Q2: revenue = tax × Q2. (The slides also show it as the parallelogram between the old and shifted supply curves; same area.)
  • Deadweight loss is the triangle between demand and supply from Q2 to Q1, pointing at the old equilibrium: DWL = ½ × tax × (Q1 − Q2).

Revenue is not a loss to society. It moves from buyers and sellers to the government, which can spend it. So total surplus with a tax is CS + PS + revenue, and the only true loss is the DWL. In the example: 12.5 + 12.5 = 25 before, and 8 + 8 + 8 + 1 = 25 after, so the pieces add back up.

What is the DWL in plain words? Edwards put it this way in Class 6: the price you see is now higher, and "you might decide not to buy it because of the tax. No other reason." Each unit between Q2 and Q1 was worth more to a buyer than it cost a seller to make (MB > MC), so trading it would have created surplus. The tax stops those trades, and the surplus they would have created simply vanishes. Nobody collects it.

6. Why DWL grows with elasticity

The DWL triangle has height equal to the tax and width equal to the drop in quantity, Q1 − Q2. For the same tax, the more elastic the curves, the more quantity falls, so the triangle is wider. Look back at the incidence pair: with steep demand the quantity falls from 5 to 4.4 and the DWL is ½ × 3 × 0.6 = 0.9; with flat demand quantity falls to 2.6 and the DWL is ½ × 3 × 2.4 = 3.6. If a curve is perfectly vertical, quantity does not change at all and the DWL is zero.

"Deadweight loss is larger when demand and/or supply are elastic. Taxing inelastic goods like cigarettes is more efficient." (Class 7 slides). Inelastic means quantity barely responds, so the tax raises revenue without destroying many trades.

A careful point on cigarettes. In Class 5 Edwards said a cigarette tax "doesn't change the consumer surplus that much." That slipped: with inelastic demand, consumers bear most of the tax, so their surplus does fall. What stays small is the drop in quantity and therefore the DWL. Most of the lost consumer surplus turns into tax revenue instead of disappearing.

7. No misallocation under a tax

A binding price ceiling creates a shortage, so the good gets handed out by queues, luck, or who got there first, and it may not reach the people who value it most (the long-time tenant in a rent-controlled apartment versus the student who would pay more). That misallocation is a loss on top of the DWL triangle.

A tax does not do this. The price still rations the good: every buyer willing to pay PD gets it, and every seller whose marginal cost is below PS sells. So the units that are still traded go to the buyers with the highest willingness to pay and come from the sellers with the lowest marginal cost. The slide's phrasing: production and consumption "are still allocated according to willingness to pay and supply," so there is no misallocation. The whole efficiency loss of a tax is the DWL triangle.

8. Solving a tax with equations

When the curves have equations, the recipe is short. The first worked example below runs it with demand P = 10 − Q, supply P = Q, and a $2 tax.

  1. Find the no-tax equilibrium (Q1, P*) by setting demand price equal to supply price.
  2. Insert the wedge: (demand price) = (supply price) + tax. Solve for Q2.
  3. Read PD off demand at Q2 and PS off supply at Q2; check that they differ by the tax.
  4. Compute revenue = tax × Q2, the CS and PS triangles, and DWL = ½ × tax × (Q1 − Q2). Check that CS + PS + revenue + DWL equals the no-tax CS + PS.
  5. Incidence: compare PD − P* (buyers' burden) with P* − PS (sellers' burden).

For a subsidy, flip the wedge: the price sellers receive equals the price buyers pay plus the subsidy, so set (demand price) + subsidy = (supply price).

9. Subsidies: a negative tax

Every subsidy slide carries the same title: "Subsidy is like a negative tax and also creates deadweight loss." A per-unit subsidy pays sellers an extra amount for each unit. Now sellers receive more than buyers pay: Pp (price producers receive) = Pc (price consumers pay) + subsidy. The wedge is still vertical and still equals the subsidy, but it now sits to the right of Q1, at a larger quantity Q2, where supply is above demand.

  • subsidy cost = subsidy × Q2, the rectangle between Pp and Pc from 0 to Q2.
  • DWL = ½ × subsidy × (Q2 − Q1), the triangle between supply (above) and demand (below) from Q1 to Q2, pointing back at Q1.
  • CS gain + PS gain = subsidy cost − DWL. In the example: 5.5 + 5.5 = 12 − 1.

Why is there a loss when everyone in the market gains? Because the extra units between Q1 and Q2 cost more to produce than they are worth to buyers. At Q2 = 6, marginal cost is $6 but marginal benefit is only $4. The subsidy pushes production above the efficient level, so at the new quantity MB < MC. Taxpayers pay for those units, and the gap between their cost and their value is gone. In Class 6 he said the extra cost of a subsidy "can't really be recovered in total." His real examples: farm price supports and crop insurance (which lead to overproduction), the government's stake in Intel, and COVID-19 vaccine purchases.

Tax: quantity below Q1, so MB > MC on the lost units. Subsidy: quantity above Q1, so MB < MC on the extra units. Either way the DWL triangle points toward the efficient quantity and grows outward to the current quantity.

10. When supply is flat: Problem Set 2, question 3

Problem Set 2 set up a domestic oil market where supply is flat at the world price: demand P = 5 − Q and MC = 1. Without a tax, the market is at Q = 4 and P = 1. CS is a triangle with base 4 and height 4, so CS = 8. PS is zero, because price equals marginal cost on every unit, so there is no area between the price and the supply curve.

With the $2 tax "paid by producers," the sellers' price stays at $1 and buyers pay the full $2 more, because supply is perfectly elastic. The key's wording is worth copying: the tax "drives a vertical wedge between supply and demand, and it doesn't matter who is asked to send the tax to the government." The full calculation is the last worked example below.

11. Evidence: the model is a guide, not gospel

Finnish hairdressers. Benzarti and coauthors (2020) studied a value-added tax (VAT, Europe's sales tax, usually around 20%) on hairdressers in Finland. The VAT on hairdressing was cut by 14 percentage points in January 2007, and the cut was repealed (raised back by 14 points) in January 2012. Beauty salons, whose tax did not change, served as the comparison group. When the tax fell, hairdressers' prices fell by only about half the cut. When the tax rose, prices rose by almost the full amount. The basic model says a cut and an increase of the same size should move prices by the same amount, so this asymmetry is not what the model predicts. The likely explanation on the slides: consumers do not pay close attention to taxes, so sellers pocket part of a tax cut, while a tax increase gives sellers an easy way to justify raising prices. Edwards' lesson: "We should not take its predictions as gospel."

Reading the EU gas tax scatter (Problem Set 1, Q5). Each dot is one EU country, with its gas tax per liter on the x-axis and its gas price per liter on the y-axis. In Class 4 he said that judging what this kind of plot shows is "very, very similar to what you'll be asked to do on an exam." The skill is reading the slope of the cloud of dots.

  • Slope 1: each extra euro of tax raises the price by one euro. Buyers bear the whole tax, which is what you expect if gas supply is perfectly elastic (the world oil price plus station costs).
  • Slope between 0 and 1: buyers and sellers share it. Slope 0.5 means an even split.
  • Slope 0: taxes do not move prices at all, so sellers bear the whole tax.
  • Slope above 1 (the actual data: about 2 by eye, about 1.3 from the regression): prices rise by more than the tax. Incidence alone cannot do that, since buyers cannot bear more than the whole tax. The official answer is selection: less advanced EU countries have cheaper land and labor, which makes their gas cheaper, and they also happen to have lower gas taxes. So countries with high taxes also have high costs for other reasons, and a simple line through the dots mixes the two. (A side fact, not in his key: European VAT is charged on the price including the gas tax, which also pushes the pump price up by a bit more than the tax.)

Watch the axes when you read the slope. In the Problem Set 1 key he notes that one grid box was 0.1 euro wide but 0.2 euro tall, so a line through the box corners has slope 2, even though it looks like a 45-degree line.

Worked examples

Example: a $2 tax with linear curves

Demand is P = 10 − Q and supply is P = Q. A $2 per-unit tax is collected from sellers. Find everything.

  1. No tax: 10 − Q = Q, so Q1 = 5, P* = 5. CS = ½ × 5 × 5 = 12.5 and PS = 12.5.
  2. With the tax, the demand price is $2 above the supply price: 10 − Q = Q + 2, so 2Q = 8 and Q2 = 4.
  3. Buyers pay PD = 10 − 4 = $6. Sellers keep PS = $4.
  4. Revenue = 2 × 4 = 8. CS = ½ × 4 × (10 − 6) = 8. PS = ½ × 4 × 4 = 8. DWL = ½ × 2 × (5 − 4) = 1.
  5. Check: 8 + 8 + 8 + 1 = 25 = 12.5 + 12.5.
  6. Collecting the tax from buyers instead (demand shifts down by $2: P = 8 − Q) gives 8 − Q = Q, Q = 4, the same answer.

Answer: Q2 = 4, buyers pay $6, sellers keep $4, revenue 8, CS 8, PS 8, DWL 1. Each side bears $1 of the $2 because the two curves are equally steep.

Example: steeper demand, so buyers bear more

Demand is P = 12 − 2Q and supply is P = Q. A $3 tax is imposed. Who bears more?

  1. No tax: 12 − 2Q = Q, so Q1 = 4 and P* = 4. CS = ½ × 4 × (12 − 4) = 16, PS = ½ × 4 × 4 = 8.
  2. Tax: 12 − 2Q = Q + 3, so 3Q = 9 and Q2 = 3.
  3. PD = 12 − 6 = $6, and PS = $3.
  4. Buyers' price rose 6 − 4 = $2. Sellers' price fell 4 − 3 = $1. Together that is the $3 tax.
  5. Revenue = 3 × 3 = 9. CS = ½ × 3 × (12 − 6) = 9. PS = ½ × 3 × 3 = 4.5. DWL = ½ × 3 × (4 − 3) = 1.5. Check: 9 + 4.5 + 9 + 1.5 = 24 = 16 + 8.

Answer: Buyers bear $2 and sellers $1. Demand is steeper (slope −2 against +1), so near the equilibrium buyers are the less elastic side, and the less elastic side bears more.

Example: a $2 subsidy

Same curves as the first example (demand P = 10 − Q, supply P = Q), but now the government pays sellers $2 per unit.

  1. Sellers receive the buyers' price plus $2: (10 − Q) + 2 = Q, so 2Q = 12 and Q2 = 6.
  2. Buyers pay Pc = 10 − 6 = $4. Sellers receive Pp = $6.
  3. Cost to the government = 2 × 6 = $12.
  4. CS = ½ × 6 × (10 − 4) = 18 (up 5.5 from 12.5). PS = ½ × 6 × 6 = 18 (up 5.5).
  5. DWL = ½ × 2 × (6 − 5) = 1. Check: gains of 11 = cost of 12 − DWL of 1.
  6. At Q = 6, MC = 6 but MB = 4, so the units from 5 to 6 cost more than they are worth.

Answer: Q2 = 6, buyers pay $4, sellers get $6, cost $12, DWL 1, and MB < MC at the new quantity.

Example: Problem Set 2, Q3(c), tax with flat MC

Demand P = 5 − Q, supply flat at MC = 1. A $2 tax that the government says producers will pay.

  1. No tax: 5 − Q = 1, so Q = 4 and P = 1. CS = ½ × 4 × 4 = 8, PS = 0.
  2. Tax: 5 − Q = 1 + 2, so Q2 = 2. Buyers pay $3; producers keep $3 − $2 = $1.
  3. CS = ½ × 2 × (5 − 3) = 2. PS = 0. Revenue = 2 × 2 = 4. DWL = ½ × 2 × (4 − 2) = 2.
  4. Check: 2 + 0 + 4 + 2 = 8, the original total.

Answer: Consumers bear all of the tax, even though the law names producers, because supply is perfectly elastic: the sellers' price stays at $1 and the buyers' price rises by the full $2.

Common mistakes

Mistake: Saying the group the law names (producers, employers, the store) bears the tax.

Correct: Who bears it depends only on elasticities. A tax on sellers and a tax on buyers give the same equilibrium. Say so explicitly; the Problem Set 2 key does.

Mistake: Drawing the tax as a gap at the old quantity Q1, or between the old price and some arbitrary line. (Edwards did this himself in Class 7 and corrected it.)

Correct: The wedge goes where the vertical gap between demand and supply equals the tax, which is the new, smaller quantity Q2. The buyers' price is on demand at Q2; the sellers' price is on supply at Q2.

Mistake: Answering "both pay" with no reason, or saying the price always rises by the full tax.

Correct: The price buyers pay usually rises by less than the tax, and the split follows the elasticities. "Both" earns credit only with an argument that neither curve is flat or vertical.

Mistake: Counting tax revenue as part of the deadweight loss.

Correct: Revenue is a transfer to the government, not a loss. The loss is only the DWL triangle between Q2 and Q1. Total surplus with a tax is CS + PS + revenue.

Mistake: Saying a tax on an inelastic good like cigarettes barely hurts consumers.

Correct: With inelastic demand, consumers bear most of the tax, so their surplus falls. What stays small is the drop in quantity and therefore the DWL.

Mistake: Saying a tax causes misallocation like a price ceiling.

Correct: Under a tax the price still rations the good, so the remaining units go to the highest-value buyers and come from the lowest-cost sellers. Only the ceiling, with its shortage, causes misallocation.

Mistake: Putting the subsidy DWL triangle to the left of Q1, or saying a subsidy has no DWL because everyone in the market gains.

Correct: The subsidy pushes quantity above Q1. The DWL triangle sits between Q1 and Q2, with supply above demand, and points back at Q1. At Q2, MB < MC. The gains to buyers and sellers are smaller than the subsidy's cost.

Mistake: Reading a scatter slope of 1.3 as "consumers bear 130% of the tax."

Correct: No side can bear more than the whole tax. A slope above 1 means something besides incidence is going on; the official answer is selection (poorer EU countries have both cheaper costs and lower taxes).

Practice questions

2 points Berkeley considers a tax on beer sold in the city. Most beer sold in Berkeley comes from small local breweries that plan their brewing months ahead and sell only locally. Who will bear most of the tax? Briefly explain.

Show answer

The breweries will bear most of it. They plan output in advance and cannot easily send their beer to other cities, so their supply is inelastic (steep). The side of the market that is more inelastic bears more of a tax, so most of the wedge shows up as a fall in the price the breweries keep, from P* down to PS, while the price buyers pay rises only a little.

Full credit: brewers bear most; supply is inelastic (steep) because output is planned ahead and sold locally; the more inelastic side bears more.

2 points Berkeley taxes soda at 1 cent per ounce, collected at the register. Who pays the tax? Would your answer change for a statewide soda tax?

Show answer

Consumers pay all of it. The sellers are global companies like Coca-Cola and PepsiCo, which can sell their soda in nearby cities instead, so supply to Berkeley is perfectly elastic (flat) at their usual price. With flat supply, the sellers' price does not change and the buyers' price rises by the full tax. That it is collected at the register does not matter. For a statewide tax, moving sales out of the whole state is much harder, so supply is less elastic and sellers might bear some of the tax.

Full credit: consumers bear all of it because supply to a small city is flat (sellers can go elsewhere); a statewide tax could put some burden on sellers.

4 points Demand is P = 12 − 2Q and supply is P = Q. A $3 per-unit tax is collected from sellers. Find the new quantity, the price buyers pay, the price sellers keep, tax revenue, CS, PS, and DWL. Who bears more of the tax, and why?

Show answer

No tax: 12 − 2Q = Q, so Q1 = 4 and P* = 4. With the tax: 12 − 2Q = Q + 3, so Q2 = 3. Buyers pay PD = 12 − 6 = $6; sellers keep PS = $3. Revenue = 3 × 3 = 9. CS = ½ × 3 × (12 − 6) = 9. PS = ½ × 3 × 3 = 4.5. DWL = ½ × 3 × (4 − 3) = 1.5. Check: 9 + 4.5 + 9 + 1.5 = 24, which equals the no-tax CS (16) plus PS (8).

Buyers bear $2 (their price rose from 4 to 6) and sellers bear $1 (their price fell from 4 to 3). Demand is steeper than supply, so buyers are the less elastic side and bear more.

Full credit: Q2 = 3, buyers $6, sellers $3, revenue 9, CS 9, PS 4.5, DWL 1.5; buyers bear $2 of the $3 because demand is steeper (less elastic). On a graph: wedge at Q2, all areas labeled, DWL pointing at (4, 4).

Full credit: (a) Q = 6, P = 2, CS = 18, PS = 0. (b) Q = 4, buyers $4, sellers $2, CS 8, PS 0, revenue 8, DWL 2; consumers bear all of it because supply is flat.

2 points Demand is P = 12 − Q and supply is P = Q. The government pays sellers a $2 subsidy per unit. Find the new quantity, the price buyers pay, the price sellers receive, the cost of the subsidy, and the DWL. Compare MB and MC at the new quantity.

Show answer

Without the subsidy, 12 − Q = Q gives Q1 = 6 and P* = 6. With it, (12 − Q) + 2 = Q, so Q2 = 7. Buyers pay Pc = 12 − 7 = $5; sellers receive Pp = $7. Cost = 2 × 7 = $14. DWL = ½ × 2 × (7 − 6) = 1. At Q = 7, MB (read off demand) is $5 but MC (read off supply) is $7, so MB < MC: the extra unit costs more to produce than buyers value it. (Check: CS and PS each rise from 18 to 24.5, gains of 13 = 14 − 1.)

Full credit: Q2 = 7, Pc = $5, Pp = $7, cost $14, DWL 1, MB < MC at Q2.

2 points Cal football has a fixed number of seats in Memorial Stadium. If the city taxed each ticket sold by $5, who would bear the tax, and how big would the deadweight loss be?

Show answer

The supply of seats is fixed, so supply is perfectly inelastic (vertical). The quantity of tickets does not change, so the price buyers pay stays where it was and the price the seller keeps falls by the full $5: the seller bears all of it. Because quantity does not change, no trades are lost, so the deadweight loss is zero.

Full credit: vertical supply, seller bears the whole $5, quantity unchanged, DWL = 0.

2 points The 12.4% Social Security payroll tax is split by law: half paid by employers, half by workers. Who actually bears most of it? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Workers bear most of it. Most people will work about the same amount whatever the after-tax wage, so labor supply is inelastic. Incidence falls on the more inelastic side, regardless of how the statute splits the payment, so the tax mostly shows up as lower take-home pay. (If you argue labor supply is fairly elastic, a more even split is a reasonable answer, as long as you say why.)

Full credit: workers bear most because labor supply is inelastic; the 50-50 legal split does not decide incidence.

2 points A 50 cents per gallon gas tax is collected from refiners. A senator proposes collecting it at the pump from drivers instead, "so oil companies stop passing it on." What would change? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Nothing real would change. Collected from refiners, the tax shifts supply up by 50 cents; collected from drivers, it shifts demand down by 50 cents. Both give the same quantity, the same price for drivers (including the tax), and the same price for sellers. Who bears it depends on elasticities, and since gas supply is elastic and short-run demand is inelastic, drivers bear most of it either way.

Full credit: same equilibrium either way (supply up by the tax versus demand down by the tax); incidence depends on elasticities, not on who remits.

2 points A plot shows one dot per EU country: gas tax per liter on the horizontal axis, gas price per liter on the vertical axis. What would a best-fit slope of 1 mean? The actual slope is about 1.3. Why might it be steeper than one-for-one?

Show answer

A slope of 1 means each extra euro of tax raises the pump price by one euro, so consumers bear the whole tax, which is what we expect if gas supply is perfectly elastic. A slope above 1 cannot come from incidence alone. The official answer is selection: less advanced EU countries have cheaper land and labor, which lowers their gas prices, and they also have lower gas taxes. So high-tax countries have high prices partly for reasons that have nothing to do with the tax.

Full credit: slope 1 = consumers bear all of it (elastic supply); above 1 = selection, since poorer countries have both lower costs and lower taxes.

2 points When Finland cut the value-added tax on hairdressing in 2007 and raised it back in 2012, what happened to hairdressers' prices? What does this say about the competitive model?

Show answer

Prices fell by only about half of the tax cut, but rose by almost the full amount when the tax went back up. In the competitive model, a cut and an increase of the same size should move prices by the same amount, so this does not fit. A likely reason is that consumers are inattentive to taxes, so sellers keep part of a cut, while a tax increase gives them a ready justification for raising prices. The model is useful but not gospel.

Full credit: cuts about 50% passed through, increases almost fully; the competitive model's prediction failed, so do not treat it as gospel.

2 points The city must raise the same revenue by taxing either cigarettes or restaurant meals (demand elasticity about −2.3). Which tax creates less deadweight loss? Briefly explain.

Show answer

The cigarette tax. Demand for cigarettes is inelastic (addiction, few substitutes), so quantity barely falls when the tax raises the price. The DWL triangle is ½ × tax × (drop in quantity), so a small drop in quantity means a small DWL. Restaurant meal demand is elastic (people can eat at home), so the same tax would cut many more trades and create a bigger DWL.

Full credit: cigarettes, because inelastic demand means quantity falls little, so the DWL triangle is small; DWL grows with elasticity.

2 points Both a $2 tax and a binding price ceiling reduce the quantity traded below the efficient level. Explain one way the losses differ.

Show answer

Under a tax, the price still allocates the good: everyone willing to pay the buyers' price gets it, so the units still traded go to the highest-value buyers and come from the lowest-cost sellers. The only loss is the DWL triangle. Under a binding ceiling there is a shortage, so the good is handed out by queues or luck and may go to people who value it less. That misallocation adds a loss on top of the DWL triangle. (Also, a tax raises revenue for the government; a ceiling does not.)

Full credit: a tax causes no misallocation (price still rations the good); a ceiling's shortage can misallocate, adding loss beyond the triangle.

One-minute review

  • A tax is a vertical wedge equal to the tax at the new quantity Q2: buyers pay PD on demand, sellers keep PS = PD − tax on supply.
  • Who writes the check does not matter: a tax on sellers shifts supply up by the tax, a tax on buyers shifts demand down by it, and the outcome is the same.
  • The more inelastic side bears more. Soda in Berkeley: flat supply, buyers pay all. Local brewery: steep supply, brewer pays most. Payroll tax: workers pay most. European gas: drivers pay most.
  • Flat side bears nothing; vertical side bears everything, and with a vertical curve quantity and DWL do not change.
  • Revenue = tax × Q2. DWL = ½ × tax × (Q1 − Q2), pointing at the old equilibrium. Revenue is a transfer, not a loss.
  • DWL is larger when curves are elastic, so taxing inelastic goods like cigarettes is more efficient.
  • A tax causes no misallocation; a price ceiling can.
  • Solve with equations: demand price = supply price + tax. For D: P = 10 − Q, S: P = Q, $2 tax: Q = 4, buyers $6, sellers $4, revenue 8, CS 8, PS 8, DWL 1.
  • Subsidy: sellers get Pp = Pc + subsidy, Q rises past Q1, cost = subsidy × Q2, DWL beyond Q1, and MB < MC at Q2.
  • Problem Set 2 (P = 5 − Q, MC = 1, $2 tax): buyers bear all, Q = 2, CS 2, PS 0, revenue 4, DWL 2. Finnish VAT: cuts half passed on, increases fully. EU scatter slope about 1.3: selection.
