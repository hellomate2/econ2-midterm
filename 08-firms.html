Classes 8 and 9 · Competitive firms

Competitive firms and supply

This unit explains how a small firm in a competitive market decides how much to produce, why its marginal cost curve is its supply curve, and how all the firms add up to the market supply curve. On the Fall 2025 midterm these ideas were worth 12 points inside the egg question (the marginal cost of an egg, fixed costs as a barrier to entry, and Denny's versus Trader Joe's), and they sit underneath every supply curve you draw.

The ideas, explained

What a firm is trying to do: maximize profit

In Econ 2 we assume firms maximize economic profit, the same way households maximize utility. Real firms may care about other things too, but profit maximization is the model.

Profit = total revenue − total cost. Total revenue = price × quantity sold. Total cost is the opportunity cost of all inputs, including the owner's own time and money.

That last part matters. If a family farmer works her own land, the wage she could have earned elsewhere is part of total cost. Edwards put it this way: the farmer "effectively pays themselves a wage," and that wage is not profit. Economic profit is whatever is left over after every input, including labor, land, and capital, has been paid what it could earn in its next best use.

A firm makes three kinds of decisions. In the short run, it decides how much to produce with the setup it already has. In the long run, it decides whether to expand, shrink, enter the industry, or exit. In both, it chooses its mix of inputs (labor, machines, raw materials). This unit is about the first two.

Perfect competition: a small firm takes the price as given

A perfectly competitive industry has many firms, each small relative to the whole market, and they do not collude. Each firm knows that nothing it does will change the market price. Edwards' examples are an oil jack in North Dakota, which takes the world oil price as given, and a family farm selling at the world price of its crop.

Here is the key picture. The market demand curve slopes down as usual. But the demand curve that one small firm sees, which Edwards labels δ (the Greek letter delta, for "demand the firm sees"), is flat at the market price. It is perfectly elastic. The reason: if the farm asked even a little more than the market price, buyers would go to one of the thousands of other farms selling the same product. And it has no reason to charge less, because it can sell everything it produces at the market price.

Marginal revenue (MR) is the extra revenue from selling one more unit. For a price taker, selling one more unit brings in exactly the market price, and it does not lower the price on any other unit. So MR equals the price. In Edwards' words, "When demand is flat, marginal revenue also is flat." This is the big difference from a monopolist, which has to cut its price to sell more, so its MR is below the price.

For a competitive (price-taking) firm, the demand curve it faces is flat at the market price, so MR = P.

Costs: fixed versus variable

Fixed costs do not change when the firm produces more or less. They are the "pay to play" costs of being in business at all. Variable costs rise when output rises. Total cost is the sum: C(q) = Cf + Cv(q).

The line is not always sharp ("everything's a little shade of gray," he said). In a Class 9 poll, electricity was the answer closest to a fixed cost, and overtime pay was the worst answer, because firms pay overtime only when they ramp up production. Ordinary wages are usually treated as variable. A student asked about benefits like health insurance and 401k contributions: Edwards said these are part of total pay for labor, so they are variable costs, although the HR department needed to set up insurance looks more like a fixed cost.

Marginal cost (MC) is the extra cost of producing one more unit. Since fixed costs do not change when you make one more unit, marginal cost comes entirely from variable cost. This has a big consequence:

Fixed cost does not change marginal cost. So a change in fixed cost does not change how much an operating firm produces. It changes only the firm's profit and whether it is worth entering at all.

Adding up marginal cost over every unit produced gives total variable cost. On a graph, total variable cost is the area under the MC curve up to the quantity produced.

What does the MC curve look like? MC might fall at first because of economies of scale (Edwards' example: you would not run the giant Dwinelle 155 lecture hall for 20 students). But eventually MC rises because of constraints, such as having only one factory or one city's labor market to hire from. In Econ 2, marginal cost always slopes upward. Edwards: "Marginal cost increases in Econ 2. That's what we're gonna say, always does." The competitive model does not work well with falling MC, which tends to lead to monopoly.

How much to produce: MR = MC, so P = MC

Every profit-maximizing firm, competitive or monopoly, produces where marginal revenue equals marginal cost. The logic is about the next unit. If the next unit brings in more revenue than it costs (MR above MC), producing it raises profit, so make it. If the last unit cost more than it brought in (MC above MR), producing it lowered profit, so make less. Profit is highest where the two are equal.

For a competitive firm, MR is the price, so the rule becomes:

A competitive firm produces the quantity where P = MC.

Example: the market price is $10 and the firm's marginal cost is MC = 2q. Set 10 = 2q, so q = 5. At the fifth unit, MC is exactly $10. Units 1 through 4 cost less than $10 each and earn $10, so they add to profit. A sixth unit would cost $12 and earn only $10, so it would lose money. You never need calculus for this: set price equal to marginal cost and solve.

The firm's supply curve is its marginal cost curve

Now raise the price from $10 to $14. The flat MR line moves up, and the firm moves up along its MC curve until MC reaches $14. Setting 14 = 2q gives q = 7. Every price picks out a quantity on the MC curve. That is exactly what a supply curve does.

You can read the supply curve two ways. Horizontally: given a price, how much will the firm supply? Vertically: given a quantity, what price does it take to get the firm to supply it? That price is the firm's marginal cost at that quantity. Both readings say the same thing: the firm's supply curve is its MC curve. Edwards: "for your intuition, you should basically think of it this way, yes, that the marginal cost curve equals the supply curve."

Industry supply: add the firms horizontally

The market supply curve is the horizontal sum of all the firms' supply curves. At each price, ask how much each firm supplies, then add up those quantities. Because each firm's supply curve is its MC curve, the market supply curve is also the industry's marginal cost curve.

Edwards' example from the slides: 100 identical firms. Each firm's marginal cost is $5 at 1,000 units, $6 at 2,000 units, and so on. At a price of $5, each firm supplies 1,000 units, so the industry supplies 100 × 1,000 = 100,000 units. At $6, each supplies 2,000, so the industry supplies 200,000. The industry's MC is $5 at 100,000 units and $6 at 200,000 units. A student asked in class how the two graphs connect; Edwards said to picture all the firm graphs "stacked up coming out of the screen" and add up their quantities at each marginal cost.

This is also why producer surplus is the area above the supply curve and below the price (Fall 2025 midterm 1(h)). Each unit sells at the market price but costs only its marginal cost, which is the height of the supply curve. Adding up price minus marginal cost over all units gives the area between the price line and supply.

Notation note: Edwards uses lowercase letters (or Greek letters like δ) for one firm and uppercase for the market: mc and s for the firm, MC and S for the industry. He admitted this sometimes confuses him too.

The entry decision: compare "total marginal profit" with fixed cost

Should a firm enter the industry at all? Once it is operating, it will produce where P = MC. At that quantity, its revenue is the rectangle P × q, and its variable cost is the area under MC. The difference, the area between the price line and the MC curve from zero up to q, is what Edwards calls the "total marginal profit." It is revenue minus variable cost, the firm's producer surplus.

Enter if the area between P and MC (revenue minus variable cost) is larger than the fixed cost. If it is smaller, the firm cannot cover its fixed cost and should stay out.

With the numbers in the graph: if the fixed cost is 20, the firm enters and earns a profit of 25 − 20 = 5. If the fixed cost is 30, it would lose 5, so it stays out. In both cases the quantity it would produce if it operated is still 5, because fixed cost does not touch MC.

Profit is not producer surplus (and not the return to inputs)

Edwards flagged this as hard: "It's not the same as producer surplus. It's not the same as returns to capital or other factor inputs." Keep three things apart:

  • Producer surplus = revenue − variable cost = the area between the price and the MC (supply) curve.
  • Profit = revenue − total cost = producer surplus − fixed cost.
  • Returns to inputs (wages for labor, rent for land and capital) are costs the firm pays. They are counted inside total cost, so they are not profit.

What shifts the supply curve

Because market supply is the sum of the firms' MC curves, anything that changes firms' marginal costs or the number of firms shifts it.

Edwards opened Class 9 with a story that shows the market view and the firm view side by side. Diesel fuel prices rose a lot (he said "almost something like 80%"). In the market for a crop, every farm's marginal cost goes up, so market supply shifts up: price rises and quantity falls, which is bad but not a disaster for the market as a whole. But one family farm faces a flat demand curve at the world price. Its MC curve shifts up, it cannot raise its price, and so it simply eats the higher cost. "With the family farmer, you're sunk. You can't affect that market price at all." The difference is size relative to the market. A firm big enough to have market power could cut its output to push the price up; a tiny farm cannot.

The long run in plain words

Profits are a signal. If firms in an industry are earning positive economic profit, outsiders notice and enter. Entry adds more firms to the horizontal sum, so market supply shifts right, the market price falls, and each firm's profit shrinks. Entry keeps happening until profit is competed down to zero. If firms are losing money, some exit, supply shifts left, the price rises, and the remaining firms' losses shrink. With free entry and exit, Edwards said, "in theory and in practice profits are competed down to zero."

"Zero profit" sounds preposterous, but it means zero excess profit. The owners still earn a normal return on their time and money, because those returns are counted as costs. The family farmer still makes a living; she just does not earn more than she could elsewhere.

Entry only works this way when it is free. Anything that makes entry expensive or impossible slows it down, which is why economists generally dislike barriers to entry. A large fixed cost (like California farmland) is a barrier in the short run. Patents and unique products are barriers that can last, which is how monopolies keep their profits. In a Class 9 poll about hometowns, most students had seen lots of entry and exit, especially restaurants and clothing stores, which is the competitive process at work.

Fall 2025 eggs: marginal cost, fixed cost, and two kinds of sellers

The marginal cost of an egg is low. Once the farm exists (land, henhouses, fencing already paid for), one more egg needs only a little more feed, water, and care for a hen that lays about one egg a day. Those are cheap. MC still rises with output, but it starts low. So the egg supply curve slopes up and sits low on the graph, which is why the Fall 2025 key drew a flat, low supply curve crossing a steep demand curve at $2 a dozen.

The fixed cost of a chicken farm is high, and that is a barrier to entry in the short run. A California chicken farm needs a lot of land, and California land is expensive. Over a year or two, that large fixed cost keeps new farms from entering even when egg prices are high. Over 5 years or more, it matters much less: the short run is the period when some costs are fixed, and given enough time a new entrant can raise money, buy land, and build. That is what the key meant by "basically by definition."

Denny's versus Trader Joe's. When bird flu sent egg prices up in early 2025, Trader Joe's kept its egg prices elevated but below the market price and limited customers to one dozen per visit, while Denny's and Waffle House added "egg surcharges." The reason is what eggs do to each seller's marginal cost. Eggs are in most of what Denny's sells, so the egg shock "would strongly raise their marginal cost curve," and a breakfast diner could not absorb that. Most of what Trader Joe's sells (beer, liquor, sweets, and many other foods) has no eggs, so the shock barely moved its overall costs, and it could absorb some of the cost or simply run out of eggs. Both companies knew that passing on big increases might bring accusations of "price gouging." Note that Trader Joe's below-market price plus a one-dozen limit works like a price ceiling it set on itself: a shortage, handled by rationing.

Worked examples

Example: how much to produce, and what is the profit?

A small competitive firm faces a market price of $10. Its marginal cost is MC = 2q, and its fixed cost is $20. How much does it produce? What if the price rises to $14? What are its producer surplus and profit at $10?

  1. The firm is a price taker, so MR = P = $10. It produces where P = MC: 10 = 2q, so q = 5.
  2. At $14: 14 = 2q, so q = 7. The firm moved up its MC curve, which is its supply curve.
  3. At $10, total revenue = 10 × 5 = $50.
  4. Variable cost = area under MC from 0 to 5, a triangle: ½ × 5 × 10 = $25.
  5. Producer surplus = revenue − variable cost = 50 − 25 = $25 (the triangle between the price line and MC).
  6. Profit = producer surplus − fixed cost = 25 − 20 = $5.

Answer: 5 units at $10 and 7 units at $14. At $10, producer surplus is $25 and profit is $5.

Example: 100 firms add up to industry supply

Edwards' slide: 100 identical firms, each with marginal cost of $5 at 1,000 units and $6 at 2,000 units. Find industry quantity supplied at $5 and $6. Then 50 more identical firms enter. What is industry quantity supplied at $6 now?

  1. Each firm's supply curve is its MC curve. At a price of $5, each firm produces up to where MC = $5, which is 1,000 units.
  2. Add horizontally: 100 × 1,000 = 100,000 units at $5.
  3. At $6, each firm supplies 2,000 units: 100 × 2,000 = 200,000 units.
  4. After entry there are 150 firms. Each firm's MC has not changed, so each still supplies 2,000 units at $6: 150 × 2,000 = 300,000 units.

Answer: 100,000 at $5 and 200,000 at $6. After entry, 300,000 at $6: market supply shifted right, while no single firm's marginal cost moved.

Example: should the almond farm enter?

A small almond farm can sell at the market price of $5 per pound. Its marginal cost is MC = q/2, where q is thousands of pounds per year. Its fixed cost is $20,000 a year. How much should it grow, and should it enter? What if the fixed cost were $30,000?

  1. Price taker, so produce where P = MC: 5 = q/2, so q = 10 (thousand pounds).
  2. Area between the price and MC from 0 to 10 is a triangle with base 10 and height 5 (MC goes from $0 up to $5): ½ × 10 × 5 = 25. Since q is in thousands, that is $25,000.
  3. Compare with fixed cost: $25,000 > $20,000, so the farm enters. Profit = 25,000 − 20,000 = $5,000.
  4. With a $30,000 fixed cost: $25,000 < $30,000, so it would lose $5,000 and stays out. If it did operate, it would still grow 10 thousand pounds, because fixed cost does not change MC.

Answer: Grow 10 thousand pounds. Enter at a $20,000 fixed cost (profit $5,000); stay out at $30,000.

Example: diesel prices rise for every farm

Edwards' Class 9 story: the price of diesel, an input every farm must use, rises sharply. What happens to one family farm, and what happens in the market?

  1. Diesel is a variable cost (more output means more tractor hours), so every farm's MC curve shifts up.
  2. Market: market supply is the sum of the MC curves, so it shifts up (to the left). With demand unchanged, the market price rises and market quantity falls.
  3. One farm: it is tiny relative to the market, so it faces a flat demand at the world price. It cannot raise its own price.
  4. At that price, its higher MC curve crosses the flat price line at a smaller quantity, so it produces less, and its profit falls. It absorbs the cost.

Answer: Market supply shifts up, price rises, quantity falls. The single farm's MC shifts up, it produces less at the given price, and it eats the higher cost because it has no market power.

Common mistakes

Mistake: Drawing a downward-sloping demand curve for one small competitive firm because market demand slopes down.

Correct: Market demand slopes down, but the demand one price-taking firm sees is flat (perfectly elastic) at the market price, so MR = P.

Mistake: Saying that a higher fixed cost (rent, a bigger building, a license fee) makes a firm produce less.

Correct: Fixed cost does not change marginal cost, so an operating firm still produces where P = MC. Fixed cost changes only profit and the decision to enter or exit.

Mistake: Treating profit and producer surplus as the same thing.

Correct: Producer surplus is revenue minus variable cost (the area between price and MC). Profit subtracts fixed cost too: profit = producer surplus − fixed cost.

Mistake: Reading "profits are competed down to zero" as "the owners earn nothing."

Correct: It means zero excess profit. Wages, rent, and the normal return on the owner's time and money are already counted in total cost (opportunity cost of all inputs).

Mistake: Saying entry of new firms shifts each firm's MC curve, or that entry shifts market supply "up."

Correct: Entry leaves every firm's MC unchanged and shifts market supply to the right (outward). Edwards once said "shifts up" out loud in Class 9; the slide's "right" is the correct version.

Mistake: Adding firm supply curves vertically (adding prices).

Correct: Add horizontally: at each price, add up the quantities. 100 firms each supplying 1,000 units at $5 make 100,000 units at $5, not $500.

Mistake: Saying a higher price of the good "shifts supply out."

Correct: A change in the good's own price is a movement along the supply (MC) curve, a change in quantity supplied. Supply shifts only when costs, technology, or the number of firms change.

Practice questions

These follow the format of his exams: 2-point "Briefly explain" answers, 4-point graphs drawn with values, and one long story question. In every firm answer, say whether the change moves marginal cost (so output changes) or only fixed cost (so only profit and entry change).

2 points A pistachio grower in the Central Valley is one of thousands of farms selling identical pistachios. Market demand for pistachios slopes down. Briefly explain what the demand curve facing this one grower looks like and what its marginal revenue is.

Show answer

The demand curve facing one grower is flat (perfectly elastic) at the market price. The farm is so small relative to the market that its output cannot move the price: if it asked for more than the market price, buyers would go to one of the thousands of other farms, and it has no reason to ask for less, because it can sell everything it grows at the market price. Each extra pound adds exactly the market price to revenue and does not lower the price on any other pound, so marginal revenue equals the price.

Full credit: flat demand at the market price because the farm is a price taker, and MR = P.

4 points A small lettuce farm sells at the market price of $20 per crate. Its marginal cost is MC = 4q, where q is hundreds of crates per week. (a) How much does it produce? (b) The market price rises to $28. How much does it produce now? (c) Draw both situations on one graph with values, and briefly explain why the farm's marginal cost curve is its supply curve.

Show answer

(a) A price taker has MR = P, and every firm produces where MR = MC, so the farm sets P = MC: 20 = 4q, so q = 5, which is 500 crates per week. Each crate before that costs less than $20 to grow, and crates after it would cost more than $20.

(b) At $28: 28 = 4q, so q = 7, which is 700 crates per week. The flat MR line moved up and the farm moved up along its MC curve.

(c) Every price picks out the quantity where the price line meets MC, so the MC curve tells you how much the farm supplies at each price. Read the other way, the price needed to get the farm to grow a given quantity is the marginal cost of that crate. That is exactly what a supply curve is.

Full credit: q = 5 (500 crates) and q = 7 (700 crates) from P = MC, a graph with flat MR lines at $20 and $28 meeting an upward MC, and "the supply curve is the MC curve" with a reason.

4 points There are 500 identical egg farms. Each farm's marginal cost is $1 per dozen at 10,000 dozen a week, $2 at 20,000 dozen, and $3 at 30,000 dozen. (a) Draw one farm's supply curve and the industry supply curve side by side, with values. (b) After a period of high egg prices, 250 more identical farms enter. Add the new industry supply curve. How much does the industry supply at $2 now?

Show answer

(a) One farm's supply curve is its mc curve through ($1, 10,000), ($2, 20,000), and ($3, 30,000). Industry supply is the horizontal sum: at each price, add up the 500 farms' quantities. At $1: 500 × 10,000 = 5 million dozen. At $2: 500 × 20,000 = 10 million. At $3: 500 × 30,000 = 15 million. That curve is also the industry's marginal cost curve.

(b) Each farm's mc does not change, because entry does not change any farm's technology or input prices. With 750 farms, at $2 the industry supplies 750 × 20,000 = 15 million dozen (and 7.5 million at $1, 22.5 million at $3). Market supply shifts to the right.

Full credit: the firm mc curve and S1 with values (5, 10, and 15 million), S2 to the right of S1, and 15 million dozen at $2, with each farm's mc unchanged.

2 points A classmate says, "When new farms enter, each farm's marginal cost curve shifts down, and that is why market supply goes up." What is wrong with this? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Entry does not change any single farm's marginal cost: each farm uses the same technology and pays the same input prices as before. What changes is the number of marginal cost curves being added up. Market supply is the horizontal sum of the firms' supply curves, so with more firms the market supplies more at every price, and the market supply curve shifts to the right. Edwards: 500 new firms would push the supply curve out to the right, "not affecting the marginal cost curve for an individual firm at all."

Full credit: each firm's MC is unchanged, and market supply shifts right because more firms are summed horizontally.

4 points A burrito-bowl truck near Sproul Plaza is one of dozens of nearly identical trucks, so it takes the market price of $12 a bowl as given. Its marginal cost is MC = 0.1q, where q is bowls per day, and its fixed costs (truck lease, insurance, and permits) are $500 a day. (a) How many bowls does it sell? (b) Draw the truck's graph and shade the area it compares with its fixed cost when deciding whether to enter. Label values. (c) Should it enter? What is its daily profit? What if its fixed costs were $900 a day?

Show answer

(a) P = MC: 12 = 0.1q, so q = 120 bowls a day.

(b) Revenue is $12 × 120 = $1,440. Variable cost is the area under MC up to 120 bowls, a triangle: ½ × 120 × $12 = $720. The area between the price line and MC is revenue minus variable cost, $1,440 − $720 = $720. Edwards calls this the "total marginal profit"; it is the truck's producer surplus.

(c) $720 is more than the $500 fixed cost, so the truck enters, and its profit is $720 − $500 = $220 a day. With $900 of fixed costs it would lose $180 a day, so it should stay out. If it did operate anyway, it would still sell 120 bowls, because fixed cost does not change marginal cost.

Full credit: 120 bowls, the shaded triangle between P and MC worth $720, enter with profit $220 at $500, stay out at $900.

2 points The city raises the truck's yearly permit fee by $3,650, which is $10 a day. Does the truck sell more bowls, fewer, or the same number? What does change? Briefly explain.

Show answer

The same number, 120 bowls a day. The permit fee is a fixed cost: the truck pays it no matter how many bowls it sells, so it does not change the marginal cost of a bowl, and the truck still produces where P = MC. What changes is profit, which falls by $10 a day (from $220 to $210 in the last question). A fixed cost matters only for whether the truck stays in business. Compare a rise in the price of chicken, which is a variable cost: that would shift MC up, and the truck would sell fewer bowls.

Full credit: "the same," because the fee is a fixed cost that does not change MC; profit falls.

3 points A gelato cart near the Campanile has $3,000 of revenue in a month. Its variable costs are $1,800, which includes $1,200 that the owner pays herself as an hourly wage, the same amount she could earn at another job. Its fixed costs (cart lease and permit) are $900 a month. (a) What is the cart's producer surplus? (b) What is its economic profit? (c) The owner says, "I made $1,500 this month." How much of that is profit? Briefly explain.

Show answer

(a) Producer surplus = revenue − variable cost = $3,000 − $1,800 = $1,200.

(b) Profit = revenue − total cost = $3,000 − ($1,800 + $900) = $300. Equivalently, profit = producer surplus − fixed cost = $1,200 − $900 = $300.

(c) Only $300 is economic profit. The $1,200 wage is the return to her labor, an input. It is a cost, measured at its opportunity cost (what she could earn elsewhere), so it is not profit. Edwards stresses keeping three things apart: profit, producer surplus, and the returns to inputs like labor and capital.

Full credit: $1,200 of producer surplus, $300 of profit, and the wage counted as a cost (a return to her labor), not profit.

2 points In a competitive industry with free entry, Edwards says profits are "competed down to zero." A friend replies, "Then why would anyone run a farm?" Briefly explain what happens over time and what "zero profit" means.

Show answer

When firms earn positive economic profit, new firms enter. Entry shifts market supply to the right, which lowers the market price, which shrinks every firm's profit, and entry continues until profit is zero. Zero profit does not mean the owners earn nothing. Total cost already includes the opportunity cost of every input, including the owner's own time and money, so a farmer earning zero economic profit still earns a normal living: she effectively pays herself a wage. Zero profit means zero excess profit above what her time and money could earn elsewhere.

Full credit: entry, supply shifts right, price falls, profit falls to zero; zero means no excess profit because the owner's wage and normal return are counted as costs.

2 points In the market for strawberries, why is the area between the market price and the upward-sloping supply curve called producer surplus? Briefly explain.

Show answer

The supply curve is the marginal cost curve, so its height at each quantity is the cost of producing that unit. Every unit sells at the market price, but each unit costs only its marginal cost to produce. The area under the supply curve is the total variable cost of the units sold, and the area between the price and the supply curve adds up price minus marginal cost over every unit. That gap is what producers receive above their costs, which is their surplus.

Full credit: producers receive the price on every unit but pay only its marginal cost (the area under supply); the difference is their surplus.

2 points A lot of factory production has been automated. Can a haircut be automated the same way? What does your answer suggest about how the marginal cost of a haircut will change over time? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Mostly no. Every haircut takes a barber's time, plus a chair and a shop, and that labor cannot easily be replaced by a machine. Since labor is the main variable cost, the marginal cost of a haircut cannot fall much over time the way it can for goods made on an automated line, and it tends to rise when wages rise. (This mirrors a Fall 2025 final question about whether a Big Mac can be automated.)

Full credit: a reasoned answer that ties the haircut's labor-heavy variable cost to a marginal cost that cannot fall much.

4 points Suppose a poor harvest in Japan doubles the price of matcha powder. A matcha-only café on Telegraph adds $2 to the price of every drink, while a large coffee chain keeps its matcha latte price the same and sometimes runs out. Why might the two sellers respond so differently? Briefly explain using marginal cost.

Show answer

Matcha is in nearly everything the matcha café sells, so the shock strongly raises its marginal cost across its whole menu. A small shop cannot absorb a cost increase that large, so it passes it on. For the coffee chain, matcha drinks are a small share of what it sells (mostly coffee and food), so the shock barely moves its overall costs. It can absorb the higher cost on matcha drinks, selling them below the price the market would bear, and running out is how that shortage gets rationed. Both may also worry that big price increases will look like price gouging to customers. This is the same logic as Denny's and Trader Joe's in the Fall 2025 egg question.

Full credit: matcha raises the café's marginal cost on most of its products, while the chain's overall costs barely move, so it can absorb the cost (and rations by running out). Mentioning fairness or price gouging helps.

2 points Starting an almond orchard means buying expensive Central Valley land, and new trees take several years before they produce a crop. If almond prices jump this year, how much of a barrier to entry is this over one year? Over ten years? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Over one year it is a big barrier to entry. The land is a large fixed cost, and even a farm that pays it cannot sell almonds until the trees mature, so few new growers can respond to high prices quickly. Over ten years it is a much smaller barrier: there is time to raise money, buy land, plant, and wait for the trees to bear. The short run is, basically by definition, the period when some costs are fixed, and in the long run those costs can be paid and entry can happen.

Full credit: a large fixed cost (expensive land plus years of waiting) is a big barrier in the short run and a smaller one over ten years.

14 points Strawberry fields for never? Hundreds of small farms around Watsonville grow strawberries and sell them at the market price, which is $20 per flat. One farm's marginal cost is MC = 2q, where q is hundreds of flats per week. Its fixed costs (land lease, irrigation, and equipment) are $6,000 per week.

(a) (2 points) What demand curve does one farm face, and what is its marginal revenue? Briefly explain.

(b) (2 points) How many flats does the farm pick per week? Show your work.

(c) (4 points) Draw the farm's MR and MC curves, label the quantity and price, and shade its producer surplus with its value. Should the farm enter? What is its weekly profit?

(d) (2 points) The county raises the property tax on farmland by $3,000 per week per farm. Does the farm pick more, fewer, or the same number of flats? What happens to its profit?

(e) (2 points) Instead (no tax change), a higher farmworker minimum wage raises the farm's marginal cost to MC = 2.5q. With the price still $20, how many flats does it pick, and what is its profit now?

(f) (2 points) There are 300 identical farms. What is the market quantity supplied at $20 before and after the wage increase? What happens to market supply and to the market price?

Show answer

(a) Flat (perfectly elastic) at $20, because the farm is one of hundreds and cannot move the market price. Marginal revenue equals the price, $20.

(b) P = MC: 20 = 2q, so q = 10, which is 1,000 flats per week.

(c) Revenue = $20 × 10 = 200, and variable cost = area under MC = ½ × 10 × $20 = 100. Since q is in hundreds of flats, those are $20,000 and $10,000, so producer surplus (the area between the price and MC) is $10,000. That beats the $6,000 fixed cost, so the farm enters, and its profit is $10,000 − $6,000 = $4,000 a week.

(d) The same 1,000 flats. The property tax does not depend on how many flats the farm picks, so it is a fixed cost and does not change MC. Profit falls from $4,000 to $1,000 a week. That is still positive, so the farm stays in business.

(e) Picking wages are a variable cost, since more flats need more pickers, so this time MC itself rises. 20 = 2.5q gives q = 8, or 800 flats per week. Producer surplus is ½ × 8 × $20 = 80, which is $8,000, so profit is $8,000 − $6,000 = $2,000 a week. Compare (d): the fixed cost increase cut profit but not output; the marginal cost increase cut both.

(f) Before: 300 × 1,000 = 300,000 flats per week at $20. After the wage increase: 300 × 800 = 240,000 flats at $20. Every farm's MC rose, so market supply shifts left (up). With demand unchanged, the market price of strawberries rises and the market quantity falls, ending up somewhere between 240,000 and 300,000 flats with a normal downward-sloping demand curve. No single farm can raise its price, but the market price rises because all of them cut back together.

Full credit: (a) flat demand and MR = $20; (b) 1,000 flats from P = MC; (c) a graph with the producer surplus triangle labeled $10,000, enter, profit $4,000; (d) same output, profit $1,000, because the tax is a fixed cost; (e) 800 flats and profit $2,000 because MC rose; (f) 300,000 and 240,000, supply shifts left, price rises, quantity falls.

One-minute review

  • Profit = total revenue − total cost, where total cost is the opportunity cost of all inputs (the owner's own wage counts as a cost).
  • A small competitive firm is a price taker: its demand is flat at the market price, so MR = P.
  • Every firm produces where MR = MC. For a competitive firm, that means P = MC.
  • Fixed cost does not change MC, so it does not change output. It changes only profit and the entry decision.
  • MC always slopes up in Econ 2. The firm's supply curve is its MC curve.
  • Market supply is the horizontal sum of firm supply curves and is the industry MC curve (100 firms × 1,000 units = 100,000 units at $5).
  • Enter if the area between P and MC (revenue minus variable cost) is bigger than fixed cost. Profit = producer surplus − fixed cost.
  • Technology every firm uses shifts supply right; a higher input price shifts it left; entry shifts market supply right without moving any firm's MC.
  • Long run: profits attract entry until profit is zero (zero excess profit). Fixed costs are a barrier to entry in the short run, much less over 5 years.
  • Eggs: MC of an egg is low, so supply is low and upward sloping. Denny's MC jumps because eggs are in most dishes; Trader Joe's can absorb the shock.
