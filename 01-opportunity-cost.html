Class 2 · Scarcity and choice

Scarcity, opportunity cost, and the PPC

This unit covers the first building block of the course: because resources are limited, every choice has an opportunity cost, and the production possibilities curve (PPC) is the picture of that tradeoff. On the exam it shows up as quick 2-point questions about the true cost of something (college, a ticket, your time) and as PPC graphs where you read opportunity cost off the slope.

How it shows up on the exam

Opportunity cost is tested every year, usually in short 2-point questions near the start of the exam. These are some of the easiest points on the midterm if you know the few rules below and use the exact vocabulary the answer key rewards.

The ideas, explained

Scarcity and choice

Edwards uses the economist's definition of scarcity: someone or something faces a constraint. People have limited time and income, firms have limited workers and machines, and whole countries have limited labor, capital, and technology. The constraints are fixed at a point in time but can change over time (a country can grow its labor force or build more machines).

Because of those constraints, people must choose, and every choice has a cost. The central subject of economics, in his words, is how people, firms, and countries do the best they can given the constraints they face. Two principles from Chapter 1 frame everything after this:

  • The scarcity principle: having more of something means having less of something else.
  • The cost-benefit principle: take an action if and only if its benefits outweigh its costs.

The cost that matters in the cost-benefit principle is the opportunity cost, which is not always the price on the tag.

Opportunity cost

Opportunity cost is the value of what must be given up to do an activity: the value of the best alternative you did not choose. If you are doing one thing, you cannot be doing something else.

Often the opportunity cost is obvious. For a good bought in a store, it is typically just the price, because that money could have bought the next best thing (if you buy pizza on Telegraph for lunch, the opportunity cost is the other lunch you could have bought). Edwards' slides list three cases where the opportunity cost is subtler than the price, and these are exactly the cases he tests.

1. Your time always has value

Time spent on any activity is time you could have spent working or doing something else you value. Economists value that time at roughly your wage. The reasoning: if an hour of leisure were worth much less than your wage, you would choose to work more hours; if it were worth much more, you would work fewer. People adjust until an hour of free time is worth about what an hour of work pays. So a consultant earning $100 an hour should value her leisure at about $100 an hour.

This kills a common excuse: "I would not work on weekends anyway, so painting my own house on Saturday costs nothing." That is wrong. Your weekend time has value (sleep and time with friends are valuable uses of time), so the cost of painting is the paint and brushes plus the value of your time.

Edwards also warned about a separate idea that people mix in. Some homeowners paint their own house because they cannot check whether a hired painter did a careful job. That is a problem of information (you cannot verify quality), which comes later in the course. It is a real reason, but it is a different point from opportunity cost, so keep the two apart in an answer.

His Kaiser Permanente poll made the same point inside a firm: if staff are assigned to a quality improvement project, the labor cost of the project is the opportunity cost of their time, meaning the other work those staff could have been doing for Kaiser. He said many mid-career master's students get this wrong.

2. Something you own costs its resale value today

Suppose you bought theater tickets and the market price has changed since then. The opportunity cost of using them is what you could sell them for at the time you use them, plus the value of your time spent going. The price you originally paid does not matter. It is a sunk cost: that money is gone whether you go or not, so it cannot change which choice is better.

Edwards' class version: if you are sick on the night of a Taylor Swift concert, the resale value of the ticket is high (prices for top acts never fall), so going has a large opportunity cost and selling may be smarter. If the show is one with low demand, the resale value is low, so the opportunity cost of going is low, and you might as well go.

A free ticket follows the same rule. Getting it for free does not make going free: going still means giving up its resale value and your time.

3. College: out-of-pocket costs plus foregone earnings

The opportunity cost of a year of college has two parts:

  • Costs you pay: tuition, fees, books, and supplies.
  • Costs you do not pay but still bear: the foregone earnings from the full-time job you are not doing. Edwards' example is working as a grocery store cashier instead of sitting in lecture.

Opportunity cost of college = tuition + fees + books + supplies + foregone earnings. Living expenses (rent, food) are left out because you pay them whether or not you go to college.

Why living expenses cancel: the opportunity cost is the difference between the two paths. Going to college costs living expenses plus tuition, fees, books, and supplies. Working instead costs living expenses but also brings in earnings, so your net position is living expenses minus earnings. Subtract the two:

(living + tuition + fees + books + supplies) − (living − earnings) = tuition + fees + books + supplies + earnings

The living expenses appear on both sides and drop out. With average earnings around $60,000 a year, foregone earnings are usually the biggest piece, larger than tuition at a public university like Berkeley. Edwards described students as "in the red" now (paying this opportunity cost) and "in the black" later, because college is an investment that pays off: about a third of U.S. adults have a college degree, and the Problem Set 1 key mentions returns to college of perhaps 10% a year. Student loans are a market that moves your future earnings back into today.

The production possibilities curve

The PPC is the course's first economic model. It shows the combinations of two goods an economy could produce using all of its available inputs:

  • Labor: people's time for work and their abilities.
  • Capital: land, buildings, machines, factories.
  • Technology: knowledge, techniques, understanding.

Edwards' running example splits U.S. output into two goods. Consumption goods satisfy a current want and are used up (food, clothing, housing services, legal services, a show at Zellerbach). Investment goods make us more productive in the future and stick around (machines, buildings, infrastructure, education, research and development). Investment goods are the flow of new capital goods into the stock of capital. About 75% of U.S. production is consumption and 25% is gross investment, where "gross" means before subtracting depreciation and "net" means after. On his graphs, consumption (C) is on the horizontal axis and investment (I) is on the vertical axis.

On, inside, and outside the curve

  • On the PPC (points a and b): the economy is using all its inputs. This is productive efficiency: it is impossible to produce more of one good without producing less of the other. Every point on the curve is efficient; which one the country picks is a choice that depends on what it wants.
  • Outside the PPC (point c): unattainable with today's labor, capital, and technology. This is what scarcity looks like on the graph.
  • Inside the PPC (point d): the economy is not producing all it is capable of, because some resources sit idle. A recession, a time when unemployment is above normal and the economy is not producing at its full potential, is a point inside the PPC. Edwards' example is the COVID recession, when restaurants shut and waiters and bartenders were suddenly idle.

A recession moves the economy to a point inside the PPC. The curve itself does not shift, because the workers, machines, and know-how still exist; they are just not being used.

Edwards added that a boom does not push the economy far outside the PPC, since the curve already represents full use of resources.

The slope is (minus) the opportunity cost

The PPC slopes down because, when all resources are in use, getting more of one good requires moving resources away from the other. How much of the other good you give up is the opportunity cost, and that is exactly what the slope measures.

The slope of the PPC is minus the opportunity cost of the good on the horizontal axis: slope = ΔI ÷ ΔC = −(OC of 1 unit of C, in units of I).

The simplest case is a straight line. In the Problem Set 1 afternoon, you can build 2 bookcases or watch 3 TV shows, or any mix in between. Put TV shows on the horizontal axis. Moving from 0 shows to 1 show costs 2/3 of a bookcase, and the slope of the line is −2/3. The opportunity cost of the vertical-axis good is the reciprocal: one bookcase costs 3/2 = 1.5 TV shows.

Two quick checks that keep the direction straight:

  • A flatter PPC means a lower opportunity cost of the horizontal-axis good (you give up little of the vertical good per unit). A steeper PPC means a higher one. Say "flatter" or "smaller in absolute value," not "smaller slope," because the slope is negative.
  • The opportunity cost of the vertical-axis good is 1 divided by the opportunity cost of the horizontal-axis good.

Why the PPC is bowed out

A curved PPC means the opportunity cost of the horizontal-axis good rises as you produce more of it. Resources are not equally good at everything. When the economy first shifts toward consumption goods, it moves the workers and machines that are best suited to consumption and worst at investment, so it gives up very little investment. As it keeps going, it has to pull in resources that are excellent at investment and poor at consumption, so each extra unit of consumption costs more and more investment. Edwards' slide example is oil: it becomes harder and harder to produce more oil in a country. This is the same specialization idea as the next unit: the lowest-opportunity-cost producers switch first.

Edwards described this as a ski slope: the flat top is the "bunny slope" (low opportunity cost of the horizontal good) and the steep bottom runs "down to the chalet" (high opportunity cost). On a curve, the opportunity cost at a particular point is the slope of the tangent line at that point.

On the slides, point a (lots of investment, little consumption) sits on the flat part, so there the opportunity cost of consumption is low and the opportunity cost of investment is high: you would give up a lot of consumption to get a little more investment. Point b (lots of consumption) sits on the steep part, so the reverse holds: consumption is expensive in terms of investment, and investment is cheap in terms of consumption.

What shifts the PPC

The PPC is drawn for fixed amounts of labor, capital, and technology, so it shifts when those change:

  • More labor: immigration, population growth, or a better-trained labor force (for example, a larger share of people with college degrees).
  • More capital: more machines, buildings, and infrastructure.
  • Better technology: new knowledge and techniques.

Any of these shifts the PPC outward, so combinations that were unattainable become possible. Losing inputs (a disaster that destroys factories, for example) would shift it in. Edwards noted it is still unknown whether AI will act like labor, capital, or technology, or replace human labor.

Choices today change the PPC in the future

Because investment goods add to the capital stock, the point a country picks today affects where its PPC will be later. Choosing point a (more investment, less consumption now) gives a big outward shift in the future. Choosing point b (more consumption now) gives only a small shift. Edwards called this a choice about the timing of enjoyment: giving up consumption today buys more of everything later. College is the same trade for a person, which is why he said students are delaying a lot of their enjoyment.

Countries make this choice differently, partly because of forward-looking decisions and partly because of age structure. His examples: India is a young country that invests heavily to set itself up for growth, while China is no longer very young because of the one-child policy.

Worked examples

Example: bookcases and TV shows (Problem Set 1)

On a free afternoon you could build 2 bookcases or watch 3 TV shows. What is the opportunity cost of one show? Of one bookcase?

  1. The whole afternoon is the constraint. It produces 2 bookcases or 3 shows, so 3 shows cost 2 bookcases.
  2. Divide by 3: one show costs 2/3 of a bookcase.
  3. Flip it for the other good: 2 bookcases cost 3 shows, so one bookcase costs 3/2 = 1.5 shows.
  4. On a graph with shows on the horizontal axis, the line runs from 2 bookcases to 3 shows, with slope −2/3, which is minus the opportunity cost of a show.

Answer: one TV show costs 2/3 of a bookcase; one bookcase costs 1.5 TV shows.

Example: the opportunity cost of a year of college

Tuition and fees are $15,000 a year, books are $1,000, rent and food are $20,000, and you could earn $60,000 a year working full time instead.

  1. Costs you pay because of college: tuition and fees ($15,000) plus books ($1,000) = $16,000.
  2. Cost you do not pay but still bear: foregone earnings of $60,000.
  3. Rent and food: you pay $20,000 for them whether you are in school or working, so they are the same on both paths and cancel out.
  4. Total: $16,000 + $60,000 = $76,000.

Answer: $76,000 a year. Foregone earnings are the largest piece, far bigger than tuition, and the answer must name them to earn full credit.

Example: a ticket whose price changed

Months ago you paid $300 for a concert ticket. Today the same ticket resells for $80. Going takes 3 hours, and you could earn $20 an hour at your campus job. What is the opportunity cost of going?

  1. By going, you give up selling the ticket now: $80.
  2. You also give up 3 hours of time, worth about your wage: 3 × $20 = $60.
  3. The $300 you paid is a sunk cost. You cannot get it back by going or by staying home, so it is not part of the decision.
  4. Total: $80 + $60 = $140.

Answer: $140, the resale value today plus the value of your time. Go only if the concert is worth more than $140 to you.

Example: is a $200 concert ticket expensive? (Fall 2025 midterm 3(b))

The Eras Tour sold about 10 million tickets for about $2 billion. Is the average price high or low compared with other ways to spend 3 hours?

  1. Average price: $2 billion ÷ 10 million tickets = $200.
  2. Value of 3 hours of time at the average wage of about $36 an hour: 3 × $36 = $108.
  3. The ticket alone costs almost twice what 3 hours of work would earn, so it is a lot to pay for 3 hours of entertainment.

Answer: high. The key gave full credit for any reasoned answer, but comparing with the value of your time is the reasoning he had in mind.

Common mistakes

Mistake: Saying a recession shifts the PPC inward.

Correct: A recession is a point inside the PPC. The inputs still exist but some sit idle, so the curve does not move. Only a change in labor, capital, or technology shifts the curve.

Mistake: Using the price you paid for a ticket (or anything you own) as its cost.

Correct: The opportunity cost is what you could sell it for today plus the value of your time. The original price is sunk and irrelevant. A free ticket is not free to use.

Mistake: Listing only tuition and books as the cost of college, or adding rent and food.

Correct: Tuition, fees, books, supplies, and foregone earnings. Rent and food cancel because you pay them either way. Full credit on the Fall 2025 midterm required the words "foregone earnings."

Mistake: "I would not work on the weekend anyway, so doing it myself costs nothing."

Correct: Your time always has value, roughly your wage, including leisure and sleep. Doing it yourself costs the materials plus the value of your time.

Mistake: Reading the slope as the opportunity cost of the vertical-axis good, or saying a flatter PPC means a higher opportunity cost.

Correct: The slope is minus the opportunity cost of the horizontal-axis good. Flatter means that good is cheaper. The vertical good's opportunity cost is the reciprocal.

Mistake: Writing "slope = 1 minus the opportunity cost" (Edwards slipped into this phrase in Class 2 before correcting himself).

Correct: Slope = minus (negative) the opportunity cost of the horizontal-axis good. On the bookcase graph, the slope is −2/3, not 1 − 2/3.

Mistake: Explaining "do it yourself" choices with information problems (you cannot check a painter's quality) and calling that opportunity cost.

Correct: Edwards said the quality-checking reason is an information problem, distinct from opportunity cost. If you mention it, label it as a separate reason.

Practice questions

2 points On a free afternoon you could build 2 bookcases or watch 3 TV shows. What is the opportunity cost of watching one TV show? Briefly explain.

Show answer

The afternoon produces either 2 bookcases or 3 shows, so 3 shows cost 2 bookcases. One show therefore costs 2/3 of a bookcase. (Going the other way, one bookcase costs 1.5 shows.)

Full credit: 2/3 of a bookcase, with the reason that the same afternoon makes 2 bookcases or 3 shows.

2 points List and discuss the most important costs of attending UC Berkeley for a year. (Hint: some costs are ones you pay and some are not.)

Show answer

The costs you pay are tuition and fees (plus books and supplies). The cost you do not pay but still bear is the foregone earnings from the full-time job you could have had instead, around $60,000 a year at average earnings. Foregone earnings are probably the larger cost, bigger than tuition except at private colleges.

Full credit: must name foregone earnings; tuition and fees as the other main cost.

2 points Why do economists leave rent and food out of the opportunity cost of going to college?

Show answer

You pay rent and food whether you go to college or work, so they are the same on both paths and cancel when you compare them: (living + tuition + fees + books + supplies) − (living − earnings) = tuition + fees + books + supplies + earnings.

Full credit: "you pay them either way, so they cancel."

2 points You win a free ticket to a Warriors game that you could resell for $150. The game plus the BART ride takes 4 hours (ignore the fare), and you could otherwise work your campus job at $20 an hour. What is your opportunity cost of going? Be specific.

Show answer

Going means giving up the $150 resale value plus 4 hours of work: 4 × $20 = $80. Opportunity cost = $150 + $80 = $230. The ticket was free to you, but using it is not.

Full credit: $230 with both pieces. $0 or only $80 misses the resale value.

2 points You paid $300 for a play ticket in the spring. On the night of the show you feel sick, and the ticket now resells for only $40. Your friend says, "You paid $300, so you have to go or you waste $300." Briefly evaluate.

Show answer

The friend is wrong. The $300 is sunk: it is gone whether you go or not. The opportunity cost of going tonight is what you give up by going, which is the $40 resale value plus the value of your time (and, here, your health). With a low resale value, the opportunity cost of going is low, so go only if the show is worth more than that to you while sick.

Full credit: the purchase price is irrelevant; the opportunity cost is the current resale value plus your time.

2 points A concert ticket costs $200 and the show takes about 3 hours. The average hourly wage is about $36. Is the ticket price high or low compared with other things you could do with 3 hours? Briefly explain.

Show answer

High. Three hours at $36 an hour are worth about $108, so the $200 ticket costs nearly twice what that time would earn at work, before even counting the value of the time itself.

Full credit: any reasoned answer; the expected one compares $200 with about $108 of time.

2 points An economy produces consumption goods (horizontal axis) and investment goods (vertical axis). Moving along its PPC from point A to point B adds 10 units of consumption and gives up 30 units of investment. What is the opportunity cost of one unit of consumption along this stretch? Where on the graph is an economy in a recession?

Show answer

Slope = −30 ÷ 10 = −3. The slope of the PPC is minus the opportunity cost of the horizontal-axis good, so one unit of consumption costs 3 units of investment (and one unit of investment costs 1/3 unit of consumption). A recession is a point inside the PPC: some workers and machines are idle. The curve itself does not shift.

Full credit: 3 units of investment, and "inside the PPC" (not an inward shift).

2 points Why is the PPC for consumption and investment goods usually bowed out rather than a straight line?

Show answer

Because the opportunity cost of consumption rises as the economy makes more of it. Resources are not equally good at both goods. The first resources moved into consumption are the ones best at it and worst at investment, so little investment is lost. Later, the economy must move resources that are very good at investment, so each extra unit of consumption costs more investment. A rising opportunity cost makes the curve steeper as you move right.

Full credit: rising opportunity cost, explained by resources being suited to different goods (specialization).

2 points A wave of immigration increases the U.S. labor force. What happens to the U.S. PPC for consumption and investment goods? Briefly explain.

Show answer

The PPC shifts outward. The PPC shows what the economy can produce using all available labor, capital, and technology; more workers mean more of both goods can be produced, so combinations that were unattainable become attainable.

Full credit: shifts out, because labor is one of the inputs behind the PPC.

4 points Draw a PPC with consumption goods on the horizontal axis and investment goods on the vertical axis. Label one point the country could choose today that has high investment (a) and one with high consumption (b). Then draw the future PPC that results from each choice and explain the difference.

Show answer

Draw a bowed-out curve. Put a on the upper-left part of the curve (more I, less C) and b on the lower-right part (more C, less I). Draw two future PPCs, both outside today's: a large outward shift labeled "future PPC if a" and a smaller one labeled "future PPC if b" (as in the last graph in this unit).

Explanation: investment goods are new capital (machines, buildings, education) that add to the capital stock and make the economy more productive later. Choosing a means giving up consumption today in exchange for a bigger PPC in the future; choosing b means more consumption now but less growth.

Full credit: labeled axes, a and b in the right places on the curve, the future PPC for a farther out than for b, and the reason (investment adds capital).

2 points True or false: "I would never take a job on weekends, so the time I spend painting my own house on Saturday has zero cost." Briefly explain.

Show answer

False. Your time always has value, even leisure and sleep, and economists value it at roughly your wage. The cost of painting is the paint and brushes plus the value of your time. (Wanting to do it yourself because you cannot check a painter's quality is a separate information reason, not a reason the time is free.)

Full credit: false, because time has value (about your wage).

2 points Using the PPC table in the worked examples (0 C and 100 I, 20 and 96, 40 and 84, 60 and 64, 80 and 36, 100 and 0), what is the opportunity cost of one unit of consumption when moving from 60 to 80 units of C? What does the pattern say about the shape of the PPC?

Show answer

Moving from 60 to 80 C costs 64 − 36 = 28 units of I, so one unit of C costs 28 ÷ 20 = 1.4 units of I. The cost per unit rises with each step (0.2, 0.6, 1.0, 1.4, 1.8), so the opportunity cost of consumption is rising and the PPC is bowed out.

Full credit: 1.4 units of investment, and rising opportunity cost means a bowed-out PPC.

One-minute review

  • Scarcity means facing constraints, so every choice has an opportunity cost: the value of the best alternative given up.
  • Your time always has value, about your wage, including leisure.
  • Something you own costs its resale value today plus your time. The price you paid is sunk and irrelevant.
  • College = tuition + fees + books + supplies + foregone earnings. Rent and food cancel. Always write "foregone earnings."
  • Afternoon: 2 bookcases or 3 TV shows, so one show costs 2/3 of a bookcase.
  • PPC slope = minus the opportunity cost of the horizontal-axis good. Flatter means cheaper. The vertical good's cost is the reciprocal.
  • On the PPC is efficient, outside is unattainable, inside means idle resources. A recession is a point inside; the curve does not shift.
  • Bowed out because opportunity cost rises: resources suited to one good are moved first.
  • More labor, capital, or technology shifts the PPC out. More investment today means a bigger PPC later.
  • Consumption goods are used up; investment goods are new capital that sticks around (75% consumption, 25% gross investment in the U.S.).
