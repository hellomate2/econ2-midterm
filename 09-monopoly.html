Class 10 · Monopoly and market power

Monopoly and market power

A monopolist is a seller with no close competitors, so it faces the whole downward-sloping demand curve and picks the quantity that maximizes its profit: less than the efficient quantity, at a higher price. Edwards said monopoly will be on this midterm as a shorter question than the Fall 2025 Taylor Swift question, so expect to draw demand and MR, find Q where MR = MC, read the price off demand, and compare with the efficient outcome.

The ideas, explained

What market power is

A firm in a perfectly competitive market is one of many sellers of an identical product. If it raises its price even a little, buyers go to a rival, so it takes the market price as given. Its own demand curve is flat at the market price.

A firm has market power when it can raise its price without losing all its customers. The extreme case is a monopoly: the only seller of a product with no close substitutes. Edwards' slide puts it as "only one producer of a differentiated good," and adds that "monopoly power is stronger when there are no close substitutes." Because the monopolist is the whole market, the demand curve it faces is the market demand curve, which slopes down. It can choose any point on that curve, but it cannot escape the curve: to sell more, it has to charge less.

Edwards stressed that monopoly is about how much of the demand curve a firm sees, not about counting firms. Uber and Lyft are two firms, but together they see most of the rideshare demand curve and have market power. On the other side, a Giants game is not a monopoly product, because if you do not like the Giants you can go watch the Oakland Ballers; there are substitutes. The many lunch places around campus each face nearly flat demand. He also said Berkeley's high rents are "probably not a story about monopoly pricing"; they come from limits on construction, zoning, and high demand.

Where market power comes from

A monopoly can last only if something keeps rivals out. A barrier to entry is "any force that prevents firms from entering a market." The sources he listed:

  • No close substitutes. There is only one Taylor Swift. Fans who want her concert cannot buy a near-identical one from someone else. The same logic applies to brands: only Apple makes iPhones.
  • Patents and other legal protections. A patent gives an inventor the legal right to be the only seller for a period of years. New medical drugs are the main example (Humira).
  • High fixed costs. Building a railway line costs a fortune before the first passenger rides. A new entrant would have to pay that cost again to compete.
  • Licenses and other gatekeeping. You need a license to practice medicine, which is a high barrier. A city health permit for a food cart is a low one.
  • Network effects. Some products are worth more when more people use them. Facebook and rideshare apps are more valuable as their customer base grows, so the biggest network keeps getting bigger and rivals struggle to start.
  • Natural monopoly. This is "a situation in which one single producer can supply the whole market at a lower average cost than multiple firms." It happens when fixed costs are huge and marginal cost is low: PG&E's gas and power lines, railways, and digital goods like software and music, which cost almost nothing to copy. Two sets of power lines down every street would waste money.

Why marginal revenue is below price

Marginal revenue (MR) is the extra revenue from producing and selling one more unit. For a competitive firm, MR equals the price, because it can sell another unit without changing the price. For a monopolist, MR is less than the price. The reason: in this model the monopolist must charge everyone the same price. To sell one more unit it has to lower the price, and the lower price applies to every unit, including all the units it could have sold at the higher price. Edwards: the price falls not only for "the last guy in the door" but "also everybody else." Can it charge different people different prices? "Maybe in reality," he said, but in this model, "No, you can't. You have to charge everybody the same price."

His area argument makes this exact. Selling one more unit does two things to revenue:

  • You gain the new price on the new unit (his area d).
  • You lose the price cut on all the earlier units (his area b, a rectangle whose width is the number of earlier units and whose height is the price cut).

So MR = d − b, which is less than the price. For the very first unit there are no earlier units to discount, so MR equals the whole price. Further out, the loss rectangle gets wider because more units get the price cut, so MR falls further and further below price, and eventually turns negative.

MR = a − 2bQ: same intercept, twice as steep

With linear demand P = a − bQ, total revenue is TR = P × Q = aQ − bQ², and marginal revenue is

MR = a − 2bQ. MR starts at the same vertical intercept as demand (a) and falls twice as fast (slope −2b instead of −b). So it hits the quantity axis at half of demand's quantity intercept.

Examples: demand P = 6 − Q/4 gives MR = 6 − Q/2. Demand P = 300 − 10Q gives MR = 300 − 20Q. Demand P = 5 − Q gives MR = 5 − 2Q. To get MR, keep the intercept and double the slope.

Edwards said demand "always will be" linear in Econ 2, so this rule always applies. The proof uses calculus, and he said plainly that he will never ask you why it is twice as steep; you just need to know that it is. A drawing warning from him: MR never looks twice as steep when you sketch it (he called it "an optical illusion"), so force it by making MR cross the quantity axis exactly halfway to where demand crosses it.

The two-step method

Every profit-maximizing firm, competitive or monopoly, produces where MR = MC. The reason is the usual marginal logic: if the next unit brings in more revenue than it costs (MR > MC), producing it raises profit, so keep going; if it costs more than it brings in (MR < MC), cut back. Profit is highest where they are equal. For a competitive firm MR is the price, so the rule becomes P = MC. For a monopolist MR is below the price, so the rule gives a different answer.

Step 1: find the quantity where MR = MC. Step 2: go straight up from that quantity to the demand curve and read the price. The price is what buyers are willing to pay for that quantity.

This second step is the trap Edwards hammered in Class 11. In his class example, MR and MC cross at a quantity of 8 and a height of $2. The quantity is 8, but the price is $4, not $2. The $2 is the marginal cost (and marginal revenue) of the 8th unit. The price comes from demand: at a quantity of 8, buyers are willing to pay $4. In his words, MR = MC "is a story about the optimum quantity, and then the pricing for that quantity depends on what the market will bear."

Two side points. First, the monopolist maximizes profit (revenue minus cost), not revenue. Revenue is highest where MR = 0 (at Q = 12 in his example), which is a bigger quantity than the profit-maximizing one. Second, fixed costs do not change the answer. "Marginal cost is not affected by fixed costs at all," so MR = MC gives the same Q and the same P whatever the fixed cost is. A fixed cost only changes how big the profit is, and whether it is positive.

Formulas when MC is constant

When marginal cost is a flat line at c (like Problem Set 2's MC = $1), setting a − 2bQ = c gives:

QuantityQm = (a − c) ÷ 2bQc = (a − c) ÷ b, twice Qm
PricePm = (a + c) ÷ 2Pc = c
Profit(Pm − c) × Qm0 (price equals MC)
Deadweight loss½ × (Qc − Qm) × (Pm − c)0

With flat MC, the monopolist sells exactly half the competitive quantity, and its price is halfway between the demand intercept and MC. Check with Problem Set 2: a = 5, b = 1, c = 1 gives Qm = 4 ÷ 2 = 2 and Pm = 6 ÷ 2 = 3. These formulas are shortcuts; if MC slopes up, go back to setting MR = MC directly.

Monopoly compared with competition

The efficient quantity is where demand meets MC. Demand is the marginal benefit (MB) curve, since its height is buyers' willingness to pay, so this is where MB = MC. Edwards called it the "social or Pareto optimum," and it is what a perfectly competitive market would produce. The monopoly point is "always to the southwest" of it on the MR curve, which means:

  • Less quantity: Qm is below Qc.
  • Higher price: Pm is above Pc, and above marginal cost.
  • Deadweight loss: between Qm and Qc, every unit has willingness to pay above marginal cost, yet it is never produced. The total value of those missing trades is the deadweight loss (DWL), the triangle between demand and MC from Qm out to Qc.
  • A transfer from consumers to the monopolist: the units that are still sold now cost more, so part of what was consumer surplus becomes the monopolist's profit.

Why does a profit-maximizer leave value on the table? Because selling those extra units would mean cutting the price on everything it already sells. Restricting quantity is profitable for the firm, and costly for society. Edwards: "the amount that the monopolist gains is not as great as the amount that's lost." He also defined deadweight loss broadly as anything "driving a wedge between" marginal cost and marginal benefit. Monopoly does it by holding price above MC; a tax does it with the tax wedge.

The a to h welfare table

His slides label every area of the monopoly graph with a letter and ask where each one goes. Q1 and P1 are the monopoly outcome; Qc and Pc are the competitive outcome.

Perfect competition (Qc)Monopoly (Q1)
Consumer surplusa + b + c + d + ea + b
Producer surplusf + g + hc + d + f + g
Total surplusa + b + c + d + e + f + g + ha + b + c + d + f + g
Deadweight loss0e + h

How to read it. Under competition, consumers pay Pc and buy Qc, so consumer surplus is everything under demand and above Pc. Under monopoly the price rises to P1, so consumers keep only a + b. Area c + d, the price increase times the units still sold, goes to the monopolist. His slide: "Area c+d is consumer surplus under perfect competition, but producer surplus under monopoly." That is a transfer; society does not lose it, it just changes hands. Areas e and h belong to the units between Q1 and Qc that are no longer produced, so nobody gets them: that is the deadweight loss.

The same table with the notebook numbers: competition gives consumer surplus 18 and producer surplus 18 (total 36). Monopoly gives consumer surplus 8 and producer surplus 24 (total 32). The transfer c + d = $1 price rise × 8 units = 8. The deadweight loss e + h = 2 + 2 = 4, and 36 − 32 = 4 checks it.

Profit with zero fixed cost: revenue minus the area under MC

Total cost is fixed cost plus the cost of each unit, and the cost of each unit is its marginal cost. So with zero fixed cost, total cost is the area under the MC curve from 0 to the quantity sold, and

Profit = P × Q − (area under MC from 0 to Q). With MC rising from the origin, that area is a triangle. Profit is the whole area between the price line and the MC curve.

This is the Taylor Swift part (f). Revenue is $200 × 10 million = $2 billion. MC rises from $0 to $100 at 10 million tickets, so total cost is ½ × 10 million × $100 = $500 million. Profit = $1.5 billion. The tempting shortcut, (P − MC) × Q = ($200 − $100) × 10 million = $1 billion, treats every ticket as if it cost $100, but only the last ticket costs $100; earlier ones cost less. Edwards wrote on the key that he got it wrong at first too, and gave full credit for $1 billion. Know the right answer, $1.5 billion. When MC is flat (Problem Set 2), every unit costs the same, so (P − MC) × Q is exactly right.

A monopolist has no supply curve

A competitive market has a supply curve: for each price, the quantity firms want to sell. A monopolist does not take the price as given, so there is no such schedule. Given the demand curve it faces, it picks one point by MR = MC. If demand or MC shifts, it picks a new point. Edwards said shifts in MC "bleed through" into the profit-maximizing choice, "but not 'along the supply curve'." He also stressed that "a monopolist doesn't produce where MC = P," which means it does not produce where MC = MB, and that is the source of the inefficiency.

His Humira example shows a demand increase. Humira was a patented drug for autoimmune diseases such as arthritis, and evidence suggested autoimmune disease was becoming more common in the U.S. When demand shifts out from D1 to D2, MR shifts out too (MR1 to MR2). The new MR2 crosses MC at a larger quantity, and the price read off the higher demand curve is higher. Both P and Q rise.

If MC rises instead, MR = MC happens at a smaller quantity, and the price read off demand is higher: Q falls and P rises.

Patents: a monopoly on purpose

"If monopoly is bad, then ... why have patents?" Innovation has high fixed costs (years of research and clinical trials for a drug) and low marginal costs once the product exists (each pill is cheap to make). If anyone could copy a new drug right away, competition would push its price down to marginal cost, and the inventor could never earn back the research cost, so fewer drugs would be invented. A patent grants a temporary monopoly: the inventor charges a monopoly price for a while, and the profit pays for the invention. The trade-off is the cost covered above: while the patent lasts, prices are high, quantity is low, and there is deadweight loss.

Humira shows both sides. Its patent let AbbVie charge monopoly prices for years. When the monopoly ended in 2023 and rival versions entered, Humira's revenue fell sharply: entry pushed the price toward marginal cost, so much of the old monopoly profit became consumer surplus.

What government can do

  • Antitrust laws: "laws designed to promote competition and prevent monopolization." In 2024 a judge ruled that Google has an illegal monopoly on search. In Problem Set 2, the point was that the Google case focused on dominance of the industry, not on the prices consumers pay (Google search is free to users; it charges advertisers).
  • Regulation: setting or capping prices, as with local water, electricity, and gas utilities.
  • Public ownership: the government owns and runs the monopoly, like the U.S. Postal Service.
  • Limits on patents and other legal protections, and moral suasion.

His summary: "Perfect competition is rare."

Worked examples

Example: Edwards' class notebook

Demand is P = 6 − Q/4 and marginal cost is MC = Q/4, with no fixed costs. Find the monopoly quantity, price, and profit, the efficient outcome, and the deadweight loss.

  1. MR: same intercept, twice the slope. MR = 6 − Q/2.
  2. Set MR = MC: 6 − Q/2 = Q/4, so 6 = 3Q/4 and Q = 8. At Q = 8, MR = MC = 6 − 4 = 2.
  3. Price from demand, not from MR = MC: P = 6 − 8/4 = $4. (Not $2.)
  4. Profit: revenue $4 × 8 = 32. Total cost is the triangle under MC: ½ × 8 × 2 = 8. Profit = 32 − 8 = 24.
  5. Efficient outcome, demand = MC: 6 − Q/4 = Q/4 gives Q = 12, and P = 3. That is "half again as big as eight," as he put it.
  6. Deadweight loss: the triangle between demand and MC from 8 to 12. Base 12 − 8 = 4; height = price minus MC at Q = 8, 4 − 2 = 2. DWL = ½ × 4 × 2 = 4.
  7. Consumer surplus: ½ × 8 × (6 − 4) = 8 under monopoly, compared with ½ × 12 × (6 − 3) = 18 under competition.

Answer: Q = 8, P = $4, profit 24. Efficient Q = 12 at P = $3. Deadweight loss 4. If you add a fixed cost of 30, Q and P stay at 8 and $4, and profit becomes 24 − 30 = −6. A fixed cost never changes the monopoly quantity or price.

Example: Taylor Swift, Fall 2025 question 3 (e) to (g)

The Eras Tour sold about 10 million tickets for about $2 billion. MC is a straight line from the origin through $100 at 10 million tickets ($150 at 15 million). Fixed costs are zero. Draw the monopoly, find profit, and decide whether more tickets should have been sold.

  1. Average price: $2 billion ÷ 10 million = $200. So the monopoly point is 10 million tickets at $200.
  2. Find demand. At 10 million, MR must equal MC = $100, and price is $200. Write demand as P = a − bQ, so MR = a − 2bQ (Q in millions). Then a − 10b = 200 and a − 20b = 100. Subtracting, 10b = 100, so b = 10 and a = 300. Demand is P = 300 − 10Q (from $300 down to 30 million), and MR = 300 − 20Q (from $300 down to 15 million).
  3. Graph: draw demand and MR, mark MR = MC at 10 million, go up to demand at $200, and label both.
  4. Profit: revenue $200 × 10 million = $2 billion. Total cost = area under MC = ½ × 10 million × $100 = $500 million. Profit = $1.5 billion.
  5. Efficient quantity: demand = MC, 300 − 10Q = 10Q, so Q = 15 million at $150.
  6. Deadweight loss: ½ × (15 − 10) million × ($200 − $100) = $250 million.

Answer: Monopoly at 10 million tickets and $200. Profit $1.5 billion (the key also accepted $1 billion). Yes, more tickets should have been sold: the efficient quantity is 15 million, where willingness to pay equals marginal cost, so the monopoly cut quantity by a third.

Example: Problem Set 2, question 3(e), monopoly with flat MC

The domestic oil market has demand P = 5 − Q and a flat supply curve, MC = $1. First find the competitive outcome, then suppose the industry becomes a monopoly.

  1. Competition: P = MC = 1, so 5 − Q = 1 and Q = 4. Consumer surplus = ½ × 4 × (5 − 1) = 8. Producer surplus = 0, since price equals MC on every unit.
  2. Monopoly: MR = 5 − 2Q. Set MR = MC: 5 − 2Q = 1, so Q = 2. (Half the competitive quantity, as the flat-MC formula says.)
  3. Price from demand: P = 5 − 2 = $3. Formula check: (5 + 1) ÷ 2 = 3.
  4. Consumer surplus: ½ × 2 × (5 − 3) = 2.
  5. Profit (producer surplus): (3 − 1) × 2 = 4. With flat MC every unit costs $1, so this box is exact.
  6. Deadweight loss: ½ × (4 − 2) × (3 − 1) = 2. Check: 2 + 4 + 2 = 8, the old total surplus.

Answer: Q = 2, P = $3, consumer surplus 2, producer surplus 4, deadweight loss 2. (The official key wrote producer surplus as zero in part (e) but then said the monopolist "profits $4 as before" in part (f). Profit of 4 is right.)

Common mistakes

Mistake: Reading the price off the MR = MC intersection (saying the notebook price is $2).

Correct: MR = MC gives only the quantity. Go straight up to demand for the price: Q = 8, P = $4. This is the exact trap he flagged in Class 11.

Mistake: Drawing MR parallel to demand, or barely steeper, or starting it at a different intercept.

Correct: MR starts at demand's vertical intercept and hits the quantity axis halfway to demand's quantity intercept (Swift: $300 down to 15 million, while demand reaches 30 million).

Mistake: Setting P = MC for the monopolist, or setting MR = 0.

Correct: P = MC is the competitive (efficient) outcome, used only for comparison. MR = 0 maximizes revenue, not profit. The monopolist sets MR = MC.

Mistake: Computing profit as (P − MC) × Q when MC slopes up (Swift: $1 billion).

Correct: With zero fixed cost, profit = revenue minus the area under MC: $2 billion − $500 million = $1.5 billion. The box only works when MC is flat.

Mistake: Counting c + d as deadweight loss.

Correct: c + d moves from consumers to the monopolist, so it is a transfer. Deadweight loss is only e + h, the triangle between demand and MC from Q1 to Qc.

Mistake: Saying a higher fixed cost makes the monopolist raise its price or cut quantity.

Correct: Fixed costs do not change MC or MR, so Q and P stay the same. Only profit changes (notebook with fixed cost 30: still 8 units at $4, profit −6).

Mistake: Drawing a supply curve for the monopolist, or saying a demand increase moves it "along supply."

Correct: A monopolist has no supply curve. When demand rises, demand and MR shift out, the new MR = MC quantity is larger, and the price read off the new demand is higher: both P and Q rise (Humira).

Mistake: Calling any big or popular seller a monopoly, or deciding by counting firms.

Correct: Ask how much of the demand curve the firm sees and whether buyers have close substitutes. Two rideshare firms can have market power; a Giants game has substitutes.

Practice questions

Edwards promised a monopoly question "shorter than" last year's 18-point Taylor Swift question, so question 1 is a 14-point story in that style. The rest are the 2-point and 4-point pieces he likes to ask on their own. Do them on paper first, with a ruler for the graphs.

14 points Monster profits. Pop Mart holds the exclusive rights to make Labubu dolls, the fanged little monsters that became a craze in 2025. Suppose that last year Pop Mart sold about 15 million Labubus in the U.S. for about $675 million in total (ignore resale). Cheap knockoffs, nicknamed "Lafufus," are sold online and on street corners.

(a) (2 points) What was the average price of a Labubu? Is this a perfectly competitive market, or does Pop Mart have market power, even though Lafufus exist? Briefly explain.

(b) (4 points) I have drawn Pop Mart's marginal cost curve for you below. Assume demand is a straight line and Pop Mart maximizes profit. Draw what demand looked like, and add any other curves you need to explain the market equilibrium, with values when you know them.

(c) (3 points) Pop Mart's fixed costs are zero. Are its profits zero? If not, how big are they? Show your work.

(d) (3 points) Should more Labubus have been sold, in the efficient sense where marginal benefit equals marginal cost? How many, and how big is the deadweight loss?

(e) (2 points) A K-pop star is photographed with a Labubu on her bag, and demand jumps to P = 80 − Q. Marginal cost does not change. What are Pop Mart's new quantity and price? Briefly explain.

Show answer

(a) Average price = $675 million ÷ 15 million = $45. Pop Mart has market power. It is the only legal producer of Labubus, so its rights to the character work as a barrier to entry, and fans who want the real thing do not see a Lafufu as a close substitute. That means Pop Mart faces a downward-sloping demand curve and can price above marginal cost. The knockoffs make demand somewhat more elastic, but they do not make the market competitive.

Full credit: $45, market power, and the reason: a unique product with no close substitute (knockoffs are imperfect substitutes) protected by a barrier to entry.

(b) The monopoly point is 15 million dolls at $45. At that quantity MR must equal MC, which is $30. Write demand as P = a − bQ, so MR = a − 2bQ (Q in millions):

  1. Demand at the monopoly point: a − 15b = 45.
  2. MR = MC at the monopoly point: a − 30b = 30.
  3. Subtract: 15b = 15, so b = 1 and a = 60.

Demand is P = 60 − Q, from $60 on the price axis down to 60 million. MR is MR = 60 − 2Q: the same $60 intercept, twice as steep, hitting the quantity axis at 30 million. MR crosses MC at 15 million (both $30), and the price is read straight up on demand: $45.

Full credit: demand from $60 to 60 million through (15 million, $45); MR from $60 to 30 million, twice as steep; MR = MC marked at 15 million and $30; dotted line up to demand at $45, with values on both axes.

(c) No, profits are not zero. Revenue = $45 × 15 million = $675 million. With zero fixed cost, total cost is the area under MC up to 15 million, a triangle: ½ × 15 million × $30 = $225 million. Profit = $675 million − $225 million = $450 million. The box ($45 − $30) × 15 million = $225 million undercounts, because only the last doll costs $30 to make; earlier dolls cost less.

Full credit: $450 million, from revenue minus the triangle under MC, with the work shown. (On the Fall 2025 key he also accepted the box version, but the triangle is the right answer.)

(d) Yes. The efficient quantity is where demand (marginal benefit) meets MC: 60 − Q = 2Q, so Q = 20 million at $40. Pop Mart cut output from 20 million to 15 million, a quarter less, because selling more would mean cutting the price on every doll. Deadweight loss = ½ × (20 − 15) million × ($45 − $30) = $37.5 million.

Full credit: yes; efficient quantity 20 million where demand = MC (not MR = MC); deadweight loss $37.5 million, the triangle between demand and MC from 15 million to 20 million.

(e) New MR = 80 − 2Q. Set MR = MC: 80 − 2Q = 2Q, so Q = 20 million. Price from the new demand: 80 − 20 = $60. Both quantity and price rise. A monopolist has no supply curve to slide along, so you redo MR = MC with the new demand and MR.

Full credit: Q = 20 million and P = $60, found from the new MR = MC and the new demand, and the statement that both rise.

2 points A wheat farmer in a competitive market can sell one more bushel without changing the price, so her marginal revenue equals the price. Why is a monopolist's marginal revenue below the price it charges? Briefly explain.

Show answer

A monopolist faces the whole downward-sloping demand curve and must charge everyone the same price. To sell one more unit it has to lower the price, and the lower price applies to every unit it was already selling, not only to the new one. So MR equals the price of the new unit minus the revenue lost on all the earlier units, which is less than the price.

Full credit: says the price cut to sell one more unit applies to all earlier units (one price for everyone), so MR is below price.

2 points A monopolist faces demand P = 30 − Q and marginal cost MC = Q. Your study partner finds that MR and MC cross at a height of $10 and says the monopoly price is $10. Is she right? Briefly explain.

Show answer

No. MR = 30 − 2Q (same intercept, twice as steep). Setting MR = MC gives 30 − 2Q = Q, so Q = 10, where MR and MC are both $10. That intersection only gives the quantity. The price is what buyers will pay for 10 units, read off the demand curve: P = 30 − 10 = $20. The $10 is the marginal cost of the 10th unit.

Full credit: Q = 10 from MR = MC, and P = $20 read off demand, with the reason that MR = MC sets quantity and demand sets the price.

4 points Cal gives one company the exclusive right to sell beer at Memorial Stadium. Demand per game is P = 20 − Q, where Q is thousands of beers, and the marginal cost of a beer is constant at $6. Fixed costs are zero. Draw demand, MR, and MC. Label the monopoly quantity and price and the competitive quantity and price with values, and shade consumer surplus, profit, and deadweight loss. How big is each? Show your work.

Show answer

Competitive benchmark: price equals MC, so 20 − Q = 6 and Q = 14 thousand at $6. Consumer surplus would be ½ × 14 × 14 = 98, or $98,000.

Monopoly: MR = 20 − 2Q. Set MR = MC: 20 − 2Q = 6, so Q = 7 thousand beers. Price from demand: 20 − 7 = $13. (With flat MC the monopolist sells exactly half the competitive quantity.)

  1. Consumer surplus = ½ × 7 × (20 − 13) = 24.5, or $24,500.
  2. Profit = ($13 − $6) × 7 thousand = $49,000. MC is flat, so every beer costs $6 and the box is exact.
  3. Deadweight loss = ½ × (14 − 7) × (13 − 6) = 24.5, or $24,500.
  4. Check: 24.5 + 49 + 24.5 = 98, the competitive total.

Full credit: MR from $20 to 10 thousand, twice as steep as demand; monopoly at 7 thousand and $13; competitive at 14 thousand and $6; consumer surplus $24,500, profit $49,000, deadweight loss $24,500, each shaded in the right place.

2 points In the stadium beer question, consumers lose $73,500 of surplus when the company gets the exclusive contract, but the company's profit is only $49,000. Where did the rest go? Which part is a transfer and which part is a deadweight loss? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Consumer surplus falls from $98,000 to $24,500, a loss of $73,500. Of that, $49,000 is a transfer: the $7 price increase on the 7 thousand beers still sold moves from fans to the company, so society as a whole does not lose it. The other $24,500 is deadweight loss: beers 7 thousand to 14 thousand were worth more to fans than the $6 they cost to serve, but they are never sold, so nobody gets that surplus. As Edwards put it, what the monopolist gains is not as great as what is lost.

Full credit: $49,000 transfer (the price rise on units still sold) and $24,500 deadweight loss (units worth more than MC that are not sold), with the difference between the two explained.

2 points Suppose the stadium beer company also pays Cal a flat fee of $30,000 per game for the exclusive contract, no matter how many beers it sells. What happens to the price of beer, the number of beers sold, and the company's profit? Briefly explain.

Show answer

The price stays at $13 and quantity stays at 7 thousand beers. The flat fee is a fixed cost: it does not change the cost of one more beer, so MC and MR are unchanged and MR = MC happens at the same quantity, with the same price on demand. Only profit changes: $49,000 − $30,000 = $19,000 per game.

Full credit: P and Q unchanged because a fixed cost does not change marginal cost; profit falls to $19,000.

2 points Go back to Pop Mart (demand P = 60 − Q, MC = 2Q, Q in millions). A new tariff raises the marginal cost of every Labubu by $6, so MC = 2Q + 6. What are the new quantity and price? Briefly explain.

Show answer

MR = 60 − 2Q. Set MR = MC: 60 − 2Q = 2Q + 6, so 4Q = 54 and Q = 13.5 million. Price from demand: 60 − 13.5 = $46.50. Higher marginal cost makes MR = MC happen at a smaller quantity, and the price read off demand at that smaller quantity is higher. Quantity falls by 1.5 million and price rises by $1.50, much less than the $6 cost increase, because the monopolist was already pricing well above marginal cost.

Full credit: Q falls to 13.5 million and P rises to $46.50, from MR = MC with the new MC and the price read off demand.

4 points A studio holds the rights to the only official video game for a hit film franchise. Once the game exists, one more download costs essentially nothing, so MC = $0. Demand is P = 40 − Q, where Q is millions of downloads. Draw demand, MR, and MC. Find the monopoly price and quantity, the studio's profit (ignore the cost of making the game), the efficient quantity, and the deadweight loss.

Show answer

MR = 40 − 2Q. Set MR = MC: 40 − 2Q = 0, so Q = 20 million downloads, and the price from demand is 40 − 20 = $20. Revenue = $20 × 20 million = $400 million, and since each download costs nothing, profit is $400 million (before the cost of making the game).

Efficient: demand meets MC where 40 − Q = 0, so Q = 40 million at a price of $0. Every download has a positive willingness to pay and costs nothing to provide. Deadweight loss = ½ × (40 − 20) million × $20 = $200 million.

One twist: because MC is zero here, MR = MC is the same as MR = 0, so profit and revenue peak at the same quantity. That is special to MC = 0; with positive MC the profit-maximizing quantity is smaller than the revenue-maximizing one.

Full credit: MR from $40 to 20 million; MC along the axis at $0; Q = 20 million and P = $20 marked; profit $400 million; efficient Q = 40 million; deadweight loss $200 million shaded between demand and MC from 20 million to 40 million.

2 points There are two big rideshare companies, Uber and Lyft. There are dozens of lunch places within a few blocks of Sproul Plaza. Which sellers are more likely to have market power? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Uber and Lyft. Market power is about how much of the demand curve a firm sees and whether buyers have close substitutes, not about counting firms. Between them Uber and Lyft see most of the demand for rideshares, and network effects (more riders attract more drivers) keep new rivals out, so they can raise prices without losing all their customers. Each lunch place faces many close substitutes a block away, so its demand curve is nearly flat and it has little market power.

Full credit: Uber and Lyft, explained by close substitutes and how much of the demand curve each seller faces (not the number of firms).

2 points Novo Nordisk holds patents on semaglutide, the drug in Ozempic and Wegovy, and charges far more than the marginal cost of a dose. If monopoly causes deadweight loss, why does the government grant patents that create monopolies on purpose? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Inventing a drug has a huge fixed cost (years of research and clinical trials), while making each dose has a low marginal cost. Without a patent, rivals could copy the drug right away, competition would push its price down to marginal cost, and the inventor could never earn back the research cost, so fewer new drugs would be invented. A patent grants a temporary monopoly whose profit pays for the invention. The price is high prices, low quantity, and deadweight loss until the patent expires and rivals enter.

Full credit: the trade-off: temporary monopoly profit pays for high fixed research costs (more innovation), at the cost of deadweight loss while the patent lasts.

2 points In 2024 a federal judge ruled that Google has an illegal monopoly in search. But Google search is free to users. How can a product that costs consumers $0 be an illegal monopoly? Briefly explain.

Show answer

The case was about Google's dominance of the search industry, not about the prices consumers pay. Google used anti-competitive practices, such as paying to be the default search engine on the iPhone, that kept rivals from getting a foothold. The prices that came up in the case were the prices advertisers pay, which Google could raise because of its dominance. (This was Problem Set 2, question 4.)

Full credit: dominance and anti-competitive practices (such as the default-search deals) as the concern, and that the relevant prices were ad prices, not consumer prices.

2 points At the monopoly quantity, buyers are still willing to pay more for one more unit than it costs to make. Why doesn't a profit-maximizing monopolist sell those extra units? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Because selling another unit means lowering the price on every unit, not only the extra one. The extra unit brings in its price, but the monopolist loses the price cut on all the units it was already selling, so its marginal revenue is below marginal cost past the MR = MC point. Those sales would add to total surplus but subtract from profit, so the monopolist restricts quantity. The units it does not sell, where willingness to pay is above marginal cost, are the deadweight loss.

Full credit: MR is below price because of the price cut on all units, so past MR = MC the extra unit lowers profit even though willingness to pay exceeds MC; the lost units are the deadweight loss.

One-minute review

  • Market power comes from no close substitutes plus barriers to entry: patents, high fixed costs, licenses, network effects, and natural monopoly.
  • MR is below price because the price cut hits every unit, not just the new one. MR = new unit's price minus the loss on earlier units.
  • Linear demand P = a − bQ gives MR = a − 2bQ: same intercept, twice as steep, hits the Q axis halfway.
  • Step 1: Q where MR = MC. Step 2: price straight up on demand. Notebook: Q = 8 and P = $4, not $2.
  • Flat MC = c: Qm = (a − c) ÷ 2b, Pm = (a + c) ÷ 2, competitive Q is twice Qm.
  • Compared with competition: less Q, higher P, deadweight loss e + h, and c + d transferred from consumers to the monopolist.
  • Zero fixed cost: profit = revenue minus area under MC. Swift: $2 billion − $500 million = $1.5 billion (key also took $1 billion); efficient 15 million, monopoly 10 million.
  • Notebook: Q 8, P $4, profit 24, efficient 12 at $3, DWL 4. Problem Set 2: Q 2, P $3, CS 2, profit 4, DWL 2.
  • No supply curve. Demand up: P and Q up (Humira). Fixed costs change only profit.
  • Patents trade deadweight loss now for innovation. Policy: antitrust (Google, where the issue was dominance, not consumer prices), regulation (utilities), public ownership (U.S. Postal Service).
  • Digital goods with MC = $0: MR = 0 sets quantity, price is half the demand intercept, and the efficient price is $0.
