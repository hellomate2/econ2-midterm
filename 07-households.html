Classes 7 and 8 · Households

Households: budgets and the Rational Spending Rule

This unit covers how a household splits a fixed income across goods: the budget line it has to stay on, the diminishing marginal utility behind every demand curve, and the Rational Spending Rule that tells it when it has the best mix. On the exam this shows up as short 2-point questions where a price or a demand curve changes and you explain, with the rule, how the household's purchases adjust.

How it shows up on the exam

On the Fall 2025 midterm (the one past midterm Edwards has written for this course), households appeared twice in the 16-point block of 2-point short answers:

  • Question 1(a) asked which curve slopes downward, demand or supply, and why. The answer is demand, because marginal benefit (marginal utility, willingness to pay) falls as quantity rises: "more is better," but at a decreasing rate.
  • Question 1(c) gave you the Rational Spending Rule for clothing and food, said the price of clothing fell, and asked how you would change your clothing purchases to restore the equality. The answer: buy more clothing, which lowers its marginal utility.

Problem Set 2 then spent two full questions on this unit. Question 1 (5 points in the answer key) used log utility for Alice and Bob: compute each person's marginal utility, check whether a $100 transfer raises their combined utility, and find the transfer that maximizes it. Question 2 (3 points) was about electricity: write the optimum condition, say what happens when the price of electricity rises, then say what a heatwave does, with the hint that "shift" is emphasized on purpose.

In Class 8 a student asked whether the budget line graph is on the midterm. Edwards said a rise in the price of one good is "a pretty fair game question," and so is a change in income. He also floated a harder version where income and one price change at the same time and called it "a very difficult question."

How he grades a Rational Spending Rule question: write the rule, say which side of the equation moved and why (the price is in the denominator), then say which quantity you change and which way that moves the marginal utility. The official answers always use diminishing marginal utility to close the loop: buy less of a good and its marginal utility rises, buy more and it falls.

The ideas, explained

The budget constraint

A household has a fixed amount of income and spends it on goods. The budget constraint says the total it spends cannot exceed its income, and in this course we assume it spends exactly all of it. With many goods, income equals the price of each good times the quantity bought, added up over every good. With the two goods Edwards uses, food and clothing:

Pf × qf + Pc × qc = Income

Edwards picked food and clothing on purpose. You need both, but eating does not change how much you enjoy your clothes and the other way around, so the example avoids the complications that come from substitutes and complements.

Watch his axes: food goes on the vertical axis and clothing on the horizontal axis. Every number below depends on that orientation.

  • The vertical intercept is what you get if you spend all your income on food: Income ÷ Pf.
  • The horizontal intercept is what you get if you spend all your income on clothing: Income ÷ Pc.
  • The slope is −Pc ÷ Pf, the price of the horizontal good over the price of the vertical good, with a minus sign because the line slopes down.

The slope is a relative price. It tells you how many meals you give up to buy one more unit of clothing, which makes it an opportunity cost. Edwards compared it directly to the opportunity costs of Bill and Chris on the island. He also admitted, "I always kind of get this one screwed up," so double-check it: rise over run is (Income ÷ Pf) ÷ (Income ÷ Pc), and the income cancels, leaving Pc ÷ Pf.

His numbers: $100 a day, food $20, clothing $50

Edwards's example: you have $100 a day to spend (about $3,000 a month). A decent meal costs $20, and $50 buys an outfit. The budget line is $20 × qf + $50 × qc = $100.

Piece of the lineCalculationResult
Food (vertical) intercept$100 ÷ $205 meals
Clothing (horizontal) intercept$100 ÷ $502 units of clothing
Slope−$50 ÷ $20−2.5 meals per unit of clothing

So one outfit costs you 2.5 meals. Edwards joked that he sits at the food intercept: "all food and no clothes."

Income changes shift the line; price changes pivot it

When income changes and prices do not, both intercepts move by the same proportion and the slope (a ratio of prices) stays the same. The line shifts out in parallel if income rises and in if it falls. Edwards's story: your grandparents give you money for your birthday, so you can buy more of everything.

When one price changes, only that good's intercept moves. Suppose the price of clothing rises from $50 to $100. The food intercept does not move, because if you spend everything on food, the price of clothing is irrelevant: $100 ÷ $20 is still 5 meals. The clothing intercept moves in, from 2 to $100 ÷ $100 = 1. The line pivots clockwise around the food intercept and gets steeper (slope −$100 ÷ $20 = −5). If the price of clothing falls instead, say to $25, the clothing intercept moves out to 4 and the line pivots outward and gets flatter (slope −1.25).

Income change: parallel shift, slope unchanged. Price change of one good: pivot around the other good's intercept. A higher price of the horizontal good makes the line steeper. His advice: "slow down and think about which price is changing or if money income is changing."

Two combinations are worth knowing. If both prices double while income stays the same, both intercepts are cut in half and the slope (the ratio) is unchanged, so the line shifts in exactly as if income had been halved. If income rises and one price rises at the same time, do it in two steps: shift the line out for the income, then pivot it in on the good whose price rose. Where it ends up depends on the sizes, which is why Edwards called that version very difficult.

Total utility and marginal utility

Utility is an index of how well off you are, measured in "utils," not dollars. Total utility is the level of well-being from everything you consume. Marginal utility (MU) is the extra utility from one more unit: the rate of change of total utility, or in calculus terms its derivative.

Two different questions get two different answers, and Edwards tested both with polls in Class 5. Why does total utility rise with quantity? "More is always better." Why does marginal utility fall? "One more is never as good as the last one more." That second idea is the law of diminishing marginal utility.

The textbook example is Sarah's ice cream. Her total utility from cones per hour is 50, 90, 120, 140, 150, and then 140 for the sixth cone. Marginal utility is the change from one row to the next: 50, 40, 30, 20, 10, and −10.

Notice that total utility keeps rising through the fifth cone even while marginal utility falls the whole time. Marginal utility only turns negative after total utility peaks. Edwards said total utility does not have to bend back down at all; it just has to rise at a decreasing rate.

The demand curve is the marginal utility curve

The height of your demand curve at any quantity is your willingness to pay for that unit, and your willingness to pay is how much that unit adds to your happiness. So the demand curve is your marginal utility schedule. Because marginal utility falls as you buy more, demand slopes down. Edwards put it this way in Class 8: "the demand curve is marginal utility."

This gives you a tool for every question in this unit. Moving down and to the right along your demand curve (buying more) means lower marginal utility. Moving up and to the left (buying less) means higher marginal utility. A shift of the whole demand curve out means marginal utility is higher at every quantity.

Goods differ in how fast their marginal utility falls. Edwards's slide says food's MU falls steeply (demand for food is more inelastic, "more vertical") and clothing's falls more slowly (flatter demand). A good whose MU falls fast changes little in quantity when its price changes.

The Rational Spending Rule

Here is the rule Edwards told the class to put on the cheat sheet, in both Class 7 and Class 8 ("for the exam, what I think you should do is write this down"):

Rational Spending Rule: MUc ÷ Pc = MUf ÷ Pf, and the same ratio for every good you buy: MUa ÷ Pa = MUb ÷ Pb = ... = MUz ÷ Pz. The marginal utility from the last dollar spent is the same for every good.

Dividing a marginal utility by a price gives marginal utility per dollar: the extra happiness you get from one more dollar spent on that good. Here is why the ratios must be equal. Suppose the last dollar on clothing gives you more extra happiness than the last dollar on food. Then you could move a dollar from food to clothing and be happier while spending the same total. Whenever the ratios differ, Edwards says, "you're making a mistake," and you should shift spending toward the good with more happiness per dollar. You stop only when no move helps, which is when the ratios are equal.

Two things trip people up. First, the rule equalizes marginal utility per dollar, not marginal utility itself and not total utility. Second, prices are set by the market and you cannot change them. The only thing you control is how much you buy, and changing quantities is what moves the marginal utilities.

What to do when a price rises

This is the core exam move. Suppose the price of clothing rises and you start out at the optimum.

  1. The price of clothing is in the denominator, so a bigger price makes MUc ÷ Pc smaller. Now MUc ÷ Pc is less than MUf ÷ Pf. Clothing gives less happiness per dollar than food.
  2. To restore equality you need to raise the left side back up. You cannot touch the price, so you raise the numerator: buy less clothing. Because of diminishing marginal utility, the last unit of clothing you still buy is worth more to you, so MUc rises.
  3. You may also buy more food. That lowers MUf and brings the right side down to meet the left.

Whether you only cut clothing or also buy more food depends on how food and clothing combine in your preferences, and Edwards said data, not theory, decides that. Either route ends at equal ratios.

Price of a good rises: buy less of it, so its MU rises. Price of a good falls: buy more of it, so its MU falls. Quantity and MU always move in opposite directions.

The most-missed poll of the unit asked exactly this. The price of something you buy rises and you buy less of it; what happens to your marginal utility of that good? It rises. About half the class answered wrong, and Edwards spent a long time on it. The picture that makes it clear: the price rise moves you up your demand curve to the northwest, and the height of the demand curve there (your willingness to pay, your marginal utility) is higher. His everyday version: lunch prices near campus go up, you still have to eat, so you buy less, and the last lunch you do buy is worth more to you.

Two more poll lessons from Class 8. When the price of X falls, "buy more X so its MU rises" is internally inconsistent, because buying more always lowers MU. The correct answer was "buy more X so its MU falls." And when the price of X falls, another valid fix is to buy less of Y so MUY rises to match the bigger left side; that makes sense if X and Y are substitutes.

A price change versus a demand shift

Problem Set 2, Question 2 tests whether you can tell these apart. A rise in the price of electricity is a movement along the electricity demand curve: the quantity demanded falls, and MUE rises because you are now at a smaller quantity. A heatwave is different. With air conditioning, every kilowatt-hour is worth more to you, so MUE is higher at every quantity. Since the demand curve is the marginal utility schedule, that is a shift of demand outward.

Log utility: MU = 1/c

Edwards's last households slides look at utility from total consumption spending, c, in dollars. Utility rises with c, but at a decreasing rate (the curve is concave), so marginal utility falls as consumption rises. His example is u(c) = log(c), the natural log (the ln key on a calculator), whose marginal utility is MU(c) = 1 ÷ c. The more you consume, the less one more dollar adds.

Problem Set 2, Question 1 used this for Alice and Bob, who have identical utility functions. Alice consumes $400 a day and Bob $160. Alice's MU is 1/400 = 0.0025 and Bob's is 1/160 = 0.00625. A dollar is worth far more to Bob because he has less. If Alice gives Bob $100, their combined utility rises from log 400 + log 160 ≈ 11.07 to log 300 + log 260 ≈ 11.26, because the dollars move from someone with low MU to someone with high MU.

To find the transfer that maximizes combined utility, think of each dollar moved as having a marginal benefit (Bob's MU) and a marginal cost (Alice's MU). Keep transferring while the marginal benefit exceeds the marginal cost, and stop where they are equal. Since their utility functions are identical, their MUs are equal only when their consumption is equal: $560 ÷ 2 = $280 each, which is a $120 transfer from Alice to Bob.

The problem set then asked whether this would please them. If they are strangers, almost certainly not: Alice loses $120, and Bob's income jumps 75%, and arguments about marginal utility persuade very few people. If they are married or partnered, an arrangement like this is more plausible, since couples share consumption and can see how happy each other is.

Worked examples

Example: the $100 budget line and its shifts

Income is $100 a day, food costs $20 a meal (vertical axis), and clothing costs $50 a unit (horizontal axis). Find the intercepts and slope. Then show what happens if income rises to $200, and separately if clothing rises to $100.

  1. Write the constraint: $20 × qf + $50 × qc = $100.
  2. Food intercept: all $100 on food buys $100 ÷ $20 = 5 meals. Clothing intercept: all $100 on clothing buys $100 ÷ $50 = 2 units.
  3. Slope: −Pc ÷ Pf = −$50 ÷ $20 = −2.5. Each unit of clothing costs 2.5 meals.
  4. Income rises to $200: the intercepts become 10 meals and 4 units, the slope stays −2.5. Parallel shift out.
  5. Clothing rises to $100 (income back at $100): the food intercept stays at 5, the clothing intercept falls to 1, and the slope becomes −$100 ÷ $20 = −5. The line pivots clockwise around the food intercept.

Answer: Intercepts 5 meals and 2 units, slope −2.5. Higher income is a parallel shift out to 10 and 4. A higher clothing price pivots the line in to a clothing intercept of 1 with slope −5, and the food intercept does not move.

Example: his 10/$50 = 2/$10 example after a 10% price rise

From the Class 8 slides. The last unit of clothing adds 10 utils and costs $50. The last unit of food adds 2 utils and costs $10. (This food price is different from the $20 in the budget line example.) Is the household optimizing? Then the price of clothing rises 10%.

  1. Check the rule: MUc ÷ Pc = 10 ÷ $50 = 0.2, and MUf ÷ Pf = 2 ÷ $10 = 0.2. Equal, so yes. Edwards's intuition: clothing is five times as expensive, "so you better like it five times as much," and 10 is five times 2.
  2. Clothing rises 10%, to $55. If purchases do not change, 10 ÷ $55 ≈ 0.182, which is less than 0.2. Clothing now gives less happiness per dollar, so this is no longer optimal.
  3. Fix 1: buy less clothing until its MU rises to 11. Then 11 ÷ $55 = 0.2 = 2 ÷ $10.
  4. Fix 2: split the adjustment. Buy somewhat less clothing so MUc rises to 10.5, and somewhat more food so MUf falls to about 1.91. Then 10.5 ÷ $55 ≈ 0.191 ≈ 1.91 ÷ $10.

Answer: At first the household is optimizing (0.2 utils per dollar on both). After the price rise it should buy less clothing, raising MUc, and possibly more food, lowering MUf, until the two ratios are equal again. (The slide captions describe Fix 2 as moves "of about 0.5%," and Edwards admitted the slide was "written a little bit strange." Use the numbers 10.5 and 1.91, or simply the MU = 11 version.)

Example: Fall 2025 midterm 1(c), the price of clothing falls

Paraphrase: you are given MUc ÷ Pc = MUf ÷ Pf. The price of clothing falls. How could you change your clothing purchases to bring the relationship back to equality? Briefly explain. (2 points)

  1. Say which side moved: Pc is the denominator on the left, so when it falls, the left side gets bigger. Clothing now gives more happiness per dollar than food.
  2. Say what fixes it: the left side has to come back down, so MUc has to fall.
  3. Say how: buy more clothing. With diminishing marginal utility, each extra unit adds less, so MUc falls until the ratios are equal.

Answer: The lower price of clothing raises the left side of the equation. Increase your purchases of clothing, which lowers the marginal utility of clothing and brings the left side back down to equal MUf ÷ Pf.

Example: Problem Set 2, Question 2, electricity

A household spends on electricity (E) and everything else (ee). (a) What condition holds at the optimum? (b) The price of electricity rises. (c) Separately, a heatwave hits and the household has air conditioning.

  1. (a) MUE(E) ÷ PE = MUee(ee) ÷ Pee: each dollar brings the same marginal utility whichever good it buys.
  2. (b) A higher PE shrinks the left side, and the right side has not changed, so equality is lost. Reduce electricity, which raises MUE and pushes the left side back up, and increase everything else, which lowers MUee and brings the right side down. This is a movement along the electricity demand curve.
  3. (c) In a heatwave each unit of electricity runs the air conditioning, so MUE jumps at every quantity. Since demand is the marginal utility schedule, demand for electricity shifts out. With prices unchanged, the left side is now bigger than the right.
  4. To restore equality, increase electricity along the new demand curve, which lowers MUE, and reduce everything else, which raises MUee.

Answer: Price rise: buy less electricity and more of everything else (movement along demand). Heatwave: demand for electricity shifts out, so buy more electricity and less of everything else.

Example: Problem Set 2, Question 1, Alice and Bob

Both have u(c) = log(c), so MU = 1/c. Alice consumes $400 a day and Bob $160. Find their MUs, check whether a $100 transfer from Alice to Bob raises the sum of their utilities, and find the transfer that maximizes the sum.

  1. MUAlice = 1/400 = 0.0025. MUBob = 1/160 = 0.00625. They differ because MU is the reciprocal of consumption, and Alice consumes more, so her MU is lower.
  2. Before the transfer: log 400 + log 160 ≈ 5.99 + 5.08 = 11.07. After $100 moves: log 300 + log 260 ≈ 5.70 + 5.56 = 11.26. The sum rises, because Bob's MU was much higher than Alice's.
  3. Maximize the sum: the marginal benefit of a transferred dollar is Bob's MU, and the marginal cost is Alice's MU. Transfer until they are equal. Identical utility functions mean equal MUs only at equal consumption: ($400 + $160) ÷ 2 = $280 each.

Answer: MUs of 0.0025 (Alice) and 0.00625 (Bob). The $100 transfer raises the sum from 11.07 to 11.26. The sum is largest with a $120 transfer, leaving each with $280.

Common mistakes

Mistake: "The price of clothing went up, so the marginal utility of clothing falls."

Correct: The price rise makes MUc ÷ Pc fall. Your response is to buy less clothing, and buying less makes MUc rise. This was the poll half the class missed.

Mistake: "The price of X fell, so buy more X so that its MU rises."

Correct: Buying more always lowers MU. Buy more X so that MUX falls. Edwards called the other version "internally inconsistent."

Mistake: Writing the budget line slope as −Pf ÷ Pc, or using the wrong axes.

Correct: With food vertical and clothing horizontal, the slope is −Pc ÷ Pf (price of the horizontal good over price of the vertical good). For $50 clothing and $20 food that is −2.5.

Mistake: Moving both intercepts when only the price of clothing changes, or drawing a price change as a parallel shift.

Correct: A change in the price of clothing moves only the clothing intercept; the line pivots around the food intercept. Only a change in income (or both prices changing by the same proportion) shifts the line in parallel.

Mistake: Thinking the rule says the marginal utilities themselves must be equal (10 should equal 2).

Correct: The rule equalizes marginal utility per dollar. A good five times as expensive must give five times the marginal utility at the optimum.

Mistake: Treating "utility" and "marginal utility" as the same thing ("I bought less, so I'm less happy, so MU fell").

Correct: Total utility is the level of well-being; marginal utility is the addition from one more unit. Total utility can fall while marginal utility rises. The demand curve is marginal utility, not total utility.

Mistake: Calling the heatwave a movement along the electricity demand curve, or calling the price rise a shift of demand.

Correct: A change in the good's own price is a movement along demand. Something that raises MU at every quantity (the heatwave) shifts demand out.

Mistake: Using the log base 10 key for log utility.

Correct: "log" here is the natural log, the ln key. Check: ln 400 ≈ 5.99 and ln 160 ≈ 5.08.

Practice questions

2 points Which curve slopes downward, demand or supply? Briefly explain why.

Show answer

Demand slopes downward. The height of the demand curve is willingness to pay, which is the marginal utility of that unit. Because of diminishing marginal utility, each additional unit adds less to your happiness than the one before, so your willingness to pay falls as quantity rises. More is better, but at a decreasing rate.

Full credit: name demand and give the reason in terms of falling marginal benefit, marginal utility, or willingness to pay.

2 points The Rational Spending Rule for clothing and food says MUc ÷ Pc = MUf ÷ Pf. Suppose the price of clothing falls. How could you change your purchases of clothing to restore the equality? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Pc is in the denominator of the left side, so a lower price makes the left side bigger than the right. To bring it back down, MUc has to fall. Buy more clothing: with diminishing marginal utility, each additional unit adds less, so MUc falls until the two ratios are equal again.

Full credit: "increase clothing purchases," plus the reason: the left side rose, and buying more lowers MUc.

1 point The price of something you buy rises, and you respond by buying less of it. What happens to your marginal utility of that good? Explain using the demand curve.

Show answer

It rises. The price rise moves you up and to the left along your demand curve (northwest). The height of the demand curve is your marginal utility, and it is higher at the smaller quantity. Intuitively, if you buy fewer lunches because lunch got expensive, the last lunch you still buy is worth more to you.

Full credit: "MU rises," tied to buying a smaller quantity and diminishing marginal utility (or moving up the demand curve).

2 points Income is $100, food costs $20 a meal, and clothing costs $50 a unit. Put food on the vertical axis and clothing on the horizontal axis. Find both intercepts and the slope. What happens to the line if the price of clothing rises to $100?

Show answer

Food intercept: $100 ÷ $20 = 5 meals. Clothing intercept: $100 ÷ $50 = 2 units. Slope: −$50 ÷ $20 = −2.5 meals per unit of clothing. If clothing rises to $100, the food intercept stays at 5 (spending everything on food does not involve the price of clothing), the clothing intercept moves in to $100 ÷ $100 = 1, and the slope becomes −5. The line pivots clockwise around the food intercept and gets steeper.

Full credit: intercepts 5 and 2, slope −2.5, and a pivot in around the fixed food intercept to a clothing intercept of 1.

2 points Start again from income $100, food $20, clothing $50. Now income rises to $200 and the price of clothing rises to $100 at the same time. Where are the new intercepts, and what is the new slope? Describe the change in words.

Show answer

Food intercept: $200 ÷ $20 = 10 meals. Clothing intercept: $200 ÷ $100 = 2 units. Slope: −$100 ÷ $20 = −5. Think of it in two steps. The income rise shifts the line out in parallel to intercepts 10 and 4. The clothing price rise then pivots it in around the food intercept, pulling the clothing intercept back from 4 to 2. In this case the clothing intercept ends up exactly where it started, while the food intercept doubles, so the new line is steeper and lies outside the old one everywhere except at the clothing intercept.

Full credit: intercepts 10 and 2, slope −5, with the shift-then-pivot reasoning.

2 points Income stays at $100, but both prices double: food to $40 and clothing to $100. What happens to the budget line? What other change would produce exactly the same line?

Show answer

Food intercept: $100 ÷ $40 = 2.5 meals. Clothing intercept: $100 ÷ $100 = 1 unit. Slope: −$100 ÷ $40 = −2.5, the same as before, because the ratio of prices did not change. The line shifts in, parallel to the original. That is the same line you would get by cutting income in half to $50 with the original prices.

Full credit: intercepts 2.5 and 1, slope unchanged, parallel shift in, equivalent to halving income.

2 points You buy coffee at $4 a cup (marginal utility 12) and burritos at $10 each (marginal utility 30). Are you following the Rational Spending Rule? Then the price of coffee rises to $6. How should you change what you buy, and why?

Show answer

Before: coffee gives 12 ÷ $4 = 3 utils per dollar and burritos give 30 ÷ $10 = 3 utils per dollar. Equal, so yes. After: coffee gives 12 ÷ $6 = 2 utils per dollar, which is less than 3. Buy less coffee, which raises its marginal utility, and possibly more burritos, which lowers theirs, until the two ratios are equal again.

Full credit: show both ratios equal at 3, then that coffee falls to 2, then buy less coffee (MU rises) and possibly more burritos (MU falls).

2 points The price of X falls. A classmate says, "To get back to MUX ÷ PX = MUY ÷ PY, buy more X so that MUX rises." What is wrong with this? Give two adjustments that do work.

Show answer

It is internally inconsistent: buying more X moves you down your demand curve, so MUX falls, it cannot rise. The lower price made the left side too big. Fix 1: buy more X so MUX falls, bringing the left side down. Fix 2: buy less Y so MUY rises, bringing the right side up; that is natural if X and Y are substitutes.

Full credit: say that more X means lower MUX, and give "more X, MUX falls" and "less Y, MUY rises."

3 points A household splits its budget between electricity (E) and everything else (ee). (a) What condition holds when it maximizes utility? (b) The price of electricity rises. How does it adjust? (c) Instead, a heatwave hits and the household runs its air conditioning. How does this change its choices, and which curve shifts?

Show answer

(a) MUE ÷ PE = MUee ÷ Pee: each dollar brings the same marginal utility whichever good it buys.

(b) The higher PE shrinks the left side. Buy less electricity, which raises MUE, and more of everything else, which lowers MUee, until the ratios are equal. This is a movement along the electricity demand curve.

(c) The heatwave raises the marginal utility of electricity at every quantity, which is an outward shift of the demand curve for electricity. At unchanged prices the left side is now bigger. Buy more electricity, moving along the new demand curve and lowering MUE, and less of everything else, raising MUee.

Full credit: the condition in (a); less E and more ee in (b); in (c), demand for electricity shifts out, then more E and less ee.

2 points Alice and Bob both have u(c) = log(c), so MU = 1/c. Alice consumes $500 a day and Bob $100. Find each person's marginal utility. What transfer from Alice to Bob makes the sum of their utilities as large as possible? Why?

Show answer

MUAlice = 1/500 = 0.002 and MUBob = 1/100 = 0.01. A dollar moved from Alice to Bob has a marginal benefit equal to Bob's MU and a marginal cost equal to Alice's MU. Since Bob's is higher, keep transferring until the two are equal. With identical utility functions that happens at equal consumption: ($500 + $100) ÷ 2 = $300 each, a $200 transfer. (Check: ln 500 + ln 100 ≈ 10.82, while 2 × ln 300 ≈ 11.41.)

Full credit: both MUs, the transfer of $200 to $300 each, and the reason: marginal benefit (Bob's MU) equals marginal cost (Alice's MU) only at equal consumption.

2 points Sarah's total utility from 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 ice cream cones per hour is 50, 90, 120, 140, 150, and 140 utils. Find the marginal utility of each cone. Where does total utility peak, and what is true of marginal utility after that?

Show answer

Marginal utility is the change in total utility from each extra cone: 50, 40, 30, 20, 10, and −10. Total utility peaks at 5 cones (150 utils). After the peak, marginal utility is negative: the sixth cone makes her worse off, so someone would have to pay her to eat it. Note that total utility kept rising through the fifth cone even though marginal utility fell the whole way.

Full credit: the six MUs, the peak at 5 cones, and negative MU for the sixth.

  • Budget line with food vertical and clothing horizontal: intercepts Income ÷ Pf and Income ÷ Pc, slope −Pc ÷ Pf. His example ($100, food $20, clothing $50): 5 meals, 2 units, slope −2.5.
  • Income change: parallel shift. Price change of one good: pivot around the other good's intercept. Both prices doubling is the same as halving income.
  • Total utility rises at a decreasing rate; marginal utility (the extra from one more unit) falls. "One more is never as good as the last one more."
  • The demand curve is the marginal utility curve. Buy less and MU rises; buy more and MU falls.
  • Write this down: MUa ÷ Pa = MUb ÷ Pb. Equal marginal utility per dollar, not equal marginal utility.
  • Price rises: the ratio falls, so buy less of that good (its MU rises) and maybe more of others (their MU falls). Price falls: buy more (MU falls).
  • 10/$50 = 2/$10 = 0.2. Clothing to $55 gives 10/$55 ≈ 0.18, so cut clothing until its MU is 11.
  • Own price change: movement along demand. Heatwave: MU higher at every quantity, so demand shifts out; buy more electricity and less of everything else.
  • Log utility (ln key): MU = 1/c. Alice $400 has 0.0025, Bob $160 has 0.00625. The sum of utilities peaks at equal consumption: $280 each, a $120 transfer.
