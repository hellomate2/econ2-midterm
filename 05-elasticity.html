Classes 6 and 7 · Elasticity

Elasticity

Elasticity measures how strongly quantity responds when price changes, and it tells you whether a curve should be drawn steep or flat. Of price elasticities, Edwards said in Class 11, "Those are gonna be on there," so expect at least one calculation and at least one question where you must argue for a steep or flat curve.

The ideas, explained

What elasticity measures

The price elasticity of demand answers one question: when the price changes by 1%, by what percent does the quantity demanded change? Edwards insists on this direction. Quantity responds to price, never the other way around. When a student in Class 6 suggested measuring how price responds to quantity, he answered that in economics elasticity is "the responsiveness of the quantity."

ε = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP, where each percentage change is (new − old) ÷ old. Quantity goes on top, price on the bottom.

To compute a percentage change, take the change and divide by the starting value. If quantity goes from 100 to 80, the change is −20, and −20 ÷ 100 = −20%. His Fall 2025 answer key uses these simple percentage changes from the starting values, not the "midpoint" method some textbooks teach, so use simple percentages.

Read the number as a multiplier. An elasticity of −3 means a 1% price increase cuts quantity demanded by 3%. In Class 6 he gave this feel for a big elasticity: a 10% change in price that causes a 40% change in quantity demanded means a size of 4, very elastic.

Why do economists use percentages instead of the slope? Elasticity is "unit free." The slope of a demand curve depends on units (dollars per gallon, dollars per pound), so you cannot compare the slope of tobacco demand with the slope of fish demand. Percent changes have no units, so elasticities can be compared across any two markets.

The sign: write it both ways

Demand slopes down, so price and quantity move in opposite directions and the demand elasticity is negative. The sources disagree on how to report it. The Class 6 slide defines elasticity "in absolute value," so the lunch answer would be 0.5. The Class 7 slides and the Fall 2025 answer key keep the minus sign, so the official lunch answer is −0.5, and the Class 7 slides write the inelastic range as −1 < ε < 0 and the elastic range as ε < −1. Either way, you classify by size (absolute value): ignore the sign and compare the number to 1.

Write both so no grader can object: "ε = −0.5, so |ε| = 0.5, which is less than 1: inelastic." When you compare two elasticities, compare sizes. −3 is more elastic than −0.5, even though −3 is the smaller number.

Supply elasticity is positive, because a higher price brings a larger quantity supplied. There is no sign issue for supply.

Slope and elasticity are related but not the same

You can rewrite the definition so it uses the slope of the curve. Since %ΔQ = ΔQ ÷ Q and %ΔP = ΔP ÷ P:

ε = (ΔQ/Q) ÷ (ΔP/P) = (ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q) = (1/slope) × (P/Q)

Why the reciprocal of the slope? Price is on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis, so the slope of a demand curve is ΔP ÷ ΔQ (rise over run). Elasticity puts quantity on top, so it uses ΔQ ÷ ΔP, which is one over the slope. This is the formula from Problem Set 2.

The formula has two parts, and the second part, P/Q, changes as you move along any curve. So a straight demand line, which has the same slope everywhere, does not have the same elasticity everywhere. Take demand P = 12 − Q, whose slope is −1, so 1/slope = −1:

Point on the lineCalculationεCategory
Q = 4, P = 8−1 × 8/4−2Elastic
Q = 6, P = 6−1 × 6/6−1Unit elastic (the midpoint)
Q = 8, P = 4−1 × 4/8−0.5Inelastic

The pattern to remember: on a straight demand line, demand is more elastic at high prices (upper left, where P/Q is big) and less elastic at low prices (lower right, where P/Q is small). Unit elasticity sits at the midpoint. Edwards got this backwards for a moment in Class 6 and then corrected himself, so trust the formula: at high prices, a $1 change is a small percent of the price but the same quantity change is a big percent of the small quantity, so demand is elastic there.

Inelastic means steep, elastic means flat

Edwards told the class to put this on the cheat sheet in exactly these words: inelastic means steep and elastic means flat. It follows from ε = (1/slope) × (P/Q). A steep curve has a big slope, so one over the slope is small, and the elasticity is small. A flat curve has a slope near zero, so one over the slope is huge. The limit cases make it exact: a horizontal line has slope 0, so ε = 1 ÷ 0, which is infinite; a vertical line has ε = 0.

The slides call this "a useful, but crude approximation," because P/Q also matters. For drawing exam graphs it is the rule you use.

Why does the shape matter? Because it decides what a shift of the other curve does. Below, the same supply shift (S1 up to S2) hits two demand curves that pass through the same starting point, (6, $6).

With inelastic demand, buyers keep buying almost the same amount, so the price has to rise a lot before quantity falls enough to match the smaller supply. With elastic demand, buyers walk away quickly, so a small price rise is enough. The flip side: a supply shift reveals how elastic demand is, and a demand shift reveals how elastic supply is. This is the egg question in reverse. If you see a supply shock produce a huge price jump and a small quantity change, demand must be steep.

Limit cases

Perfectly inelastic demand is a vertical line: buyers want the same quantity at any price, and ε = 0. Perfectly elastic demand is a horizontal line at some price P*: demand is zero if the price is above P*, and buyers would take any amount at P* (the slides write "infinite if P < P*"). The same shapes apply to supply. A vertical supply curve means the quantity is fixed no matter the price (Edwards' examples: Cal football tickets, since the stadium has a fixed number of seats, and visas under a quota). A horizontal supply curve means sellers will supply any amount at one price (his example: soda sold in Berkeley, since Coca-Cola and PepsiCo can sell in nearby cities, so they will not accept a lower price in Berkeley).

In class, a poll asked: demand is vertical and supply shifts inward. What happens to quantity? The answer was "absolutely nothing." What happens to price? It rises "a whole lot." He redrew it afterward because, in his words, "I fooled a lot of people there," so practice reading the limit cases off a quick sketch.

What makes demand elastic or inelastic

Demand elasticity comes from how easily buyers can do without the good when its price rises.

  • Substitutes. If there is a close substitute, buyers switch when the price rises, so demand is elastic. If not, demand is inelastic. This was the egg answer: no one-for-one substitute, so steep demand.
  • Need. Goods people need (food, gasoline to get to work, health care) have inelastic demand. Goods people can skip (restaurant meals, since you can stay home and eat ramen) have elastic demand. Numbers he showed from a health economics textbook: all health care about −0.22 ("if you break a leg, you kind of have to get the leg set"), restaurant meals −2.3, fresh tomatoes −4.6.
  • Time horizon. Demand is more elastic in the long run, because buyers have time to find substitutes. Gasoline in the short run is inelastic: you still have to commute, BART is a poor substitute, and you cannot buy an electric vehicle or move overnight. In the long run you can do both, so demand is more elastic.
  • Narrow versus broad goods. A narrowly defined good is more elastic than its whole category, because the rest of the category is a substitute. A tax on cigars alone sends smokers to other tobacco products; a tax on all tobacco has no obvious substitute. One lunch spot raising its prices loses customers to the next spot (Edwards guessed −1.5 or larger in size), while lunch out in general was −0.5 on the Fall 2025 midterm.

Firms know this. Restaurants face elastic demand, so when food or labor costs rise, Edwards noted, they often shrink portions instead of raising prices.

The reverse use: from a quantity shock to a price change

The formula works in both directions. If you know the elasticity of demand and a supply shock changes the quantity sold by some percent, solve for the price change:

%ΔP = %ΔQ ÷ ε. Example: ε = −0.5 and a supply shock cuts quantity by 10%, so %ΔP = −10% ÷ −0.5 = +20%.

The logic: the supply shock moves the market along the demand curve, so the price must change by whatever amount makes buyers willing to accept the new quantity. The less elastic the demand, the bigger the price change needed. This is the use Edwards described in Class 11 as one of the two "pretty important uses of the elasticity measure."

Elasticity and total spending

Total spending (total expenditure) is price times quantity. When the price rises, two things happen at once: buyers pay more on each unit they still buy, and they buy fewer units. Which effect wins depends on the elasticity.

%Δ(P × Q) ≈ %ΔP + %ΔQ (add the signed percentage changes). If demand is inelastic, a price rise raises spending. If demand is elastic, a price rise lowers spending. If demand is unit elastic, spending does not change.

The Class 7 slides worked this out from a starting price of $100 and quantity of 200 (spending 20,000), with a 1% price increase. The third row is his poll question.

Edwards called 1 "this magical number": a 1% price increase brings an exactly offsetting 1% drop in quantity, so "P times Q doesn't change at all."

On a graph, spending is the rectangle with height P and width Q. When supply shifts back and price rises, you gain a rectangle on top (the higher price on the units still bought) and lose a strip on the side (the units no longer bought, at the old price).

The shortcut only works for small changes. For big changes like the ones in this graph (a 50% price rise), multiply P × Q before and after instead. The slides also show a method with logs for big changes; Edwards said in Class 7 that it is not on the midterm, so skip it.

Two applications from the slides. First, an illicit drug crackdown that raises the price would increase total spending on the drug if demand is inelastic. Second, "at sufficiently high prices, demand always becomes elastic (because budget is finite)," which matches the straight-line pattern: high on the demand curve, demand is elastic. The same logic runs in reverse: when the Strait of Hormuz reopens and gasoline supply shifts out, inelastic demand means price falls a lot, quantity rises a little, and total gas spending falls.

The Brown University trap

In Class 7, Edwards criticized a Wall Street Journal story ("Americans Have Spent $100 Billion More on Fuel") built on a Brown University projection. The projection multiplied the price increase by the quantity people bought before the increase. That assumes quantity demanded stays exactly the same, which is only true if demand is perfectly inelastic (vertical). In reality people respond: they drive less or take BART. So "price rise times old quantity" overstates the extra spending. His summary: "Prices change and then quantities change. That's the thing." Even with gas, where demand is inelastic, quantity still falls somewhat.

Price elasticity of supply

εS = %ΔQS ÷ %ΔP. It is positive. Elastic if above 1, inelastic if below 1, 0 for a vertical supply curve, infinite for a horizontal one. Steep supply is inelastic and flat supply is elastic, just as with demand.

Supply elasticity comes from how easily sellers can change how much they produce.

  • Short run: usually inelastic. Sellers face resource constraints. Oil takes time to explore and drill. Farmers can only harvest crops already in the ground. A local brewery plans its brews in advance. So producing more quickly means sharply rising marginal cost, and the supply curve is steep.
  • Long run: usually more elastic. With time, sellers can build capacity, enter the market, or adopt new technology. His example: oil supply became more elastic in the long run through fracking.
  • Sellers who can sell elsewhere: very elastic. Soda sold in Berkeley and gasoline sold at the world oil price have nearly flat supply, because sellers will not accept a lower price from one city or country when they can sell to someone else.

Supply elasticity is revealed by a demand shift. If demand shifts out and supply is very elastic, quantity rises a lot and price barely moves. If supply is very inelastic (Cal football seats), price rises a lot and quantity barely moves. These shapes are also what decides who bears a tax: the more inelastic side of the market bears more of it. The taxes unit builds on this.

Worked examples

Example: lunches around Berkeley (Fall 2025 midterm, question 1(b))

The price of lunch rises from $10 to $11, and quantity demanded falls from 50,000 to 47,500. Find the price elasticity of demand. Is demand elastic or inelastic?

  1. Percentage change in price, from the starting value: (11 − 10) ÷ 10 = +10%.
  2. Percentage change in quantity, from the starting value: (47,500 − 50,000) ÷ 50,000 = −2,500 ÷ 50,000 = −5%.
  3. Divide quantity by price: ε = −5% ÷ 10% = −0.5.
  4. Compare the size to 1: |ε| = 0.5 < 1, so demand is inelastic.
  5. Bonus check on spending: $10 × 50,000 = $500,000 before and $11 × 47,500 = $522,500 after. Spending rose, as it should with inelastic demand (the shortcut gives 10% − 5% = about +5%).

Answer: ε = −0.5 (0.5 in absolute value), relatively inelastic because its size is less than 1.

Example: elasticity at points on P = 12 − Q

Use ε = (1/slope) × (P/Q) to find the elasticity at Q = 4 and Q = 8. Where is demand unit elastic?

  1. The slope of P = 12 − Q is −1, so 1/slope = −1.
  2. At Q = 4, the price is 12 − 4 = 8. So ε = −1 × (8/4) = −2. Elastic.
  3. At Q = 8, the price is 12 − 8 = 4. So ε = −1 × (4/8) = −0.5. Inelastic.
  4. Unit elastic needs (1/slope) × (P/Q) = −1, so P/Q = 1, meaning P = Q. With P = 12 − Q, that gives Q = 6 and P = 6, the midpoint of the line.

Answer: −2 at Q = 4, −0.5 at Q = 8, unit elastic at Q = 6. Same slope, different elasticities, because P/Q changes along the line.

Example: the spending poll (Class 7)

The elasticity of demand is −3 and the price rises 1%. What happens to total spending?

  1. Quantity response: %ΔQ = ε × %ΔP = −3 × 1% = −3%.
  2. Spending: %Δ(P × Q) ≈ %ΔP + %ΔQ = 1% + (−3%) = −2%.
  3. Reason: demand is elastic (size 3 is above 1), so the percentage drop in quantity is bigger than the percentage rise in price.

Answer: Quantity demanded falls 3%, and total spending falls by about 2%.

Example: gasoline from $3 to $4 (Class 7)

Gas rises from $3 to $4 a gallon. A typical driver buys 50 gallons a month, and the elasticity of gasoline demand is −0.37 (the newer Dallas Fed estimate; older studies said −0.04). Using simple percentage changes, how much does the driver buy now, and what happens to monthly spending?

  1. %ΔP = (4 − 3) ÷ 3 = +33.3% (an increase of a third).
  2. %ΔQ = ε × %ΔP = −0.37 × 33.3% = about −12.3%.
  3. New quantity: 50 × (1 − 0.123) = about 43.8, so about 44 gallons a month.
  4. This is a big change, so compute spending directly instead of using the shortcut: before, $3 × 50 = $150; after, $4 × 43.8 = about $175.
  5. Spending rises because demand is inelastic (0.37 < 1), but drivers still cut back about 6 gallons. Quantity responds even here.

Answer: About 44 gallons a month, and spending rises from $150 to about $175.

Example: the reverse use (Class 11)

The price elasticity of demand is −0.5. A supply shock cuts the quantity sold by 10%. By about how much does the price rise, and what happens to spending?

  1. Start from ε = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP and solve for the price change: %ΔP = %ΔQ ÷ ε.
  2. %ΔP = −10% ÷ −0.5 = +20%.
  3. Spending: %ΔP + %ΔQ = 20% − 10% = about +10%. It rises, because demand is inelastic.

Answer: The price rises about 20%, and spending rises (about 10% by the shortcut; this is a fairly big change, so 1.2 × 0.9 = 1.08 gives the exact +8%).

Common mistakes

Mistake: Dividing the price change by the quantity change (−10% ÷ −5% = 2 for the lunch problem).

Correct: Quantity goes on top: ε = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP. Elasticity is how quantity responds to price, so the lunch answer is −5% ÷ 10% = −0.5.

Mistake: Treating the slope as the elasticity, or saying a straight demand line has the same elasticity everywhere.

Correct: ε = (1/slope) × (P/Q). The slope is constant on a straight line, but P/Q is not, so demand is more elastic at the top of the line and less elastic at the bottom.

Mistake: Computing extra spending as the price increase times the old quantity (the Brown University projection).

Correct: When the price rises, quantity demanded falls, so this overstates the extra spending. It is only right if demand is perfectly inelastic. Use the new quantity, or add the percentage changes.

Mistake: Calling −3 "less elastic" than −0.5 because −3 is the smaller number.

Correct: Judge elasticity by size. |−3| = 3 is above 1 (elastic) and |−0.5| = 0.5 is below 1 (inelastic), so −3 is far more elastic.

Mistake: Using %ΔP + %ΔQ for a big change, such as a 33% or 100% price jump.

Correct: The shortcut is only accurate for small changes. For big ones, multiply price times quantity before and after and compare.

Mistake: Drawing steep demand for a good with many close substitutes, or flat demand for a necessity.

Correct: Inelastic means steep and elastic means flat. No close substitute, a necessity, or a short time to adjust gives steep demand. Close substitutes, a narrow good, or a long time to adjust gives flat demand.

Mistake: Computing percentage changes from the new value or from the midpoint.

Correct: His answer key divides each change by the starting value: from $10 to $11 is +10%, not +9.5% (midpoint) or +9.1% (new value).

Mistake: Assuming supply responds just as much in the short run as in the long run.

Correct: Supply is usually inelastic (steep) in the short run because of resource constraints like crops already planted or wells not yet drilled, and more elastic (flatter) in the long run when sellers can expand and enter.

How it shows up on the exam

Elasticity appeared in several forms on the Fall 2025 midterm and the problem sets. Here is what he has asked before.

  • Two-point calculation (Fall 2025 midterm, question 1(b), 2 points). The price of lunch around Berkeley rose from $10 to $11 and quantity demanded fell from 50,000 to 47,500. You had to find the price elasticity of demand and say whether demand is elastic or inelastic. The official answer: price rose 10%, quantity fell 5%, so the elasticity is −5% ÷ 10% = −0.5, relatively inelastic because its size is below 1.
  • Shape of a curve from substitutes (Fall 2025 midterm, question 2(a), 2 points). What is the likely shape of the market demand curve for eggs? The official answer: inelastic, relatively vertical in the short run, because eggs have no obvious one-for-one substitute and willingness to pay for them can be very high.
  • Graphs that must match your elasticity story (Fall 2025 midterm, questions 2(e) and 2(g), 4 points each). You drew the egg market at about $2 a dozen and 10 billion dozen a year with a steep demand curve, then showed an avian flu supply shock. Because demand is steep, the price jumped to about $6 while quantity fell only a little. A flat demand curve here would have lost points even with correct labels.
  • Elasticity from a slope (Problem Set 2, question 3(b)). With demand P = 5 − Q and flat marginal cost of $1, you used ε = (1/slope) × (P/Q). Supply elasticity is infinite because the slope of a flat line is zero. Demand elasticity at the equilibrium (Q = 4, P = 1) is (1/−1) × (1/4) = −0.25, inelastic.
  • Spending and elasticity (Class 7 poll). If the elasticity is −3 and price rises 1%, what happens to total spending? It falls about 2%. He called this kind of question "challenging" and said he could not remember whether he asked it last year. Know it anyway.
  • The reverse use (Class 11). He said the elasticity also tells you the percentage change in price when a supply shock changes quantity, and that "you'll be able to do that." Treat this as a likely question.

What earns full credit on a calculation: show both percentage changes (each computed from the starting value), divide quantity by price, state the number, and compare its size to 1 ("−0.5, so |ε| = 0.5 < 1: inelastic"). On a shape question, give the reason (substitutes, necessity, time to adjust) and then the shape (steep or flat). For big changes, multiply price times quantity before and after.

Practice questions

These are written the way Edwards writes his midterm: 2-point short answers that end in "Briefly explain" or "Show your work," 4-point graphs drawn "with values when you know them," and one long story question with several parts. Budget about one minute per point (60 minutes for 60 points), write your answer on paper first, then open the key. Each key is the length his answer boxes reward: a few sentences, the number, and the reason.

2 points Boba on Bancroft. A boba shop on Bancroft raises the price of a milk tea from $6.00 to $6.60, and cups sold per week fall from 8,000 to 6,400. What is the price elasticity of demand? Is demand relatively elastic or inelastic? Briefly explain why that makes sense for this shop.

Show answer

The price rose (6.60 − 6.00) ÷ 6.00 = +10%. Quantity fell (6,400 − 8,000) ÷ 8,000 = −20%. So ε = −20% ÷ 10% = −2, and |ε| = 2 is above 1: relatively elastic.

That fits the story. There are several other boba shops within a block, so this shop's milk tea has close substitutes, and customers walk next door when it raises its price. (Its revenue falls too, from $6.00 × 8,000 = $48,000 to $6.60 × 6,400 = $42,240.)

Full credit: both percentage changes computed from the starting values, ε = −2 (size 2), "elastic," and a close-substitutes reason.

2 points Graduation weekend. On a normal weekend, Berkeley hosts list 1,000 short-term rentals at an average of $200 a night. On commencement weekend the average price rises to $300 and listings rise to 1,100. What is the price elasticity of supply? Is supply elastic or inelastic? Briefly explain.

Show answer

%ΔP = (300 − 200) ÷ 200 = +50%. %ΔQ = (1,100 − 1,000) ÷ 1,000 = +10%. So εS = 10% ÷ 50% = 0.2, which is below 1: supply is inelastic, so the supply curve is steep.

In the short run the number of homes and spare rooms in Berkeley is fixed. A big price increase only persuades a few extra owners to list, so quantity supplied barely responds.

Full credit: 0.2 (positive), inelastic, and the fixed short-run stock of rooms as the reason.

2 points The reverse use. Suppose a disruption of oil shipping through the Strait of Hormuz cuts the quantity of gasoline sold in California by 4%. Short-run demand for gasoline has a price elasticity of about −0.2, and gas started at $5.00 a gallon. By about what percent does the price of gas rise, and to about what price? Show your work.

Show answer

Rearrange ε = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP to get %ΔP = %ΔQ ÷ ε = −4% ÷ −0.2 = +20%. The price rises about 20%, from $5.00 to about $6.00 a gallon.

The supply shock moves the market up along a steep demand curve. Drivers still have to get to work, so it takes a large price increase before they cut back even 4%. Spending on gas rises (1.20 × 0.96 = 1.15, so about 15% more).

Full credit: %ΔP = %ΔQ ÷ ε written out, about +20%, and about $6.00 a gallon.

2 points Same shock, two markets. A supply shock cuts the quantity sold by 10% in two markets: gasoline, with a demand elasticity of −0.25, and restaurant meals, with a demand elasticity of −2.5. By about what percent does the price rise in each market? Briefly explain the difference.

Show answer

Gasoline: %ΔP = −10% ÷ −0.25 = +40%. Restaurant meals: %ΔP = −10% ÷ −2.5 = +4%.

Gas demand is inelastic (steep): drivers have no good short-run substitute, so the price must rise a lot before buyers give up 10% of their purchases. Restaurant demand is elastic (flat): people can stay home and cook, so a small price increase is enough to cut purchases by 10%.

Full credit: +40% and +4% with the division shown, and steep versus flat demand (substitutes) as the reason.

2 points Raising ticket prices. Cal Athletics raises the price of student season tickets by 10%. Suppose the price elasticity of demand for these tickets is −0.4. What happens to the number of tickets sold and to Cal's ticket revenue? Briefly explain.

Show answer

%ΔQ = ε × %ΔP = −0.4 × 10% = −4%, so 4% fewer tickets are sold. Revenue changes by about %ΔP + %ΔQ = 10% − 4% = +6% (exactly, 1.10 × 0.96 = 1.056).

Revenue rises because demand is inelastic: the percentage drop in quantity is smaller than the percentage rise in price, so the higher price on the tickets still sold outweighs the lost sales.

Full credit: quantity down 4%, revenue up about 6%, and "inelastic, |ε| below 1" as the reason.

2 points A price cut. A food truck near Sproul cuts its burrito price by 5%. Demand for its burritos has an elasticity of −3. What happens to the number of burritos sold and to the truck's revenue? Briefly explain.

Show answer

%ΔQ = −3 × (−5%) = +15%. Revenue changes by about −5% + 15% = +10% (exactly, 0.95 × 1.15 = 1.09, about 9%).

Demand for one truck's burritos is elastic because there are many other places to eat nearby. The price cut pulls in many customers from the competition, and the extra burritos sold more than make up for the lower price on each one. With elastic demand, a price cut raises revenue.

Full credit: quantity up 15%, revenue up about 9 to 10%, and elastic demand (substitutes) as the reason.

2 points Time to adjust. Suppose the price elasticity of demand for gasoline is −0.1 in the first month after a price change and −0.4 after five years. The price of gas rises 25%. By how much does quantity demanded fall in each case? Which demand curve should you draw steeper? Briefly explain.

Show answer

First month: −0.1 × 25% = −2.5%. After five years: −0.4 × 25% = −10%. Short-run demand is less elastic, so the short-run curve is the steeper one.

In the first month people still have to commute in the cars they own. Over five years they can buy an electric vehicle, move closer to work, or switch to BART, so quantity responds more.

Full credit: −2.5% and −10%, the short-run curve drawn steeper, and the time-to-adjust reason.

2 points Required reading. A professor requires a $150 custom edition of a textbook, and homework can only be submitted with the access code that comes inside a new copy. What is the likely shape of the demand curve for this book: horizontal, vertical, or somewhere in between? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Nearly vertical (very inelastic). There is no substitute: used and library copies do not come with the access code, and students need it for their grade, so willingness to pay is high and the quantity bought hardly changes with the price.

If used copies or library reserves worked for the homework, students would have substitutes and demand would be flatter.

Full credit: "steep" or "nearly vertical," with the no-substitute (required for the grade) reason.

4 points No vacancy. Berkeley has 2,000 hotel rooms, and no hotel can add rooms on short notice. On a normal May weekend all 2,000 rooms rent at $200 a night. On commencement weekend, demand rises and rooms rent for $500 a night. Draw the supply curve and both demand curves, with values when you know them. What is the price elasticity of supply, and why does the price rise so much?

Show answer

Supply is vertical at 2,000 rooms, because the number of rooms is fixed this weekend. The elasticity of supply is 0: quantity changes by 0% while the price rises by 150%.

The demand shift moves the equilibrium straight up the vertical supply curve, from (2,000, $200) to (2,000, $500). Quantity cannot respond at all, so the whole demand shift shows up in the price.

Full credit: vertical supply at 2,000 labeled, D1 and D2 with equilibria at $200 and $500 marked on the price axis, εS = 0, and the fixed number of rooms as the reason. It must be clear that quantity does not change.

2 points Headline math. A headline says: "New tariff raises the price of imported bikes by $100. Americans bought 2 million imported bikes last year, so the tariff will cost bike buyers $200 million more a year." Briefly explain what is wrong with this calculation. When would it be right?

Show answer

It multiplies the price increase by the old quantity, which assumes quantity demanded does not change. When the price rises, some people buy a domestic bike, a used bike, or no bike, so fewer than 2 million imported bikes are sold and the extra spending is less than $200 million.

The calculation is only right if demand is perfectly inelastic (vertical, ε = 0). This is the mistake Edwards pointed out in the Brown University fuel projection: prices change, and then quantities change.

Full credit: says quantity demanded falls when the price rises, so the estimate is too high, and that it holds only with perfectly inelastic demand.

4 points Plugged in (a nonstandard case like Problem Set 2). Stores in Berkeley can order as many phone charging cables as they want from wholesalers at $2 each, so supply is flat at MC = $2. Demand is P = 6 − Q, with Q in thousands of cables per month. (a) Draw the market and label the equilibrium, CS, and PS with values. (b) Using ε = (1/slope) × (P/Q), find the elasticity of supply and the elasticity of demand at the equilibrium.

Show answer

(a) Set 6 − Q = 2, so Q = 4 thousand cables and P = $2. CS is the triangle under demand and above the price: ½ × 4 × (6 − 2) = 8, so $8,000 a month. PS = 0, because the price equals marginal cost on every unit, so there is no area between the price and the supply curve.

(b) Supply is flat, so its slope is 0 and 1/slope is infinite: supply is perfectly elastic. Demand has a slope of −1, so ε = (1/−1) × (2/4) = −0.5, which is inelastic at this point.

Full credit: (4, $2) marked, CS = 8 (thousand dollars), PS = 0, supply elasticity infinite, demand elasticity −0.5.

2 points Vertical demand. Suppose demand for a life-saving drug is perfectly vertical at 10,000 doses a month. A factory problem shifts supply up and to the left. What happens to the quantity and the price? What is the elasticity of demand? In this case, is "price increase times old quantity" a correct estimate of the extra spending?

Show answer

Quantity does not change at all: it stays at 10,000 doses. The price rises by the full upward shift of supply. The elasticity of demand is 0 (perfectly inelastic).

This is the one case where price increase times old quantity is right, because buyers purchase exactly the same amount after the price rises.

Full credit: quantity unchanged, price up a lot, ε = 0, and "yes, because quantity does not change."

Long question: Brewed awakening (16 points)

Most American coffee drinkers have a cup every morning. Tea and energy drinks exist, but for many people nothing replaces coffee. In a normal year, coffee sells for about $4 a pound and Americans buy about 3 billion pounds.

2 points (a) What is the likely shape of the market demand curve for coffee in the short run: horizontal, vertical, or in between? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Steep (inelastic). Many people drink coffee every day out of habit and for the caffeine, and there is no one-for-one substitute, so willingness to pay is high and quantity barely responds to price in the short run.

Full credit: "steep" or "inelastic," with the no close substitute or daily habit reason.

4 points (b) Draw typical supply and demand for coffee, at about $4 a pound and 3 billion pounds a year. Make sure the slopes match your story.

Show answer

Draw demand steep and supply upward sloping, crossing at (3 billion, $4), with dotted lines to both axes.

Full credit: the equilibrium at 3 billion pounds and $4 marked on both axes, demand clearly steep, both curves labeled.

4 points (c) A frost in Brazil destroys part of the coffee crop. The price rises to $6 a pound and Americans buy 2.7 billion pounds. Is this a supply shock or a demand shock? Redraw the market and show the shock, with values when you know them.

Show answer

A supply shock. The frost reduces how much coffee sellers can bring to market at every price, so supply shifts left (up). Nothing about the frost changes how much coffee drinkers want at each price, so demand stays put.

The new equilibrium is on the same steep demand curve at (2.7 billion, $6): a big price increase and a small drop in quantity.

Full credit: supply shifted left, demand unchanged, the new point (2.7 billion, $6) marked, and the old point (3 billion, $4) kept.

2 points (d) Using the two equilibria, compute the price elasticity of demand for coffee. Show your work.

Show answer

Only supply moved, so both points lie on the same demand curve and measure the elasticity of demand. %ΔP = (6 − 4) ÷ 4 = +50%. %ΔQ = (2.7 − 3) ÷ 3 = −10%. So ε = −10% ÷ 50% = −0.2, and |ε| = 0.2 is below 1: inelastic, which matches the steep curve.

Full credit: +50%, −10%, ε = −0.2, inelastic.

2 points (e) What happened to total spending on coffee? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Before: $4 × 3 billion = $12 billion. After: $6 × 2.7 billion = $16.2 billion. Spending rose by $4.2 billion, because demand is inelastic: the 50% price increase outweighs the 10% drop in quantity.

These are big changes, so multiply price times quantity directly. The shortcut 50% − 10% = 40% overstates the actual 35% increase.

Full credit: $12 billion before, $16.2 billion after, spending rises because demand is inelastic.

2 points (f) Forecasters expect a drought next year to cut the quantity of coffee by another 5%. Using your elasticity from (d), by about what percent will the price rise?

Show answer

%ΔP = %ΔQ ÷ ε = −5% ÷ −0.2 = +25%. From $6, that is about $7.50 a pound.

Full credit: +25% from %ΔP = %ΔQ ÷ ε (about $7.50 a pound).

One-minute review

  • ε = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP, with each percent change computed from the starting value. Quantity responds to price.
  • Lunches $10 to $11, 50,000 to 47,500: ε = −5% ÷ 10% = −0.5. Write "−0.5, so |ε| = 0.5 < 1: inelastic."
  • Elastic if the size is above 1, inelastic if below 1, unit elastic at 1. Vertical is 0, horizontal is infinite.
  • Inelastic means steep and elastic means flat. Steep demand: a supply shift moves price a lot and quantity a little.
  • ε = (1/slope) × (P/Q). On a straight line, demand is more elastic at higher prices. P = 12 − Q: −2 at Q = 4, −1 at Q = 6, −0.5 at Q = 8.
  • Inelastic when there is no close substitute, the good is a need, or time is short. Elastic with substitutes, a narrow good, or a long horizon.
  • Reverse use: %ΔP = %ΔQ ÷ ε. With ε = −0.5, a 10% quantity cut raises price 20%.
  • %Δ spending ≈ %ΔP + %ΔQ. Price up 1%: ε = −0.75 gives +0.25%, −1.5 gives −0.5%, −3 gives −2%. For big changes, multiply P × Q directly.
  • Gas $3 to $4 with ε = −0.37: about 44 gallons instead of 50, spending $150 to about $175. Never use the old quantity (the Brown trap).
  • Supply elasticity is positive, inelastic in the short run (crops planted, wells not drilled) and more elastic in the long run.
