Class 4 · Supply and demand

Supply and demand

This unit is the core toolkit of the course: why demand slopes down, why supply slopes up, where they cross, and what happens to price and quantity when one or both curves move. On the Fall 2025 midterm, supply and demand questions were worth 14 of the 60 points, including two 4-point graphs, and nearly every other question leans on these same curves.

The ideas, explained

What a market is, and the price-taker assumption

To Edwards, "the market" is any group of buyers and sellers. It does not have to be a physical place. Buyers make up the demand side and sellers make up the supply side, and they are two different groups of people. Keeping those groups separate is the single most useful habit in this unit.

The basic model assumes that both buyers and sellers are price takers. There are many small buyers and many small sellers who do not collude, so no single one of them can change the market price. Each one simply decides how much to buy or sell at the going price. Edwards calls this setup the competitive model. Later units (monopoly) drop the assumption on the seller side.

Price always goes on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis. That is a convention. In economics P and Q are determined together, at the point where the two curves cross; neither one causes the other.

Why demand slopes down: marginal benefit falls

A demand curve shows, at each price, how much buyers want to buy. Read it the other way and it shows, at each quantity, how much buyers are willing to pay for one more unit. That second reading is the important one: the demand curve is the marginal benefit curve, also called marginal utility or willingness to pay.

Marginal benefit falls as you get more of something. Edwards' cold drink example: on a hot day you might pay $5 for the first cold drink, maybe $4 for the second, and less for each one after that. The slides use ice cream: the first serving is fantastic, the second is good, the third you eat on a dare, and the fourth sends you to the Tang Center. Because each extra unit is worth less to you, you only buy more when the price falls. So demand slopes down.

Drink1st2nd3rd4th
Willingness to pay for that drink$5$4$3$2

At a price of $3 you buy 3 drinks: each of the first three is worth at least $3 to you, and the fourth is worth only $2. Lower the price to $2 and you buy 4. That is a downward-sloping demand curve, built from falling marginal benefit.

For a whole market there is a second reason. People differ. Edwards' world oil graph labels the top of the demand curve "rich consumers, high value users only" and the lower part adds poorer, lower-value users. At a high price only buyers who are both willing and able to pay stay in the market. As the price falls, more buyers enter. He told the class to blend the two ideas: each person's marginal utility falls (he called that "very, very ironclad"), and different people enter or leave at different prices.

Demand slopes down because marginal benefit (marginal utility, willingness to pay) falls as quantity rises: "more is better, but at a decreasing rate." This is a 2-point answer he has already asked and read aloud in class.

Why supply slopes up: marginal cost rises

A supply curve shows, at each price, how much sellers want to sell. Read it the other way and it shows the cost of producing one more unit: the supply curve is the marginal cost curve.

Marginal cost rises as output grows because producing more pulls in resources and people with higher opportunity costs. Tie it to Bill and Chris from Class 2. The first coconuts are cheap: Bill gathers them and gives up only 1 fish per coconut. Once Bill is fully on coconuts, more coconuts require Chris, who gives up 4 fish per coconut. The marginal cost of a coconut rises from 1 fish to 4 fish, so the supply curve for coconuts slopes up. The world oil supply graph shows the same thing with real producers: low-cost producers like Saudi Arabia (oil near the surface) at the bottom, and high-cost producers like U.S. fracking at the top. Edwards' own example: if he tried to raise cattle, he would be a very high marginal cost producer. Expanding output pulls in people who "used to be doing other things that they were better at."

He called supply "the most mercurial" curve. It can be flat (a vending machine will sell you as many drinks as you want at $2.99) or vertical (a lemonade stand with a fixed number of cups, or soybeans already harvested). The usual shape slopes up, and "basically it never slopes down."

Supply slopes up because marginal cost rises as production increases: extra output comes from producers with higher opportunity costs.

Equilibrium, shortage, and surplus

Equilibrium is where the demand and supply curves cross. At that price, the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers want, so the market clears. Every buyer who is willing and able to pay the equilibrium price gets the good, and every seller whose marginal cost is below that price sells.

If the price is below equilibrium, quantity demanded is larger than quantity supplied. That gap is a shortage (excess demand) equal to QD − QS. Buyers who cannot get the good bid the price up. If the price is above equilibrium, quantity supplied is larger than quantity demanded. That gap is a surplus (excess supply) equal to QS − QD, and sellers cut the price to sell their extra output. Either way the price moves back toward the crossing point.

Notice what does not happen: neither curve moves. When the price is too low or too high, buyers and sellers slide along the curves they already have. That leads to the most tested distinction in the unit.

Shift or movement: the good's own price never shifts its curve

Each curve is drawn holding everything else constant (ceteris paribus, "other things being equal") except the good's own price. So a change in the good's own price is already built into the curve. It moves you along the curve. Edwards: "A change in price will result in a movement along the curves."

Anything else that changes how much buyers want at every price, or how much sellers offer at every price, shifts the whole curve. The words matter. "Demand" means the whole curve, and "quantity demanded" means one point on it. A price rise lowers quantity demanded and leaves demand unchanged. The same goes for "supply" versus "quantity supplied."

A change in the good's own price is a movement along both curves. Only something other than the good's own price shifts a curve.

Two ways to describe the same shift, and both are correct:

  • A demand increase is a shift right (more quantity at every price) or, equivalently, up (higher willingness to pay at every quantity). Edwards: "Same thing."
  • A supply increase is a shift right (more quantity at every price) or, equivalently, down (lower marginal cost at every quantity). He called this one "a little bit hard to kind of think about, but it's true": the curve is down "because this is price and it's also cost." So a supply decrease is a shift left, which is the same as up.

He prefers to think "outward versus inward" for both curves, because outward always means more and inward always means less. One warning: one slide labels a supply increase as "QS shifts up," meaning more quantity supplied at each price, while the graphs show a supply increase moving the curve down. If an exam question says "supply shifts up," the safest reading is the graph's (left, higher marginal cost), and he has said that writing down how you read an unclear question earns partial or full credit.

What shifts demand

Demand shifts when something changes buyers' willingness to pay at every quantity. From his slides and polls:

The labor market flips the usual roles: firms are the demanders and workers are the suppliers, and the price is the wage. When AI cuts firms' demand for new computer science graduates, labor demand shifts in, and both the wage and employment fall (his poll answer D).

What shifts supply

Supply shifts when something changes sellers' marginal cost at every quantity, or the number of sellers.

The four single-shift cases

When only one curve shifts, the new equilibrium slides along the other curve, and the direction of P and Q is always known. The four graphs below use the same starting market, demand P = 10 − Q and supply P = Q, which cross at (5, $5). Each shift is 2 units.

ShiftPQWhere the new point is
Demand increasesupupnortheast
Demand decreasesdowndownsouthwest
Supply increasesdownupsoutheast
Supply decreasesupdownnorthwest

A pattern worth memorizing: a demand shift moves P and Q in the same direction, and a supply shift moves them in opposite directions.

When both curves shift

If demand and supply both move, one of P or Q is certain and the other depends on which shift is bigger.

  • If both shift the same way (both increase or both decrease), Q is certain, P is unclear. Both increases push Q up, but the demand increase pushes P up while the supply increase pushes P down.
  • If they shift opposite ways (demand up and supply down, or the reverse), P is certain, Q is unclear. Demand up and supply down both push P up, but they pull Q in opposite directions.

Both curves shift the same direction: you know Q, not P. Opposite directions: you know P, not Q. Say "it depends on which shift is larger" for the other one.

Reading data: P and Q together or apart

In real data we never see the curves. We see only where they cross, one point per year or month. Work backward from how P and Q moved:

  • P and Q moved in the same direction (both up or both down): demand shifted, or at least the demand shift was the bigger one.
  • P and Q moved in opposite directions: supply shifted, or the supply shift was the bigger one.

Be careful with the wording. If P and Q both rose, you can say demand increased, but you cannot say supply did not move; supply could have shifted a little too. Timing helps as well. The egg key used it: prices peaked around January after the fall bird migration, in 2023 and again in 2025, and it is hard to imagine a reason cooks would suddenly bake far more in January 2023, not in 2024, and again in January 2025. In Class 3 he made the same call for beef: prices rose, and of the two possible stories (more demand or less supply) "it's more a story of that reduction in supply," from costly feed and fertilizer. The industry's fix, rebuilding herds, is a supply story too.

The same logic is how economists measure a demand curve. The slides say a demand curve "is only revealed in real data when there is a supply shift with a stable demand curve": when only supply moves, the equilibrium points slide along demand and trace it out. The studies on those slides (old potato data, price experiments with health products) are background; what can earn points is reading which curve moved from price and quantity data.

Steepness of one curve decides what a shift of the other does

When one curve shifts, the new equilibrium slides along the other curve. So the shape of the other curve decides how the change splits between price and quantity.

  • If the other curve is flat, the shift shows up mostly as a change in Q, with little change in P.
  • If the other curve is steep, the shift shows up mostly as a change in P, with little change in Q.
  • In the extreme, a vertical curve means only P changes (planted soybeans on the Fall 2025 final), and a horizontal curve means only Q changes.

Edwards told the class to write "inelastic means steep and elastic means flat" on the cheat sheet. Steep demand (no close substitutes, like eggs or short-run gasoline) means a supply shock causes a big price swing and a small quantity change. In a Class 6 poll with vertical demand, a supply cut changed quantity by nothing at all and raised price a lot; he said "I fooled a lot of people there." The Problem Set 1 key used the supply side of the same idea for Houston: "where zoning restrictions are less onerous, so supply is more elastic (i.e., flatter)," a demand increase raises quantity, and then asks whether the effect would be the same in California. (It would not: steep California supply puts most of the effect into rents.)

Shift one curve, and the change splits according to the other curve: the flatter the other curve, the more Q moves and the less P moves.

Market demand is the horizontal sum

Market demand adds up every buyer's quantity at each price. On a graph that means adding sideways (horizontally): pick a price, read off each person's quantity, add them. Never add prices.

The kink is the heterogeneity story in a picture: at high prices only high-value buyers are in the market, and as the price falls, lower-value buyers join. Market supply works the same way. Later in the course it is the horizontal sum of every firm's marginal cost curve.

How to handle a story question

He called this "the hardest part": "if you've got a vignette ... you've got to figure out whether it's a shift in supply or in demand or possibly neither one." Use the same steps every time. The Fall 2025 egg question is the model.

  1. Name the market. What good, which place, what price? (The U.S. market for eggs, price per dozen, quantity in billions of dozens.)
  2. Name the buyers and the sellers. (Buyers: households, bakers, diners like Denny's. Sellers: egg farms.)
  3. Ask whether the story is about buyers or sellers. A story about buyers moves only demand. A story about sellers moves only supply. (Bird flu kills hens on farms: a seller story, so supply.)
  4. Decide between a shift and a movement. If the only thing that changed is this good's own price, nothing shifts. If something else changed, a curve shifts. Pick the direction by asking whether there is more or less at every price. (Fewer hens means fewer eggs at every price and higher marginal cost at every quantity: supply shifts left, which is up.)
  5. Use the shape of the other curve. (Demand for eggs is steep because nothing replaces eggs one for one, so the price jumps a lot and quantity falls a little.)
  6. State what happens to P and Q, with values if you have them. (Price rises from about $2 to about $6; quantity falls from 10 to about 8 billion dozen.)

A student in Class 5 asked: if fewer people need gas, wouldn't supply go up? Edwards answered that "less people needing gas is a story about the demand curve," and "you've got to try to think about the demanders and suppliers as being different groups." The suppliers did nothing new. The simplest story tied to the case earns full credit; an elaborate side story can get partial credit, but he called one answer "untethered to the case study."

Worked examples

Example: the egg market (Fall 2025 midterm, question 2, parts e to g)

Eggs have no single substitute. Once a farm exists, the marginal cost of an egg (feed, water, upkeep of a hen that lays about one egg a day) is low and rises slowly. A dozen eggs usually sells for about $2, and about 10 billion dozen are sold each year. In early 2025, after bird flu spread, the price hit about $6.

  1. Decide the slopes from the story. No close substitute means buyers will pay a lot rather than go without, so demand is steep (inelastic). Low, slowly rising marginal cost means supply is low and fairly flat, and still upward sloping.
  2. Draw the baseline. Put the crossing exactly at 10 billion dozen and $2, with dotted lines to both axes.
  3. Identify the shock. Bird flu kills hens, which is a problem for sellers, so it moves supply. The price spikes in January 2023 and January 2025 follow the fall migration, and no demand story explains that timing.
  4. Shift the right curve. Fewer hens means fewer eggs at every price, and higher marginal cost at every quantity: supply shifts left, which is up. Demand does not move.
  5. Read the new point off the steep demand curve: about $6, with quantity only a little lower, about 8 billion dozen.

Answer: Supply shifts left (up); demand stays put. Because demand is steep, price rises a lot (about $2 to about $6) and quantity falls a little (10 to about 8 billion dozen).

Example: Houston rental housing (Problem Set 1, question 3)

In an unregulated rental market like Houston, show what each event does to rent (vertical axis) and apartments rented (horizontal axis).

  1. (a) Climate change makes summers worse and people move north. This is about renters, so demand. Living in Houston is worth less at every quantity: demand shifts in (left, or down). The equilibrium slides down the supply curve: rent falls and the number of apartments rented falls.
  2. (b) A new light rail line cuts commuting costs. Again about renters: living in Houston is worth more, so willingness to pay rises at every quantity and demand shifts out (right, or up). Rent rises and, eventually, quantity rises. Because Houston's loose zoning makes supply fairly flat, much of the effect shows up as more apartments rather than higher rents.
  3. (c) High inflation leads the city to set a rent ceiling below the market rent. "Be careful." Renters value apartments exactly as before, and landlords' costs are the same, so neither curve shifts. The law holds the price at P2 below P1. Landlords supply only QS, which is below the old Q1, while renters want QD, above Q1.

Answer: (a) Demand in: P and Q fall. (b) Demand out: P and Q rise, with Q rising a lot where supply is flat. (c) No shift: the quantity rented falls to QS and there is a shortage of QD − QS.

Example: same shift, different steepness (light rail in Houston versus California)

Start from demand P = 10 − Q and an equilibrium at (5, $5). Demand rises by 2, to P = 12 − Q. Compare flat supply P = 3 + 0.4Q (Houston) with steep supply P = 2.5Q − 7.5 (California). Both pass through (5, $5).

  1. Flat supply: set 12 − Q = 3 + 0.4Q, so 9 = 1.4Q and Q ≈ 6.4. Then P = 12 − 6.4 ≈ $5.6.
  2. Steep supply: set 12 − Q = 2.5Q − 7.5, so 19.5 = 3.5Q and Q ≈ 5.6. Then P = 12 − 5.6 ≈ $6.4.
  3. Compare. Flat supply: Q up about 1.4, P up about $0.6. Steep supply: Q up about 0.6, P up about $1.4.

Answer: The demand shift is identical, but the new equilibrium slides along supply. Flat (elastic) supply turns it mostly into more apartments; steep (inelastic) supply turns it mostly into higher rents.

Common mistakes

Mistake: "Latte prices rose, so demand for lattes fell," treating a change in the good's own price as a shift of its demand curve.

Correct: The good's own price moves buyers along the demand curve: quantity demanded falls, demand does not. If the price rose, something else shifted a curve first (for example, pricier coffee beans shifted latte supply left).

Mistake: Letting a buyer story move supply: "fewer people need gas, so supply goes up."

Correct: Buyers and sellers are different groups. Fewer people needing gas is a story about buyers, so demand for gasoline shifts in, and both P and Q fall. Sellers did nothing new.

Mistake: Reading "supply shifts up" as "more supply."

Correct: On a graph, up means higher marginal cost at every quantity, which is less supply (left). A supply increase moves the curve right, which is down. If a question's wording is unclear, write how you are reading it.

Mistake: When both curves shift, claiming to know both P and Q.

Correct: Same direction: only Q is certain. Opposite directions: only P is certain. The other one "depends on which shift is larger."

Mistake: Shifting a curve for a rent ceiling (Problem Set 1, 3(c)).

Correct: Neither curve shifts. The law caps the price, so quantity rented is read off supply at the ceiling (QS), and the shortage is QD − QS.

Mistake: Drawing the egg graph with ordinary 45-degree curves and no numbers.

Correct: The slopes must match your story: steep demand (no 1:1 substitute) and flat, low supply (low marginal cost). Put the crossing at (10, $2) with dotted lines, and after the shock at about $6 with quantity only a little lower.

Mistake: Building market demand by adding prices (stacking curves vertically).

Correct: Add quantities at each price (horizontally). The market curve kinks at the price where another buyer enters.

Mistake: Assuming a demand shift always raises quantity, even when supply is fixed (the famous artist poll).

Correct: If supply is vertical (a fixed number of paintings that owners will not sell more of), only P rises. Q rises only if supply slopes up. State which supply shape you are assuming.

Practice questions

These are written the way Edwards writes his midterm: 2-point short answers that end in "Briefly explain," 4-point graphs on axes with numbers, and one long story question built like the Fall 2025 egg question. Work each one on paper before you open the answer. Keep written answers to two to four sentences; his cover page says "Less is more."

2 points The Daily Californian runs this headline: "Boba on Telegraph jumped from $6 to $7 a cup, so the demand for boba fell." Is the headline using economics words correctly? Briefly explain what probably happened.

Show answer

No. A change in a good's own price never shifts its own demand curve; it moves buyers along the curve. So the quantity demanded of boba fell, while demand (the whole curve) did not change. The price rise itself must come from a curve shifting, most likely supply: if tapioca, milk, or shop workers' wages got more expensive, marginal cost rose at every quantity, so supply shifted left (up), raising P and lowering Q.

Full credit: says the price rise is a movement along demand (quantity demanded fell, not demand), and names something that shifts a curve, such as higher input costs shifting supply left.

2 points Briefly explain why the supply curve of Uber rides in Berkeley on a Friday night slopes upward. Use opportunity cost.

Show answer

The supply curve is the marginal cost curve, and a big part of a driver's marginal cost is the value of his or her time. At a low fare, only drivers with the lowest opportunity cost drive, for example people with nothing else planned that night. To get more rides, the fare has to rise enough to pull in drivers who give up more: a shift at another job, a party, or studying for a midterm. So more rides come only at a higher price, and supply slopes up.

Full credit: supply is marginal cost, and marginal cost rises because each extra ride comes from a driver with a higher opportunity cost of time.

4 points Taquerías on Telegraph Avenue usually sell about 20,000 burritos a week at about $12. A drought raises the price of avocados, a key ingredient. Afterward, burritos sell for $14 and 16,000 are sold each week. Draw the market before and after, with values when you know them, and say which curve shifted and why.

Show answer

Avocados are an input, so this is a story about sellers. Each burrito now costs more to make, so marginal cost is higher at every quantity: supply shifts left (up), from S to S'. Buyers' willingness to pay for a burrito did not change, so demand stays put, and the new equilibrium slides up along demand from (20,000, $12) to (16,000, $14). Price and quantity moved in opposite directions, which is the sign of a supply shift.

Full credit: price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal, both curves labeled, both equilibria at the given values with dotted lines to the axes, supply shifted left (up) with demand unchanged, and one sentence saying pricier avocados raise marginal cost.

2 points At a campus shop, demand for Cal sweatshirts is P = 60 − Q and supply is P = 2Q, where Q is sweatshirts per day. Find the equilibrium. If the price is stuck at $30 because the tags were printed months ago, is there a shortage or a surplus, and how big? Show your work.

Show answer

Set 60 − Q = 2Q, so 3Q = 60, Q = 20, and P = 60 − 20 = $40. At $30, buyers want QD = 60 − 30 = 30 sweatshirts, but sellers offer only QS = 30 ÷ 2 = 15. Quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied, so there is a shortage of 30 − 15 = 15 sweatshirts a day. Neither curve shifts; if the tags could change, buyers who miss out would bid the price up toward $40.

Full credit: equilibrium Q = 20 at $40, and a shortage of 15 found as QD − QS = 30 − 15, with the work shown.

2 points Suppose that over one year the price of matcha powder rose about 50% and the amount sold rose about 20%, while matcha lattes took over social media and cafés near campus added them to their menus. Was this mainly a supply shift or a demand shift, and which way? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Demand increased (shifted right). Price and quantity both rose, and when P and Q move in the same direction, demand shifted, or at least the demand shift was the bigger one. The story fits: matcha became fashionable, which is a change in buyers' tastes. A supply decrease, such as a poor tea harvest, would have raised the price but lowered the quantity sold.

Full credit: demand shifted right, justified by P and Q moving in the same direction, ideally with the tastes or fashion story about buyers.

2 points Gas prices in California jump from $5 to $6 a gallon. What happens in the market for used hybrid cars like the Toyota Prius? Briefly explain which curve shifts.

Show answer

Gasoline is a complement to ordinary gas cars, so pricier gas makes driving a regular car more expensive. A hybrid uses much less gas, so it is a substitute for a regular car, and buyers' willingness to pay for a used hybrid rises at every quantity: demand for used hybrids shifts right. The price and quantity of used hybrids rise; since the number of used hybrids is nearly fixed in the short run, mostly the price rises. Supply does not shift, because the sellers did nothing new.

Full credit: demand for used hybrids shifts right (not supply), explained through gas as a complement to regular cars or hybrids as a substitute for them, and the price rises.

2 points Cheaper batteries cut the cost of making e-scooters. At the same time, UC Berkeley bans scooters inside campus buildings, which makes them less convenient for students. What do you know for sure about the price and quantity of e-scooters sold to students? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Cheaper batteries lower marginal cost, so supply shifts right (down). The ban lowers students' willingness to pay, so demand shifts left. Both shifts push the price down, so P falls for sure. The supply increase raises Q while the demand decrease lowers it, so what happens to Q depends on which shift is larger.

Full credit: supply right, demand left, price definitely falls, quantity ambiguous because the two shifts push it in opposite directions.

2 points The local utility raises its electricity rates sharply, and at the same time new factories make solar panels cheaper to produce. What happens to the price and quantity of rooftop solar systems? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Grid electricity is a substitute for rooftop solar, so higher rates raise willingness to pay for solar: demand shifts right. Cheaper panels lower marginal cost: supply shifts right. Both shifts raise Q, so the quantity of solar systems rises for sure. Demand pushes the price up and supply pushes it down, so P depends on which shift is larger.

Full credit: both curves shift right, Q definitely rises, P is ambiguous.

2 points The Big Game at Memorial Stadium sells out every year, and the stadium has a fixed number of seats. This year Cal is undefeated going into the game, and many more fans want tickets. What happens to the price and quantity of Big Game tickets? Briefly explain the shape of supply you are using.

Show answer

Fans' willingness to pay rises, so demand shifts right. Supply is vertical: the stadium cannot add seats for one game, so the same number of tickets exists at any price. With vertical supply, the whole demand increase shows up as a higher price (on the resale market, if Cal keeps its own prices fixed), and the quantity of tickets stays the same. This is the same twist as the planted soybeans on the Fall 2025 final: when the other curve is vertical, only price moves.

Full credit: demand shifts right, supply is vertical because the seats are fixed, the price rises and the quantity does not change.

4 points Berkeley's limits on new construction make the short-run supply of apartments steep. Suppose 20,000 apartments rent for an average of $2,500 a month. The university then enrolls more students who want to live off campus, and rent rises to $3,100 while the number of apartments rented rises only to 21,000. Draw the market before and after, with values, and briefly explain why rent rose so much while quantity barely moved.

Show answer

More students means more renters, which is a story about buyers: demand shifts right, from D to D'. Supply does not move. The new equilibrium slides up along the supply curve, and because supply is steep (few apartments can be added quickly), most of the shift shows up as higher rent ($2,500 to $3,100, up 24%) and little of it as more apartments (20,000 to 21,000, up 5%). In a city with loose zoning like Houston, where supply is flatter, the same demand shift would add many more apartments and raise rent less.

Full credit: axes labeled, steep upward-sloping supply, demand shifted right with supply unchanged, both equilibria labeled with values and dotted lines, and a sentence that steep (inelastic) supply puts most of the change into price.

Long question: A bittersweet market (20 points)

This question is built like the Fall 2025 egg question. Chocolate makers buy cocoa beans, the one ingredient that makes chocolate taste like chocolate. Most of the world's cocoa grows on small farms in West Africa, and a newly planted cacao tree takes several years before it produces any pods. The numbers below are round values to work with, the way his exams give them.

2 points (a) What is the likely shape of the market demand curve for cocoa beans: close to horizontal, close to vertical, or in between? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Close to vertical (steep, inelastic), at least in the short run. There is no close substitute for cocoa in chocolate: a chocolate maker cannot swap in another bean and still sell the same candy bar, so its willingness to pay for cocoa is very high before it would go without. With few substitutes, the quantity demanded barely responds to price.

Full credit: steep or inelastic demand, because cocoa has no close (one-for-one) substitute.

4 points (b) Once a farm has mature cacao trees, what determines the marginal cost of another kilogram of beans? Is it high or low? Then think about starting a new cocoa farm: how big a barrier to entry is the fixed cost over a year or two, and over ten years?

Show answer

With mature trees in the ground, the marginal cost of another kilogram is mostly the labor to pick and open the pods, plus fermenting, drying, and hauling the beans. That is low, although it rises with output as farmers hire more workers and reach less productive trees.

Starting a farm takes a large fixed cost: land, seedlings, and several years of care with no harvest at all. Over a year or two that is a huge barrier to entry, because a new farm cannot produce anything that soon. Over ten years it is a much smaller barrier, because new trees have time to mature.

Full credit: marginal cost is low (harvest labor, drying, transport) and rises somewhat with output; the fixed cost is large, a big barrier in the short run and much less of one in the long run.

4 points (c) Suppose the world cocoa market usually clears at about $3,000 per metric ton and about 5 million metric tons a year. Draw supply and demand at that equilibrium, with slopes that match your answers to (a) and (b).

Show answer

Draw demand steep, because cocoa has no close substitute. Draw supply low and fairly flat but still upward sloping, because the marginal cost of harvesting from existing trees is low and rises slowly. Put the crossing exactly at 5 million tons and $3,000, with dotted lines to both axes.

Full credit: price on the vertical axis, steep demand, low upward-sloping supply, crossing at (5 million, $3,000) with dotted lines to both axes.

2 points (d) Suppose a chart shows that over about a year and a half the cocoa price roughly tripled, to about $9,000 a ton, while the amount of cocoa sold fell about 20%. During the same period West Africa had unusually heavy rain followed by drought, and a plant virus spread through cacao farms. Was the price spike a supply shock or a demand shock? Briefly explain.

Show answer

A supply shock. Price rose while quantity fell, and when P and Q move in opposite directions, supply shifted. The events are all problems for sellers: bad weather and disease destroyed part of the crop, so fewer beans are available at every price. A demand story, such as people suddenly wanting far more chocolate, would have raised the quantity sold, not cut it.

Full credit: supply shock, using the opposite movement of P and Q, or the weather and disease story about sellers.

4 points (e) Redraw supply and demand and show the shock, with values when you know them.

Show answer

Demand does not move. Supply shifts up (left) to Supply', because each ton now costs more to produce and fewer tons are available at every price. The new crossing on the steep demand curve is at about $9,000 and 4 million tons. Because demand is steep, the price triples while the quantity falls only 20% (5 million to 4 million).

Full credit: the original curves with the baseline at (5 million, $3,000), Supply' drawn above the original supply, demand unchanged, the new equilibrium at about (4 million, $9,000) with dotted lines, and price rising a lot while quantity falls a little.

4 points (f) Suppose big chocolate makers raised candy bar prices and shrank their bars, while a Berkeley café that sells one chocolate croissant among dozens of other items kept its prices the same. Why the difference?

Show answer

For a chocolate maker, cocoa is the main ingredient in nearly everything it sells, so the price spike strongly raises its marginal cost curve. It has to pass much of that on, through higher prices or smaller bars (a smaller bar at the same price is a hidden price increase per ounce).

For the café, cocoa is a tiny share of total costs, since most of what it sells (coffee, sandwiches, other pastries) contains none. Its marginal cost barely moves, so it can absorb the increase. This is the same logic as Trader Joe's versus Denny's in the Fall 2025 egg question.

Full credit: cocoa is a large share of the chocolate maker's costs (its marginal cost rises a lot) and a tiny share of the café's (its marginal cost barely moves), so only the chocolate maker raises prices.

One-minute review

  • Demand slopes down because marginal benefit (willingness to pay) falls as quantity rises: "more is better, but at a decreasing rate."
  • Supply slopes up because marginal cost rises: more output comes from producers with higher opportunity costs (Bill at 1 fish per coconut, then Chris at 4).
  • Price below equilibrium: shortage QD − QS, price is bid up. Price above: surplus QS − QD, price falls. Neither curve moves.
  • The good's own price moves you along the curves. Only other causes shift them. A price ceiling shifts nothing.
  • Demand shift: P and Q move the same way. Supply shift: P and Q move opposite ways. Use this backward to read data.
  • Supply increase = right = down (lower marginal cost). Supply decrease = left = up.
  • Both curves shift the same way: Q known, P unclear. Opposite ways: P known, Q unclear.
  • The flatter the other curve, the more Q moves and the less P moves. Steep egg demand turned the bird flu supply shock into $2 to about $6 with quantity falling only from 10 to about 8 billion dozen.
  • Market demand is the horizontal sum: add quantities at each price, never prices.
  • Story questions: name the market, name buyers and sellers, ask whose story it is, decide shift or movement, use the other curve's shape, state P and Q with values.
