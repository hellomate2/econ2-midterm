The Fall 2025 midterm and the problem sets, walked through

Edwards told the class that last year's midterm "will be pretty indicative" of this one, so this page works through every question on the Fall 2025 midterm and every problem set question that covers midterm material. Each question has a model answer, what the official key looked for, and the graphs drawn the way the key drew them.

How to use this page

Treat each question as a real exam question. Read it, cover the screen, and write your answer on paper in the space you would actually get: two to four sentences for a 2-point part, a labeled sketch for a graph. Only then open "Show answer" and compare. The point is to find out what you cannot yet produce cold, because the exam gives you 60 minutes and no time to reconstruct ideas from scratch.

Question 1: eight short answers (16 points)

Every part is worth 2 points and tests one idea. A full-credit answer names the right answer and gives the economic reason in one or two sentences.

2 points 1(a). Of the two curves, demand and supply, which one slopes downward? Why does it slope down?

Show answer

Demand slopes downward. The height of the demand curve at any quantity is the marginal benefit of one more unit, which is the same thing as buyers' willingness to pay for it (or their marginal utility). Because of diminishing marginal utility, each extra unit adds less benefit than the one before, so the amount buyers are willing to pay falls as the quantity rises. Edwards' phrase for this is that more is better, but at a decreasing rate.

Official key: demand, because marginal benefit, marginal utility, or willingness to pay falls as quantity increases.

Full credit: say "demand" and give the falling marginal benefit (or marginal utility, or willingness to pay) reason. Saying "people buy less when the price rises" only restates the slope; the key wants the reason behind it.

2 points 1(b). In the market for lunches out around Berkeley, the price rose from $10 to $11 and quantity demanded fell from 50,000 to 47,500. Find the price elasticity of demand. Is demand relatively elastic or inelastic? Briefly explain.

Show answer
  1. Percent change in price, measured from the starting price: ($11 − $10) ÷ $10 = +10%.
  2. Percent change in quantity, measured from the starting quantity: (47,500 − 50,000) ÷ 50,000 = −2,500 ÷ 50,000 = −5%.
  3. Elasticity: ε = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP = −5% ÷ 10% = −0.5.
  4. The magnitude, 0.5, is less than 1, so demand is relatively inelastic: quantity changes proportionally less than price.

A quick check that the answer makes sense: spending went from $10 × 50,000 = $500,000 to $11 × 47,500 = $522,500. Spending rose when the price rose, which is exactly what inelastic demand implies.

Official key: −0.5 (or −½); relatively inelastic because the elasticity is smaller than 1 in magnitude. The key uses simple percent changes from the starting values, not the midpoint method.

Full credit: −0.5 with the 10% and −5% shown, plus "inelastic because the magnitude is less than 1."

2 points 1(c). The Rational Spending Rule says the marginal utility from the last dollar spent must be equal across goods. With clothing and food, that means muc ÷ pc = muf ÷ pf. If the price of clothing falls, how could you change your clothing purchases to restore the equality? Briefly explain.

Show answer

A lower pc shrinks the denominator, so the left side gets bigger: a dollar spent on clothing now buys more marginal utility than a dollar spent on food. To restore equality, buy more clothing. Because of diminishing marginal utility, each additional piece of clothing adds less utility, so muc falls as you buy more, and that pulls the left side back down until it equals the right side. (Buying less food, which raises muf, also helps, but the question asks about clothing.)

Official key: the left side rises when pc falls; increase purchases of clothing, which reduces muc.

Full credit: "buy more clothing," with the reason that the price cut raised the left side and buying more lowers the marginal utility of clothing.

2 points 1(d). Assuming Bill and Chris each want some fish and some coconuts, who will specialize in what, and why? (His hint: this is a simple question with a simple answer.)

Show answer

Chris fishes and Bill gathers coconuts. Each person should do the task with the lower opportunity cost. A coconut costs Chris 4 fish but costs Bill only 1 fish, so Bill has the comparative advantage in coconuts. A fish costs Chris only ¼ of a coconut but costs Bill a whole coconut, so Chris has the comparative advantage in fish. Chris being faster at both tasks does not matter.

Official key: Chris fishes and Bill gathers coconuts, because Chris's opportunity cost of coconuts is higher than Bill's (equivalently, Bill's opportunity cost of fishing is higher than Chris's).

Full credit: the right assignment plus a comparison of opportunity costs (4 fish versus 1 fish per coconut).

2 points 1(e). A new fishhook doubles fishing productivity for both of them: each now catches twice as many fish per hour. What, if anything, changes about their best specialization? Briefly explain.

Show answer
  1. New rates: Bill catches 2 fish or gathers 1 coconut per hour; Chris catches 16 fish or gathers 2 coconuts per hour.
  2. New opportunity cost of a coconut: Bill 2 fish, Chris 16 ÷ 2 = 8 fish.
  3. Chris's coconut still costs four times what Bill's costs, so the ranking is the same.

Nothing changes: Chris still fishes and Bill still gathers coconuts. A shock that scales both people's fishing by the same factor leaves the ratio of their opportunity costs unchanged, and comparative advantage depends only on that ratio.

Official key: nothing changes qualitatively; Chris is still comparatively much better at fishing. The key adds that they might get sick of fish and have Chris fish a bit less, but Bill would still always specialize in coconuts.

Full credit: "nothing changes," supported by the recomputed rates or opportunity costs.

2 points 1(f). List and briefly discuss the most important costs of attending UC Berkeley. (His hint: some important costs are ones you literally pay, like a check to the Regents, and some are not.)

Show answer

The two biggest costs are tuition and fees, which you pay directly, and foregone earnings, the wages you could have earned by working full time instead of enrolling. Foregone earnings are an opportunity cost: no one sends you a bill, but you give up that income. With average annual earnings around $60,000, foregone earnings are probably larger than tuition at a public university. Books and supplies count too. Living costs mostly do not, because you would pay rent and buy food whether you went to college or worked (Problem Set 1 asks exactly this).

Official key: tuition and fees, and foregone wages from full-time work; earnings around $60,000 probably exceed tuition except at private colleges. Room and board and books were also accepted, but "full credit answers need to state foregone earnings."

Full credit: you must name foregone earnings. Tuition alone, or tuition plus housing, loses points.

2 points 1(g). From the point of view of ECON 2, can there be an "optimal" amount of pollution, or is the optimal amount always zero? Refer to social marginal cost and social marginal benefit.

Show answer

The optimal amount of pollution is usually above zero. Pollution adds a damage cost to each unit produced, which raises social marginal cost above private marginal cost. The socially optimal quantity is where social marginal cost equals social marginal benefit (the demand curve, or willingness to pay). Up to that point, each unit is worth more to buyers than its full cost to society, including the pollution damage, so producing it makes society better off. That intersection usually happens at a positive quantity, which means some pollution. It could be at zero if the damage were enormous, but there is no reason it must be.

Official key: the socially optimal quantity is typically nonzero and finite, at the intersection of social marginal cost and social marginal benefit (demand, willingness to pay); it could be zero, but there is no reason to expect it must be.

Full credit: "usually not zero," with the optimum located where social marginal cost equals social marginal benefit.

2 points 1(h). Explain why the area above an upward-sloping market supply curve and below the market price is called producer surplus. What are those producers getting, and why is it a surplus?

Show answer

The supply curve is the marginal cost curve: its height at each quantity is what it costs to produce that unit. Every unit to the left of the equilibrium sells at the market price, but costs only its own marginal cost to produce. The gap between the price and the marginal cost is extra money the producer keeps on that unit. Adding up those gaps over all units gives the area between the price line and the supply curve. Put another way, producers receive price times quantity but pay only the total variable cost, which is the area under the marginal cost curve. It is a surplus because it is more than the minimum producers needed to be willing to sell.

Official key: producers receive the market price but pay the total variable cost, the area under marginal cost; they get the price above the marginal cost of each unit. Saying that marginal cost is the reservation price is "also true (but not required for credit)."

Full credit: price received minus marginal cost of each unit, summed over the units sold.

Question 2: the egg market (24 points)

The setup: eggs are an ingredient in many cooked and baked dishes, especially breakfast. Egg substitutes exist for particular dishes (you can bake an eggless cake or scramble tofu), but no single substitute replaces eggs everywhere. The question then walks through the market step by step, and each answer feeds the next.

2 points 2(a). What does this suggest about the shape of the market demand curve for eggs? Horizontal, vertical, or in between, and why?

Show answer

Demand for eggs is inelastic, so the curve is steep (close to vertical), at least in the short run. Elasticity depends on how easy it is to switch to substitutes. Since no single product replaces eggs across recipes, cooks and bakers keep buying eggs even when the price rises, which means their willingness to pay is high. It is not perfectly vertical, because some people do switch for some dishes. Edwards' cheat sheet line applies: inelastic means steep, elastic means flat.

Official key: inelastic or relatively vertical in the short run, because there is no obvious one-for-one substitute; willingness to pay is potentially very high.

Full credit: "steep, inelastic," because there is no close substitute.

2 points 2(b). Once a farmer already owns a chicken farm, what determines the marginal cost of an egg? Give a few examples and say whether they are expensive, so whether the marginal cost of an egg is high or low.

Show answer

Marginal cost is the extra cost of producing one more egg, given that the farm already exists. It comes from keeping an egg-laying hen, which lays about one egg per day: chicken feed, water, a bit of henhouse space, and some protection such as fencing or a guard dog. These are cheap, so the marginal cost of an egg is low. It probably still rises as the farm produces more, but it stays low. The land and buildings do not enter marginal cost, because the farmer pays for them whether or not one more egg is produced.

Official key: marginal cost is low: maintaining a hen (about 1 egg per day), feed, water, henhouse, fencing or a guard dog; it likely rises with output but stays relatively low.

Full credit: a few per-egg inputs, the observation that they are cheap, and the conclusion "low."

4 points 2(c). Does a chicken farm in California cost a little or a lot? How big a barrier to entry is that fixed cost over a short period like a year or two? What about over five years?

Show answer

It costs a lot. A chicken farm needs a fair amount of land, and land in California is expensive, even in agricultural areas away from the coast. That makes the farm a large fixed cost.

Over a year or two, that fixed cost is a big barrier to entry. A newcomer cannot quickly buy land, build henhouses, and raise laying hens, so existing farms face little new competition even when egg prices are high.

Over five years, it is a much smaller barrier. Given enough time, new producers can buy land and build farms. Edwards says this is true "basically by definition": the long run is the period long enough that no cost is fixed, so the land becomes one more input a new farm can choose to buy.

Official key: land in California is expensive, so a large fixed cost and a big barrier to entry in the short run; in the medium term and beyond, not as big a barrier, basically by definition.

Full credit: all three pieces: it costs a lot (expensive land), it is a big barrier in the short run, and it is a smaller barrier over five years because new farms can be built.

2 points 2(d). What do your answers suggest about the usual shape and position of the egg supply curve?

Show answer

Supply is the marginal cost curve, so it slopes upward, because marginal cost rises as output rises for most physical goods. It also sits low on the graph, because the marginal cost of an egg is low. Put together: a low, fairly flat, upward-sloping supply curve.

Official key: supply slopes upward because marginal cost typically rises; marginal cost is usually pretty low.

Full credit: "upward sloping because marginal cost rises" and "low because marginal cost is low."

4 points 2(e). Draw typical supply and demand for the U.S. egg market, where a dozen eggs usually costs about $2 and about 10 billion dozen are sold per year. Get the equilibrium and the general slopes right; other details do not matter. (His printed axes ran from $0 to $8 for the price of a dozen and from 0 to 20 billion dozen.)

Show answer
  1. Mark the equilibrium first: 10 billion dozen on the horizontal axis and $2 on the vertical axis, with dotted lines to both axes.
  2. Draw demand steep through that point, because you argued in 2(a) that demand is inelastic.
  3. Draw supply low and flat, rising gently through the same point, because you argued in 2(b) and 2(d) that marginal cost is low and rises slowly.
  4. Label both curves.

Full credit: steep demand, flat low upward supply, crossing exactly at (10 billion, $2) with the values marked, curves labeled. Two random crossing lines at the right point would miss the slope story that the question explicitly asks for.

2 points 2(f). A Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED) chart of the average price of a dozen eggs since 2020 shows two spikes, one in early 2023 and one in early 2025. Which is the more plausible explanation: a supply shock from seasonal bird migrations spreading avian flu to chickens, or a demand shock from lots of baking? Briefly explain.

Show answer

A supply shock. Wild birds migrate in the fall and spring, and the price spikes peak around January, right after the fall migration, which fits avian flu spreading to flocks and taking laying hens out of production. A demand story does not fit the timing: there is no reason cooks and bakers would want far more eggs in January 2023, not in January 2024, and then again in January 2025. The size of the spikes also fits: when demand is steep, a cut in supply produces a big jump in price.

Official key: the supply shock story is much more convincing; birds migrate in fall and spring and prices peak around January after the fall migration; it is hard to tell a demand story with spikes in January 2023 and then not again until January 2025.

Full credit: "supply shock," with the timing argument.

4 points 2(g). Redraw the typical supply and demand, then also show what happened in early 2025. Model the equilibria precisely, with values where you know them.

Show answer
  1. Redraw the baseline from 2(e): steep demand, flat low supply, equilibrium at (10 billion, $2).
  2. Avian flu raised the cost of producing eggs and removed hens, so supply shifts up and to the left. Label the new curve Supply'. Demand does not move: people's willingness to pay for eggs did not change.
  3. Find the new equilibrium where Supply' crosses the same steep demand curve. The FRED chart peaks a little above $6 in early 2025, so mark the new price at about $6.
  4. Read the new quantity off the graph. Because demand is steep, quantity falls only a little, to roughly 7.7 billion dozen in the key's drawing.

This is also an elasticity fact, the use of elasticity Edwards flagged in Class 11 (a quantity shock tells you the price change). On this drawing, the price rises 200% ($2 to $6) while quantity falls about 23% (10 to 7.7), an elasticity of about −23% ÷ 200% ≈ −0.12. Very inelastic demand turns a modest supply cut into a huge price jump.

Official key: the original demand and supply at (10, $2), plus Supply' shifted up, crossing demand at about $6.00 with quantity only somewhat below 10 billion.

Full credit: both equilibria shown, Supply' labeled, demand unchanged, new price marked at about $6, and quantity falling only a little.

4 points 2(h). In early 2025, Trader Joe's and some other grocers sold eggs at prices that were higher than usual but below the market price, and limited customers to one dozen per visit. Denny's and Waffle House added "egg surcharges" starting in February. Why would Trader Joe's keep egg prices low while Denny's passed the cost on to customers?

Show answer

The main reason is how much of each business depends on eggs. Trader Joe's sells many products that contain no eggs (beer, liquor, sweets, and lots of other food), so the egg shock barely raises its overall costs, and it can absorb the loss on eggs or simply run out. Denny's sells mostly dishes that contain eggs, so the shock strongly raises the marginal cost of most of its menu; a breakfast diner cannot absorb that.

Both businesses also expected that passing on the full increase would look like "price gouging," since customers care about fairness. Trader Joe's could afford to avoid that. Its below-market price works like a price ceiling: at that price, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied, so it rations with a one-dozen limit and empty shelves.

Official key: Trader Joe's sells many products with no eggs, while diners sell few products without eggs, so the shock would strongly raise Denny's marginal cost curve but not Trader Joe's. Both foresaw accusations of price gouging, but breakfast diners could not feasibly weather the shock, while grocery stores could absorb some of the cost or run out of eggs.

Full credit: the marginal cost difference (eggs are a large share of a diner's costs and a small share of a grocer's) plus the price gouging or fairness concern and why only the grocer could absorb it.

Question 3: Taylor Swift's Eras Tour as a monopoly (18 points)

The setup: from March 2023 to the end of 2024, the Eras Tour sold about 10 million tickets and grossed about $2 billion at the box office. Ignore resale prices. Edwards has said this year's monopoly question will be shorter, but the logic below is exactly what it will test.

2 points 3(a). What was the average ticket price? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Average price is total revenue divided by tickets sold: $2 billion ÷ 10 million = $2,000,000,000 ÷ 10,000,000 = $200.

Full credit: $200, with the division shown.

2 points 3(b). Does that seem low or high for a concert ticket, compared with other things you could do with about 3 hours? Briefly explain.

Show answer

High. A good benchmark is the opportunity cost of three hours of time. At an average hourly wage of about $36, three hours are worth about $108, and the $200 ticket costs nearly twice that before you count the time itself. Most other ways to spend an evening cost far less.

Official key: high, because the average hourly wage is about $36, or about $108 for three hours. "Full credit for reasoned answers of any kind."

Full credit: any reasoned answer; comparing the price with the value of three hours of time is the strongest one.

2 points 3(c). How many Taylor Swifts are there in the world, not counting Olivia Rodrigo or Sabrina Carpenter? Briefly explain.

Show answer

Exactly one. Her concerts are a perfectly differentiated product, and no one else can enter the market for Taylor Swift concerts. That total barrier to entry is the source of her market power.

Official key: only one; product differentiation and barriers to entry; "She's cornered the market."

Full credit: "one," tied to product differentiation or barriers to entry.

2 points 3(d). Is Taylor Swift in a perfectly competitive concert market alongside Olivia Rodrigo, Sabrina Carpenter, Bruno Mars, and Rosé, or does she have market power? Briefly explain.

Show answer

She has market power. Perfect competition requires many sellers of the same product, each taking the price as given. She is the only seller of her product. Some fans may see other artists' shows as substitutes, which keeps her demand from being perfectly inelastic, but nobody else can sell a Taylor Swift concert. So she faces a downward-sloping demand curve and chooses her own price.

Official key: market power; with perfect product differentiation there is no other producer of her concerts, even if some demanders see substitutes.

Full credit: "market power," because no one else can produce her concert.

4 points 3(e). Draw supply and demand for the Eras Tour, adding any other curves needed to explain the market outcome. The supply (marginal cost) curve is already drawn: a straight line from the origin through 10 million tickets at $100 and 15 million at $150. Axes: price $0 to $300, tickets 0 to 30 million.

Show answer
  1. Work backward from what you know. The outcome was 10 million tickets at an average price of $200. A monopolist picks the quantity where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, and marginal cost at 10 million is $100. So marginal revenue must pass through (10 million, $100), and demand must pass through (10 million, $200).
  2. Use the rule that a linear marginal revenue curve starts at the same vertical intercept as demand and is twice as steep. Write demand as P = a − bQ and marginal revenue as MR = a − 2bQ, with Q in millions. Then a − 10b = 200 and a − 20b = 100. Subtracting gives b = 10, so a = 300.
  3. Draw demand from $300 on the price axis to 30 million on the quantity axis (P = 300 − 10Q), and marginal revenue from $300 to 15 million (MR = 300 − 20Q).
  4. Mark where marginal revenue crosses marginal cost: 10 million tickets, at $100. That point sets the quantity.
  5. Go straight up from 10 million to the demand curve and read the price: $200. The price comes from demand, not from the $100 where MR meets MC.
  6. Also mark where demand crosses marginal cost: 15 million tickets at $150. You need it for part (g).

You do not need calculus. Edwards said you only need to know that marginal revenue is twice as steep, not why.

Official key: under monopoly, marginal revenue is twice as steep as demand; the quantity is set where MR = MC, at 10 million tickets; the price is read off the demand curve at that quantity, $200.

Full credit: demand and marginal revenue drawn with the correct intercepts, MR = MC marked at 10 million, and the $200 price read up on demand, with dotted guide lines.

3 points 3(f). Assume fixed costs are zero. Using this model, are profits zero? If not, what are they?

Show answer
  1. Total revenue = price × quantity = $200 × 10 million = $2 billion.
  2. Total cost = fixed cost + the area under the marginal cost curve up to the quantity sold. Fixed cost is zero, and the area under MC from 0 to 10 million is a triangle: ½ × 10 million × $100 = $500 million.
  3. Profit = total revenue − total cost = $2 billion − $0.5 billion = $1.5 billion.

Why the triangle is right: marginal cost starts at zero and rises, so the early tickets cost much less than $100 to supply. Charging every ticket the $100 marginal cost of the last one overstates cost.

Official key: "This is difficult, I got it wrong at first!" Total cost is the triangle under marginal cost, $500 million; revenue is $2 billion; profit is $1.5 billion. Full credit was also given for $1 billion, the rectangle ($200 − $100) × 10 million, which is what he first thought.

Full credit: profits are not zero; $1.5 billion with revenue and total cost shown. ($1 billion was accepted last year, but $1.5 billion is the correct number.)

3 points 3(g). Using this model, should more tickets have been sold, in the efficient sense of where supply meets demand? Or was the number exactly right? Be specific and briefly explain.

Show answer

Yes, more tickets should have been sold. The efficient quantity is where willingness to pay (demand, marginal benefit) equals marginal cost: 15 million tickets at $150. The monopoly sold only 10 million, where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, which is 5 million fewer, a reduction of one third. Between 10 and 15 million, every extra ticket was worth more to a fan than it cost to supply, so those sales would have added surplus. The monopolist stops early because selling more would mean cutting the price on all tickets, so its marginal revenue is below the price. The lost surplus is the deadweight loss triangle, ½ × 5 million × ($200 − $100) = $250 million (the key does not ask for this number).

Official key: monopoly pricing cuts tickets from the 15 million where demand = MC to the 10 million where MR = MC, a reduction of a third; the monopolist pushes output below the efficient point where willingness to pay, or marginal benefit, equals marginal cost.

Full credit: "yes," with both numbers (15 million efficient, 10 million sold) and the reason: monopoly output is below where marginal benefit equals marginal cost.

Problem Set 1: the midterm-relevant questions

Problem sets are graded on completion, but Edwards puts exam-style point values on them, and he said the midterm "will be similar to the work you do in discussions and problem sets." The answers below follow his answer key.

2 points Question 1(a). On a free afternoon you can either build two bookcases or watch three TV shows. What is the opportunity cost of watching one show?

Show answer

Three shows cost two bookcases, so one show costs 2 ÷ 3 = 2/3 of a bookcase. Check: three shows at 2/3 of a bookcase each is 2 bookcases, the whole afternoon.

Full credit: 2/3 of a bookcase. The common slip is 3/2, which is the opportunity cost of a bookcase measured in shows.

2 points Question 1(b). Suppose you would live in the same place with the same living situation whether you attend college or work. The opportunity cost of college includes tuition, fees, books, supplies, and the earnings you give up. Why are living expenses like food and rent left out?

Show answer

Opportunity cost compares the two choices, and you pay living expenses under either one, so they cancel. In the key's words, the cost of college minus the cost of working is:

(living + tuition + fees + books + supplies) − (living − earnings) = tuition + fees + books + supplies + earnings

The key adds that living costs could differ in reality, possibly higher at college, and college may still be worth it because each year of college raises future earnings by perhaps 10%.

Full credit: living expenses are paid either way, so they drop out of the comparison.

2 points Question 2(a). Draw the economy-wide production possibilities curve (PPC1) with no specialization, where each person divides their 10 hours between fish (vertical axis) and coconuts (horizontal axis) in the same way. Explain the vertical intercept, slope, and horizontal intercept.

Show answer
  1. Vertical intercept (everyone fishes all day): 10 × 1 + 10 × 8 + 10 × 2 = 110 fish.
  2. Horizontal intercept (everyone gathers coconuts all day): 10 × 1 + 10 × 2 + 10 × 2 = 50 coconuts.
  3. Because all three switch time in the same proportion, the curve is a straight line. Slope = −110 ÷ 50 = −2.2: the economy gives up 11 fish for every 5 coconuts.

Full credit: intercepts of 110 and 50 with the sums shown, slope −2.2.

2 points Question 2(b). If production follows comparative advantage to get the most fish and coconuts, how many of the three gather coconuts, and which ones? Refer to each person's opportunity cost of a coconut.

Show answer

Two people gather coconuts: Bill and Robinson. A coconut costs Bill 1 fish (1 ÷ 1) and Robinson 1 fish (2 ÷ 2), while it costs Chris 4 fish (8 ÷ 2). Bill and Robinson tie for the lowest opportunity cost, so they should do the same thing: they both gather only coconuts, and Chris only fishes.

Official key: "This is tricky." Bill and Robinson have the same opportunity cost because their production ratios are the same, so Bill and Robinson only collect coconuts and Chris only fishes.

Full credit: Bill and Robinson gather coconuts and Chris fishes, justified by the opportunity costs of 1, 1, and 4 fish.

4 points Question 2(c). Draw PPC2 with specialization by comparative advantage, and redraw PPC1 for comparison. Explain the intercepts, slopes, and any kinks, and describe the shape.

Show answer
  1. Start at the all-fish point, 110 fish and 0 coconuts. The intercepts are the same as PPC1, because if everyone does one task, specialization does not matter.
  2. The first coconuts come from the cheapest producers, Bill and Robinson, at 1 fish each. Together they gather 10 + 20 = 30 coconuts and give up 10 + 20 = 30 fish. That segment has slope −1 and ends at the kink (30 coconuts, 80 fish).
  3. Any further coconuts must come from Chris, at 4 fish each. Chris's 20 coconuts cost 80 fish, taking the economy to (50 coconuts, 0 fish). That segment has slope −4.
  4. Shape: bowed outward with one kink. The slope gets steeper because higher-cost producers are drawn in as the economy makes more coconuts. PPC2 lies outside PPC1 everywhere between the intercepts: that area is the gain from specialization.

Full credit: intercepts 110 and 50, one kink at (30 coconuts, 80 fish), slopes −1 and −4 explained by who is switching, and PPC2 drawn outside PPC1.

Question 3 is about an unregulated rental market like Houston, Texas. For each event, draw a separate graph with rent on the vertical axis and apartments on the horizontal axis, and say which curve shifts (if any) and what happens to rent and quantity.

2 points Question 3(a). Climate change makes summers hotter, and people decide to move north.

Show answer

Demand shifts left. Hotter summers lower the benefit of living in Houston, so willingness to pay for apartments falls at every quantity, and some renters take their demand to other cities. Supply does not change. The new equilibrium has a lower rent (P2) and fewer apartments rented (Q2).

Full credit: demand shifts left; rent and quantity both fall, shown on a labeled graph.

2 points Question 3(b). A new light-rail system cuts the commuting costs that had been driving some people away.

Show answer

Demand shifts right. Cheaper commuting raises the benefit of living in Houston, so willingness to pay for apartments rises at every quantity. Rent rises to P2, and the quantity rented eventually rises to Q2 as more apartments are built. The key notes that this is plausible in Houston, where loose zoning makes supply relatively elastic (flat), so much of the demand increase turns into more apartments. It then asks whether the effect would be the same in California. With strict zoning, supply is steep, so the same demand shift would mostly raise rents and add few apartments.

Full credit: demand shifts right; rent and quantity both rise.

2 points Question 3(c). High inflation changes local politics, and the city imposes a rent ceiling below the market rent. ("Be careful....")

Show answer

Neither curve shifts. A ceiling changes the price that is allowed, not what renters are willing to pay or what it costs landlords to supply apartments. Rent is held at the ceiling, P2, below the old equilibrium P1. At that rent, landlords supply only QS, read off the supply curve, which is less than Q1, and that is the number of apartments actually rented. Renters want QD, which is more than Q1. The gap is a shortage equal to QD − QS.

Official key: "This one is tricky." Neither demand nor supply shifts; the price is P2 < P1, quantity transacted is on the supply curve at QS < Q1, quantity demanded is QD > Q1 > QS, and there is a shortage of QD − QS. The policy does not force landlords to supply QD; it only caps the price.

Full credit: "no curve shifts," with the ceiling below equilibrium, quantity transacted at QS, and the shortage QD − QS marked.

Problem Set 2: the midterm-relevant questions

Question 1 uses natural log utility. Alice and Bob have identical tastes: utility is u(c) = log c, and the formula sheet gives marginal utility as mu(c) = 1 ÷ c. Each part is worth 1 point.

1 point Question 1(a). Alice consumes $400 a day and Bob consumes $160. What is each person's marginal utility? Are they different? Explain mathematically and in economic terms.

Show answer

Alice: 1 ÷ 400 = 0.0025. Bob: 1 ÷ 160 = 0.00625. They differ because, with log utility, marginal utility is the reciprocal of consumption, and their consumption differs. In economic terms, this is diminishing marginal utility: Alice already consumes more, so one more dollar adds less to her happiness than it adds to Bob's. Bob is like a version of Alice with fewer resources.

Full credit: both numbers, plus the reciprocal reason and the diminishing marginal utility reason.

1 point Question 1(b). Alice gives Bob $100, and each spends everything they have. Does the sum of their utilities rise or fall? Why?

Show answer

It rises. Before: log 400 + log 160 ≈ 5.99 + 5.08 = 11.07. After: log 300 + log 260 ≈ 5.70 + 5.56 = 11.26. The reason is that Bob's marginal utility (0.00625) was much higher than Alice's (0.0025), so the $100 adds more utility for Bob than it takes away from Alice.

Full credit: "rises," with the numbers or the marginal utility comparison.

1 point Question 1(c). How much should Alice transfer to Bob to maximize the sum of their utilities? Why?

Show answer

Think of each dollar transferred as having a marginal benefit, Bob's marginal utility, and a marginal cost, Alice's marginal utility. Keep transferring while marginal benefit exceeds marginal cost, and stop where they are equal. Since their utility functions are identical, their marginal utilities are equal only when their consumption is equal. Total consumption is $400 + $160 = $560, so each should have $560 ÷ 2 = $280, which takes a transfer of $120 from Alice to Bob.

Full credit: $120 (both end at $280), justified by marginal benefit equals marginal cost.

1 point Question 1(d). If Alice and Bob were total strangers, would the arrangement in (c) please both of them? Discuss.

Show answer

Probably not. Alice loses $120 and has no reason to care about a stranger's utility. Bob might even feel uncomfortable, since the transfer raises his income by 75% ($120 ÷ $160). An argument about equal marginal utilities is unlikely to persuade anyone, even if their utility functions really were identical.

Full credit: a reasoned "no," centered on Alice's loss.

1 point Question 1(e). If Alice and Bob were married or partners, would the arrangement please both of them? Discuss.

Show answer

More likely yes; many couples already run something like it. Couples consume many things jointly (meals, vacations, raising children), which are often more enjoyable together. Partners can also see how happy each other is, so they could tell that pooling resources raises their combined well-being.

Full credit: a reasoned answer based on joint consumption and caring about the partner's well-being.

Question 2 is about a household that buys electricity (E) and everything else (ee). Each part is worth 1 point.

1 point Question 2(a). What condition holds when the household maximizes utility? Describe it.

Show answer

muE(E) ÷ PE = muee(ee) ÷ Pee. The last dollar spent on electricity brings the same marginal utility as the last dollar spent on everything else. If it did not, moving a dollar toward the good with more utility per dollar would make the household better off.

Full credit: the equation plus "each dollar brings the same marginal utility."

1 point Question 2(b). The price of electricity rises. How does the household change its purchases of electricity and everything else? Use your answer to (a).

Show answer

A higher PE lowers the left side, so the equation no longer holds. To restore it, the household buys less electricity, which raises muE and pushes the left side back up, and buys more of everything else, which lowers muee and brings the right side down. (The hint says to ignore the fact that electricity is used together with other goods.)

Full credit: less electricity and more of everything else, explained through the marginal utilities.

1 point Question 2(c). An intense heatwave hits, and the household has air conditioning that runs on electricity. Compared with (a), with normal prices, how does this shift its choices? (His hint: "The emphasis on shift is intentional.")

Show answer

During a heatwave, every unit of electricity is worth much more, because it runs the air conditioner. Marginal utility of electricity rises at every quantity, and since the demand curve is the marginal utility schedule, demand for electricity shifts right. At the old choice, the left side of the equation is now bigger than the right side. To restore equality, the household buys more electricity, moving along the new demand curve, which lowers muE, and buys less of everything else, which raises muee.

Full credit: "demand for electricity shifts out" (a shift, not a movement along the curve), so the household buys more electricity and less of everything else.

Question 3 is the nonstandard case: a domestic market for oil, a globally traded good, so supply is perfectly elastic. Demand is P = 5 − Q and supply is MC = 1, a horizontal line. Each part is worth 1 point. These are the numbers to know cold.

1 point Question 3(a). Draw supply and demand, find the competitive equilibrium, and report consumer surplus and producer surplus.

Show answer
  1. Set demand equal to supply: 5 − Q = 1, so Q = 4 and P = 1.
  2. Consumer surplus is the triangle below demand and above the price: base 4, height 5 − 1 = 4, area ½ × 4 × 4 = 8.
  3. Producer surplus is zero. Supply is flat at the price, so there is no area between the price and the supply curve: every unit sells for exactly its marginal cost.

Full credit: equilibrium (4, 1), consumer surplus 8, producer surplus 0 with the reason.

1 point Question 3(b). Using ε = (1 ÷ slope) × (P ÷ Q), what is the supply elasticity? What is the demand elasticity at the equilibrium, and is demand elastic or inelastic there?

Show answer

Supply: the slope is 0, so 1 ÷ slope is infinite, and supply is perfectly elastic. Demand: the slope is −1, so at (4, 1), ε = (1 ÷ −1) × (1 ÷ 4) = −0.25. The magnitude is below 1, so demand is inelastic at that point.

Full credit: supply elasticity infinite; demand elasticity −0.25, inelastic.

1 point Question 3(c). The government imposes a $2 per unit tax and says producers will pay it. Show the tax and find the new equilibrium, the prices consumers pay and producers keep, consumer and producer surplus, tax revenue, and deadweight loss. Who really pays the tax, and why?

Show answer
  1. A per-unit tax drives a $2 vertical wedge between what consumers pay and what producers keep. Who mails the check to the government does not matter.
  2. Producers must still keep at least their marginal cost of 1, so consumers must pay 1 + 2 = 3. Set 5 − Q = 3: Q = 2.
  3. Consumers pay $3 and producers keep $1. Consumers bear the whole tax, because perfectly elastic supply means producers will not accept any less than $1.
  4. Consumer surplus: ½ × 2 × 2 = 2. Producer surplus: still 0. Tax revenue: $2 × 2 units = 4. Deadweight loss: the triangle from Q = 2 to Q = 4 between demand and MC, ½ × 2 × 2 = 2.
  5. Check: 2 + 0 + 4 + 2 = 8, the original total surplus.

Full credit: (2, 3), consumer surplus 2, producer surplus 0, revenue 4, deadweight loss 2, and "consumers pay all of it because supply is perfectly elastic."

1 point Question 3(d). Production causes pollution, with an external cost of $2 per unit. Without a tax, find the equilibrium, producer and consumer surplus, total external cost, and deadweight loss. What tax would restore efficiency?

Show answer
  1. Social marginal cost is private marginal cost plus the damage: SMC = 1 + 2 = 3, a flat line $2 above supply.
  2. The unregulated market ignores the damage, so it stays at (4, 1). Producer surplus 0, consumer surplus 8.
  3. Total external cost: $2 × 4 units = 8.
  4. The efficient quantity is where SMC meets demand: 5 − Q = 3, so Q = 2. The units from 2 to 4 cost society more than buyers value them. Deadweight loss is the triangle between SMC and demand from Q = 2 to 4: ½ × 2 × 2 = 2.
  5. A corrective tax of $2 per unit, equal to the external cost, moves the market to Q = 2.

Full credit: market at (4, 1), consumer surplus 8, producer surplus 0, external cost 8, deadweight loss 2, corrective tax $2 per unit.

1 point Question 3(e). Instead, suppose the industry is a monopoly, with no tax and no externality. What price and quantity does it choose? What are producer surplus, consumer surplus, and deadweight loss?

Show answer
  1. Marginal revenue has the same intercept as demand and twice the slope: MR = 5 − 2Q.
  2. Set MR = MC: 5 − 2Q = 1, so Q = 2.
  3. Read the price off demand at Q = 2: P = 5 − 2 = 3.
  4. Consumer surplus: ½ × 2 × 2 = 2. Deadweight loss: the triangle between demand and MC from Q = 2 to 4, ½ × 2 × 2 = 2.
  5. The monopolist's profit, its surplus over cost, is (3 − 1) × 2 = 4. Check: 2 + 4 + 2 = 8.

The answer key writes that producer surplus is "still zero" here, but its very next part says the monopolist "profits $4 as before." The $4 is right: the monopolist sells 2 units at $3 that cost $1 each. If this comes up, write the profit as (P − MC) × Q = $4.

Full credit: Q = 2 from MR = MC, P = 3 from demand, consumer surplus 2, deadweight loss 2.

1 point Question 3(f). Now suppose both things are true: the industry is a monopoly, and production causes the $2 per unit pollution. The monopolist cares only about its private marginal cost. Find the price, quantity, consumer and producer surplus, external cost, and deadweight loss. Does this show it was good that Standard Oil had a monopoly from 1890 to 1910?

Show answer
  1. The monopolist ignores the pollution and still sets MR = private MC: Q = 2, P = 3.
  2. Social marginal cost is 3, and SMC crosses demand at exactly Q = 2. So the monopoly produces the efficient quantity, which the key calls "seemingly through magic" and "a lucky coincidence."
  3. Consumer surplus 2, monopoly profit 4, external cost −$2 × 2 units = −4. Net social surplus = 2 + 4 − 4 = 2, the most this market can produce, so deadweight loss is 0.

On Standard Oil, the key gives no direct answer. A sensible one: no, you cannot conclude that. The result only holds because the monopoly's cut in output happens to match the size of the externality exactly. With a different external cost, the monopoly would produce too little or still too much, and nothing makes a real monopolist pick the right amount.

Full credit: Q = 2, P = 3, consumer surplus 2, profit 4, external cost 4, net surplus 2, and the observation that the efficient outcome is a coincidence.

4 points Question 4. Based on the coverage of the U.S. Justice Department's case against Google, is modern U.S. antitrust policy more concerned with the prices consumers pay or with a firm's dominance over an industry? (10 to 15 lines.)

Show answer

Dominance over an industry. The case was not about the prices consumers pay for search, which is free to users. It was about anticompetitive practices that kept Google dominant, such as paying to be the default search engine on Apple's iPhone. The judge agreed that Google had built a cycle of dominance that blocked rivals and let it raise ad prices above what a competitive market would allow. Prices do appear, but they are the prices advertisers pay, not consumers; as the key puts it, "they are not the prices that consumers pay!"

The second part asked why Apple's businesses (phones, personal electronics, computers, and cloud and streaming services) mattered. The judge let Google's payments to Apple continue, worried that barring them would likely mean fewer products and less innovation from Apple.

Full credit: "dominance," supported by the default search engine practice and the point that the prices at issue were ad prices.

Patterns he repeats

  • Every topic gets at least one 2-point short answer. The winning formula is the answer plus one sentence of reasoning in course vocabulary: marginal benefit, marginal cost, willingness to pay, opportunity cost, foregone earnings, social marginal cost.
  • He reuses the same numbers. Bill catches 1 fish or 1 coconut per hour, Chris 8 fish or 2 coconuts, Robinson 2 and 2, everyone 10 hours. Average earnings are about $60,000 a year and the average wage about $36 an hour.
  • The long question follows a fixed sequence: infer how steep demand is from substitutes, reason about marginal cost and fixed costs, draw the baseline at the given numbers, decide from data whether supply or demand shifted, draw the shift with values, then explain how a firm responds.
  • Graphs are worth 4 points each. He gives axes with numbers and sometimes one curve, and you must hit the stated equilibrium exactly, make the slopes match your story, and label every curve and point.
  • Monopoly always runs the same way: marginal revenue has the same intercept as demand and is twice as steep, MR = MC sets the quantity, the price comes from demand, and the efficient quantity is where demand meets marginal cost. Expect a shorter version of this on Thursday.
  • He likes twists: identical opportunity costs (Bill and Robinson), a shock that scales everyone equally and changes nothing, a price ceiling where no curve shifts, perfectly elastic supply where producer surplus is zero and consumers bear the whole tax, and monopoly plus pollution landing on the efficient quantity.
  • Profit with zero fixed cost is revenue minus the area under marginal cost, a triangle when marginal cost starts at zero. He got this wrong himself at first.
  • Fairness and market power keep coming back: Trader Joe's rationing eggs below the market price, Taylor Swift tickets priced below what fans would pay, and the price gouging reading in Problem Set 1 (binding price controls cause shortages, but people value fairness and sellers may have market power).
  • Elasticity runs in both directions. Given price and quantity changes, compute ε = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP from the starting values. Given a quantity shock, find the price change with %ΔP = %ΔQ ÷ ε, as in the egg spike.
  • Opinion questions get full credit for any reasoned answer, and he gives partial credit generously. Write something sensible for every part, keep it inside the box, and remember "Less is more."
