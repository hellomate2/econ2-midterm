Class 5 · Welfare analysis

Surplus, efficiency, and price controls

This unit turns the supply and demand picture into a way of measuring how well off buyers and sellers are, and shows what happens to that well-being when a market produces too little, too much, or is held at a price set by law. On the exam it shows up as short "why is this area called surplus" questions, numeric CS, PS, and deadweight loss calculations on straight-line curves, and price ceiling graphs and stories (rent control, price gouging, Trader Joe's eggs).

How it shows up on the exam

Edwards writes every midterm question as a short written answer or a graph on pre-printed axes. There is no multiple choice on the midterm. Welfare ideas appear all over his exams, sometimes as their own question and sometimes folded into a bigger story.

The ideas, explained

Marginal utility and why demand slopes down

Utility is an index of how well off you are, measured in made-up units called utils. Total utility is the level of well-being from everything you consume. Marginal utility (MU) is the extra utility from one more unit, so the MU of the nth unit is total utility at n minus total utility at n − 1. In calculus terms MU is the rate of change (the derivative) of total utility.

The textbook example from Class 5 is Sarah eating ice cream cones:

Total utility rises, but at a decreasing rate, and marginal utility falls with each cone. This is the law of diminishing marginal utility. The sixth cone actually lowers her utility (negative MU); Edwards joked that someone would have to pay you to eat it, like on a dare. Total utility peaks at 5 cones. The 50 is utils, not dollars, so it is not a $50 cone.

Edwards gave two short phrases in a class poll, and they answer two different questions. Why does total utility rise with quantity? "More is always better." Why does marginal utility fall? "One more is never as good as the last one more." That second idea is the demand curve: your willingness to pay for one more unit falls as you have more of the good, so demand = marginal benefit = marginal utility = willingness to pay, and it slopes down. His Fall 2025 key phrased it as "more is better" but at a decreasing rate.

Demand slopes down because marginal utility (marginal benefit, willingness to pay) falls as you consume more. Supply slopes up because marginal cost rises, which comes from opportunity cost.

The supply side mirrors this. In the textbook's Ushi example, Ushi can earn $6 an hour washing dishes or collect cans for the deposit. Each hour spent on cans gives up dish wages, which is an opportunity cost, so he only collects more cans when the deposit per can rises. Supply is the marginal cost (MC) curve, and it slopes up because each extra unit pulls in resources with a higher opportunity cost (the same reason pulling Chris off fishing raises the cost of coconuts on the island).

Edwards does not test why the first cone's MU is plotted at 0.5 instead of 1. He said so directly: "not going to be on the test, not ever." What he wants is the shape: total utility increasing at a decreasing rate, and demand sloping down.

Consumer surplus

Consumer surplus (CS) is the benefit consumers get from a good above and beyond the price they pay. Think of buyers lined up from highest willingness to pay to lowest. The height of the demand curve above the first unit is the marginal benefit of that unit to the buyer who values it most; the height above the second unit is the next buyer's marginal benefit, and so on.

  • Total benefit of buying Q units is the whole area under the demand curve from 0 to Q (a trapezoid when demand is a straight line).
  • Total spending is the rectangle P × Q.
  • CS is total benefit minus spending: the area under the demand curve and above the price, out to the quantity bought. With straight-line demand it is a triangle.

Edwards described CS as what consumers "take away in the bag": their willingness to pay above the market price.

Producer surplus, in the words his key rewards

Producer surplus (PS) is the benefit producers get from selling a good above and beyond the cost of producing it. Line sellers up from lowest marginal cost to highest. The height of the supply curve at each unit is the marginal cost of that unit.

  • Total (variable) cost of producing Q units is the area under the MC (supply) curve from 0 to Q.
  • Total revenue is P × Q.
  • PS is revenue minus total variable cost: the area above the supply (MC) curve and below the price.

Why the area above supply and below price is producer surplus (Fall 2025 key, in plain words): producers of the units to the left of where price meets supply receive the market price on every unit, but they pay only the total variable cost of producing them, which is the area under the marginal cost curve. So on each unit they get the price above that unit's marginal cost, and adding up those gaps gives the area. His key adds that MC is the lowest price at which the producer would ever sell (the reservation price), which is true but not required for credit.

A student in Class 5 described PS as the gains producers get "above the market equilibrium." Edwards corrected it: PS is the area above the marginal cost curve and below the price. The price is the relevant line, not "the equilibrium."

One special case he uses: when supply is flat (constant MC), every unit sells at exactly its marginal cost, so PS = 0. That is why PS was zero in Problem Set 2, question 3.

Total surplus, triangles, and trapezoids

Total economic surplus = CS + PS. It is the net gain to society from all the trades in a market, and on the graph it is the area between the demand (MB) curve and the supply (MC) curve, out to the quantity actually traded.

Every area on his exams is a triangle, a rectangle, or a trapezoid:

  • Triangle: area = ½ × base × height
  • Rectangle: area = width × height
  • Trapezoid (two parallel vertical sides): area = ½ × (left height + right height) × width

With demand P = 10 − Q and supply P = Q, the curves cross where 10 − Q = Q, so Q = 5 and P = $5. CS is the triangle with base 5 and height 10 − 5 = 5, so CS = ½ × 5 × 5 = 12.5. PS is the triangle with base 5 and height 5 − 0 = 5, so PS = 12.5. Total surplus is 25.

Allocative efficiency: three conditions

An allocation is allocatively efficient (also called Pareto efficient) when total surplus is as large as possible, so there is no other allocation that would deliver more surplus. The measure makes no judgment about who gets the surplus: one dollar is one dollar, whether it goes to a consumer or a producer, rich or poor. Efficiency requires three things:

  1. The good is produced up to the point where marginal benefit = marginal cost.
  2. The good goes to the consumers with the highest marginal benefit.
  3. The good is produced by the producers with the lowest marginal cost.

Condition 1 is about how much is traded. Conditions 2 and 3 are about who buys and who sells. A price ceiling can break condition 1 (too little is traded) and also condition 2 (the units may go to the wrong buyers), which is why its true cost can be larger than the triangle on the graph.

Deadweight loss: too little and too much

Deadweight loss (DWL), also called deadweight burden, is the reduction in total surplus when the quantity differs from the efficient quantity, because trades that would have created more benefit than cost do not happen, or trades that cost more than they are worth do happen.

Too little. If only Q = 3 units are traded when the efficient quantity is 5, then for each unit between 3 and 5 some buyer values it more than it costs to make (at Q = 3, MB = $7 and MC = $3). Those gains from trade are never realized. The lost surplus is the triangle between demand and supply from 3 to 5: ½ × (5 − 3) × (7 − 3) = 4. Edwards' plain-language version for a tax: you skip a purchase only because of the tax, "no other reason," and that skipped trade is the deadweight loss.

Too much. If 7 units are produced, each unit past 5 costs more to make than it is worth to the buyer (at Q = 7, MC = $7 but MB = $3). Those units destroy surplus. The DWL is the triangle between supply (now on top) and demand (on the bottom) from 5 to 7: ½ × (7 − 5) × (7 − 3) = 4. Edwards' real-world case is farm price supports and crop insurance, which produce excess supply "that you didn't really need. And that's deadweight loss."

Key practical rule (his slide): the deadweight loss triangle points toward the efficient allocation and grows outward to the current quantity. Both too little and too much production create deadweight loss.

For straight lines: DWL = ½ × |Qefficient − Qactual| × (gap between MB and MC at Qactual).

The First Welfare Theorem, and why equity is separate

The First Theorem of Welfare Economics says that a competitive equilibrium, where supply equals demand, maximizes total economic surplus. In other words, competitive markets are efficient. All three conditions hold automatically: trade stops exactly where MB = MC, every buyer with willingness to pay above the price buys, and every seller with marginal cost below the price sells. Edwards adds that this needs a market that works: people honor contracts, nobody sells "a bad coconut that's rotten in the middle," buyers understand what they buy, and there is no pollution.

The theorem is blind to who gets the surplus. Willingness to pay depends a lot on income, and economic surplus just counts dollars regardless of who receives them. So a market can be efficient without being equitable, which the slides describe as allocations that do not keep us awake at night feeling sad for others (the Batman example: Bruce Wayne only became Batman because his parents were rich). Efficiency is still a worthy goal, but interfering with prices to help equity usually costs some surplus. This is the equity versus efficiency tradeoff.

Price ceilings

A price ceiling is a legal maximum price. It only matters (it is binding) when it is set below the equilibrium price. A ceiling above equilibrium changes nothing; as Edwards put it, we are following the law, "And then who cares?"

Walk through a binding ceiling step by step:

  1. Neither curve shifts. Buyers value the good exactly as before, and the cost of producing it has not changed. The law only changes the price, so we move along both curves.
  2. At the ceiling price PC, sellers supply less: quantity supplied QS is below the old Q1. Buyers want more: quantity demanded QD is above Q1.
  3. Quantity traded = QS, because no one can force sellers to sell more than they want at that price. The short side of the market wins.
  4. There is a shortage = QD − QS, the horizontal gap at the ceiling price.
  5. Because price cannot ration the good, something else does: queues, luck, or government rationing (the slides mention COVID vaccines in 2021 and rationing after disasters).
  6. There is a deadweight loss: the triangle between demand and supply from QS to Q1, pointing at the efficient quantity.

Who wins and who loses. Producers always lose: they sell fewer units at a lower price. Consumers gain on the units they still get, since they now pay less for them (the rectangle (P1 − PC) × QS moves from producers to consumers), but they lose the surplus on the units no longer traded (the part of the DWL triangle above the old price P1). So consumer surplus can rise or fall, depending on which effect is bigger. The slide says the ceiling reduces total surplus "but it could increase consumer surplus (at the expense of producer surplus)."

Misallocation. The CS area on the graph assumes the units go to the buyers with the highest willingness to pay. With a shortage there is no reason to expect that. Edwards' example: an elderly person keeps a rent-controlled apartment while a rich young college student who would pay much more cannot get one. When the wrong buyers get the good, condition 2 of efficiency fails and the loss is larger than the DWL triangle. A tax does not cause this problem, because the price still rations the good.

Binding price ceiling: set below equilibrium, neither curve shifts, quantity traded = QS, shortage = QD − QS, DWL triangle from QS to Q1, possible misallocation on top, and CS can rise or fall while PS falls.

Rent control

Until 1998 Berkeley had rigid rent controls. Since 1999, rent "stabilization" caps increases only once a unit is occupied; a vacated unit can go back to market rent. In the model, rent control is a price ceiling, so it creates a shortage and a deadweight loss. Edwards' summary: "Rent control is great if you're in that group that has access to it and it's pretty bad if you're not." It is a handout to long-time residents, and it hurts newcomers (students hunting for apartments) and landlords. In the real world it also protects tenants from swings in rent when supply and demand shift. An alternative used around the world is subsidized public housing, where the government controls both price and quantity.

He also explained why the equilibrium is the stable point. Under a loosely enforced ceiling, landlords turn a closet into an extra room and rent it out, because the shortage means people are knocking on doors willing to pay more. "That's why this intersection is the one that's stable. It's because people are shifty." A binding ceiling needs the government to police it.

Price floors

A price floor is a legal minimum price. It binds only when set above equilibrium. Again neither curve shifts. At the floor, buyers want less (QD below Q1) and sellers want to sell more (QS above Q1). Quantity traded is QD, because sellers cannot make buyers purchase more. The gap QS − QD is excess supply (a surplus of the good). The DWL triangle runs from QD to Q1. Now it is producers who may gain (higher price on the units sold) and consumers who always lose. If the government buys up the excess, as with farm price supports, production rises past the efficient quantity and you get the "too much" deadweight loss instead.

Price gouging laws (Problem Set 1)

Many states ban "price gouging," meaning big price increases after a shock such as a hurricane or a pandemic. In the textbook model, an anti-gouging law is a binding price ceiling: price cannot rise enough for quantity supplied to meet quantity demanded, so there is a shortage, and there is no guarantee that the buyers with the highest willingness to pay get the good. His Problem Set 1 key used Taylor Swift tickets as an example of below-market pricing where fans fight each other and scalpers to get in line.

His key then names two things the textbook model leaves out:

  • Fairness. Consumers value fairness, not just goods. Concert tickets are not priced at market levels because that would feel unfair; only wealthy fans could go.
  • Market power. The model assumes competitive sellers who take the price as given. After a shock, some sellers suddenly have market power (the article's examples were hand sanitizer and protective equipment early in the pandemic). Fighting unfair pricing that comes from market power is part of what gouging laws do.

Below-market pricing with limits: Trader Joe's eggs

When avian flu sent egg prices up in early 2025, Trader Joe's sold eggs at elevated but below-market prices and limited customers to one dozen per visit, while Denny's and Waffle House added egg surcharges. Selling below market price works like a price ceiling on the store's own shelves: quantity demanded exceeds what the store has, so there is a shortage, and the limit (plus who shows up first) rations the eggs instead of willingness to pay.

His Fall 2025 key explains the difference through marginal cost. Trader Joe's sells many products with no eggs in them (beer, liquor, sweets), so the egg shock barely raised its overall costs, and it could absorb some of the cost or simply run out. For a breakfast diner, eggs are in most dishes, so the shock strongly raised its marginal cost curve and could not be absorbed. Both businesses probably expected that passing on higher prices would draw accusations of price gouging, which is the fairness concern from Problem Set 1.

Shortages without any law

Prices are sometimes rigid even without controls: restaurants on Saturday night, street parking during football games. Repricing in real time is impractical or upsets people (the backlash to Uber surge pricing). The shortage gets resolved by queuing, which economists call inefficient, though people like that a rich person cannot bribe his way to the front.

Worked examples

Example: Sarah's marginal utility

Sarah's total utility from 0 to 6 cones per hour is 0, 50, 90, 120, 140, 150, 140 utils. Find the marginal utility of each cone, where total utility peaks, and what this says about demand.

  1. MU of each cone is the change in total utility: 50 − 0 = 50, 90 − 50 = 40, 120 − 90 = 30, 140 − 120 = 20, 150 − 140 = 10, 140 − 150 = −10.
  2. MU falls with every cone: diminishing marginal utility.
  3. Total utility is highest at 5 cones (150). The sixth cone has negative MU, so it lowers her total utility.
  4. Since MU is her willingness to pay for one more cone, falling MU means she would pay less for each additional cone. That is a downward-sloping demand curve.

Answer: MU = 50, 40, 30, 20, 10, −10. Total utility peaks at 5 cones. Falling MU is why demand slopes down.

Example: CS, PS, and total surplus at equilibrium

Demand is P = 10 − Q and supply is P = Q. Find the equilibrium, CS, PS, and total surplus.

  1. Set demand equal to supply: 10 − Q = Q, so Q = 5 and P = $5.
  2. CS is the triangle under demand and above $5, from 0 to 5. Base = 5, height = 10 − 5 = 5. CS = ½ × 5 × 5 = 12.5.
  3. PS is the triangle above supply and below $5, from 0 to 5. Base = 5, height = 5 − 0 = 5. PS = ½ × 5 × 5 = 12.5.
  4. Total surplus = 12.5 + 12.5 = 25. This is the most surplus the market can produce (First Welfare Theorem).

Answer: Q = 5, P = $5, CS = 12.5, PS = 12.5, total surplus = 25.

Example: deadweight loss from too little and too much

Same curves. (a) A quota allows only 3 units. (b) A program pushes production to 7 units. Find the DWL each time.

  1. (a) At Q = 3, MB is read off demand: 10 − 3 = $7. MC is read off supply: $3. Buyers value units 3 to 5 more than they cost, so those trades are lost.
  2. DWL = ½ × (5 − 3) × (7 − 3) = ½ × 2 × 4 = 4. The triangle's point is at (5, $5), the efficient quantity.
  3. (b) At Q = 7, MC = $7 and MB = 10 − 7 = $3. Units 5 to 7 cost more to make than they are worth to buyers.
  4. DWL = ½ × (7 − 5) × (7 − 3) = 4. Supply is on top of this triangle, and it still points back to Q = 5.

Answer: DWL = 4 in both cases. Total surplus falls from 25 to 21 either way.

Example: a price ceiling of $3, with every area

Same curves. The government sets a ceiling of $3. Find quantity supplied, quantity demanded, the shortage, CS, PS, DWL, and the change in CS and PS.

  1. The ceiling is below the equilibrium price of $5, so it binds. Neither curve shifts.
  2. Quantity supplied at $3: P = Q gives QS = 3. Quantity demanded at $3: 3 = 10 − Q gives QD = 7. Shortage = 7 − 3 = 4.
  3. Only 3 units trade. CS is the area under demand and above $3 from 0 to 3. Demand is 10 at Q = 0 and 7 at Q = 3, so the area is a trapezoid with heights 10 − 3 = 7 and 7 − 3 = 4: CS = ½ × (7 + 4) × 3 = 16.5. This assumes the buyers with the highest willingness to pay get the 3 units.
  4. PS is the triangle above supply and below $3 from 0 to 3: PS = ½ × 3 × 3 = 4.5.
  5. DWL is the triangle between demand and supply from 3 to 5. At Q = 3 the gap is 7 − 3 = 4: DWL = ½ × (5 − 3) × 4 = 4.
  6. Check: 16.5 + 4.5 + 4 = 25, the original total surplus.
  7. Changes: CS goes from 12.5 to 16.5 (up 4). Consumers gain the rectangle (5 − 3) × 3 = 6 on the units they still buy and lose ½ × 2 × 2 = 2 on units no longer traded. PS goes from 12.5 to 4.5 (down 8): it loses the same 6 to consumers plus 2 of its own surplus on the lost units.

Answer: QS = 3, QD = 7, shortage = 4, CS = 16.5, PS = 4.5, DWL = 4. Consumers gain 4 and producers lose 8 here, but with misallocation the real loss can exceed 4.

Example: a price floor of $7

Same curves. A floor of $7. Find the quantity traded, the excess supply, CS, PS, and DWL.

  1. The floor is above $5, so it binds. Neither curve shifts.
  2. QD at $7: 7 = 10 − Q gives 3. QS at $7: 7. Only 3 units trade, because buyers will not take more. Excess supply = 7 − 3 = 4.
  3. CS = triangle under demand above $7 from 0 to 3 = ½ × 3 × (10 − 7) = 4.5.
  4. PS = trapezoid above supply and below $7 from 0 to 3, with heights 7 − 0 = 7 and 7 − 3 = 4: PS = ½ × (7 + 4) × 3 = 16.5 (assuming the lowest-cost sellers make the 3 units).
  5. DWL = ½ × (5 − 3) × (7 − 3) = 4. Check: 4.5 + 16.5 + 4 = 25.

Answer: 3 units trade, excess supply = 4, CS = 4.5, PS = 16.5, DWL = 4. The floor is the mirror image of the ceiling: now producers can gain and consumers lose.

Common mistakes

Mistake: Shifting supply or demand when a price ceiling is imposed.

Correct: Neither curve shifts. Buyers' values and sellers' costs are unchanged; only the allowed price changes, so you move along both curves to QS and QD.

Mistake: Saying the quantity traded under a ceiling is QD, or reading CS out to QD.

Correct: Quantity traded is QS, the smaller of the two. CS, PS, and the DWL triangle all stop or start at QS.

Mistake: Defining producer surplus as "gains above the market equilibrium" or as profit.

Correct: PS is the area above the marginal cost (supply) curve and below the price: on each unit, the price received minus that unit's marginal cost. It ignores fixed costs.

Mistake: Saying a price ceiling always helps consumers.

Correct: Consumers gain on the units still sold but lose the units no longer traded, and the units may go to the wrong buyers. CS can rise or fall. With demand P = 10 − Q and supply P = Q, a $3 ceiling raises CS to 16.5, but a $1 ceiling lowers it to 8.5.

Mistake: Drawing the DWL triangle on the wrong side of equilibrium, or thinking only "too little" creates DWL.

Correct: The triangle always sits between the actual quantity and the efficient quantity, points toward the efficient quantity, and grows outward to the current quantity. Producing too much creates DWL too.

Mistake: Drawing a "binding" ceiling above the equilibrium price (or a floor below it).

Correct: A ceiling binds only below equilibrium and a floor only above it. Otherwise the market just clears at equilibrium and nothing changes.

Mistake: Claiming the competitive equilibrium is the "best" or "fair" outcome.

Correct: It is efficient (maximizes total surplus), which says nothing about equity. Surplus counts dollars regardless of who gets them, and willingness to pay depends on income.

Mistake: Using the triangle formula on the CS area under a ceiling.

Correct: Under a ceiling, the CS region is a trapezoid (it is cut off at QS). Use ½ × (left height + right height) × width, or split it into a rectangle and a triangle.

Practice questions

2 points Briefly explain why the area above an upward-sloping market supply curve and below the market price is called producer surplus. What are producers getting, and why is it a surplus?

Show answer

The supply curve is the marginal cost curve: its height at each unit is the cost of producing that unit. Producers of all the units to the left of where price meets supply receive the market price on each unit, but they only pay the total variable cost of producing them, which is the area under the marginal cost curve. On every unit they get the price above that unit's marginal cost, so the area between the price and the supply curve adds up these gains. It is a surplus because it is what producers receive beyond the cost of producing.

Full credit: price received on each unit, minus marginal cost of that unit (area under MC = total variable cost). Mentioning that MC is the lowest price a seller would accept is fine but not required.

2 points Which curve slopes downward, demand or supply? Briefly explain, using Sarah's ice cream numbers (total utility 50, 90, 120, 140, 150 for 1 to 5 cones).

Show answer

Demand. Sarah's marginal utility from each cone is 50, 40, 30, 20, 10: each cone adds less than the one before (diminishing marginal utility). Demand is marginal benefit, or willingness to pay, so as she consumes more she is willing to pay less for one more, and the demand curve slopes down. More is better, but at a decreasing rate.

Full credit: names demand and gives the reason as falling marginal utility, marginal benefit, or willingness to pay.

QS = 3 (from P = Q) and QD = 10 − 3 = 7, so the shortage is 4. Only 3 units trade.

CS = trapezoid under demand above $3 from 0 to 3 = ½ × (7 + 4) × 3 = 16.5 (if the highest-value buyers get the units). PS = ½ × 3 × 3 = 4.5. DWL = ½ × (5 − 3) × (7 − 3) = 4. Check: 16.5 + 4.5 + 4 = 25.

The graph looks like the ceiling figure above: horizontal line at $3, dotted lines to QS = 3, Q1 = 5, QD = 7, shortage bracket from 3 to 7, DWL triangle from 3 to 5 pointing at (5, $5).

Full credit: QS = 3, QD = 7, shortage 4, CS 16.5, PS 4.5, DWL 4, with the graph labeled (ceiling below P1, areas shaded, DWL triangle between QS and Q1).

2 points Same curves, but now the ceiling is $1. Does consumer surplus rise or fall compared with no ceiling? Show your work.

Show answer

QS = 1, so only 1 unit trades. Demand is 10 at Q = 0 and 9 at Q = 1. CS = ½ × (9 + 8) × 1 = 8.5, down from 12.5, so CS falls. Consumers save $4 on the one unit they still buy (a gain of 4 × 1 = 4) but lose the surplus on units 1 to 5 that are no longer traded. Here the second effect is bigger. (PS = ½ × 1 × 1 = 0.5 and DWL = ½ × 4 × 8 = 16; check: 8.5 + 0.5 + 16 = 25.)

Full credit: CS = 8.5, it falls, with the reason: a ceiling helps consumers on units still sold but costs them the units no longer traded.

2 points An unregulated city adopts a rent ceiling set below the market rate. Which curve shifts, and what happens to the quantity of apartments rented? Briefly explain. (Be careful.)

Show answer

Neither curve shifts. Renters value apartments the same as before and landlords' costs are unchanged; the law only caps the price. At the lower price, the quantity rented is on the supply curve at QS, below the old Q1, while quantity demanded rises to QD above Q1. The shortage is QD − QS. The policy does not force landlords to supply QD; it only forces the price down.

Full credit: "neither curve shifts," quantity rented falls to QS, shortage = QD − QS.

2 points List the three conditions for allocative efficiency. Which of them can a binding price ceiling violate?

Show answer

(1) The good is produced up to where marginal benefit equals marginal cost. (2) It goes to the consumers with the highest marginal benefit. (3) It is produced by the producers with the lowest marginal cost.

A binding ceiling violates (1), because at QS marginal benefit is above marginal cost, so too little is traded (the DWL triangle). It can also violate (2): with a shortage, the good is rationed by queues or luck, so it may go to buyers who value it less (the elderly tenant and the rich student). Condition (3) still holds, since the sellers who supply are the ones with MC below the ceiling.

Full credit: all three conditions, plus (1) and (2) named for the ceiling with a reason.

3 points Price supports and crop insurance lead farmers to grow more corn than the competitive equilibrium. Is this efficient? Where is the deadweight loss on a graph? Briefly explain.

Show answer

No. Past the equilibrium quantity, the marginal cost of growing each extra bushel is above the marginal benefit to buyers (supply is above demand), so those bushels destroy surplus. The deadweight loss is the triangle between supply (on top) and demand (on the bottom) from the efficient quantity Q1 out to the actual quantity. It points back toward Q1. Edwards' line: the policy produces excess supply that is not really needed, "and that's deadweight loss."

Full credit: not efficient, MC above MB past Q1, DWL triangle to the right of Q1 pointing back at it.

2 points A classmate says: "A competitive market maximizes total surplus, so its outcome is the best one for society." Briefly evaluate.

Show answer

The first part is the First Welfare Theorem: a working competitive market maximizes total surplus, so it is efficient. But the theorem is blind to distribution. Surplus counts dollars regardless of who gets them, and willingness to pay depends heavily on income, so an efficient outcome can still be inequitable. Improving equity by interfering with prices usually sacrifices some surplus: the equity versus efficiency tradeoff.

Full credit: efficient yes (First Welfare Theorem), equitable not necessarily, because willingness to pay depends on income.

4 points During an egg shortage, a grocery chain keeps egg prices below the market price and limits each customer to one dozen, while breakfast diners add egg surcharges. What does the grocery chain's policy look like in the supply and demand model, what does it cost, and why might it make sense for the grocery chain but not for diners?

Show answer

It works like a price ceiling on the store's own eggs: at the below-market price, quantity demanded exceeds the eggs available, so there is a shortage, and the one-dozen limit (plus who shows up first) rations the eggs instead of willingness to pay. The cost is that eggs may not go to the buyers who value them most.

It makes sense for a grocery store because most of what it sells contains no eggs, so the egg shock barely raises its overall marginal cost; it can absorb some cost or run out. For a breakfast diner, eggs are in most dishes, so the shock strongly raises its marginal cost curve and it cannot absorb it. Both expected that passing on high prices would draw price gouging accusations; customers care about fairness.

Full credit: shortage and non-price rationing (a de facto ceiling), plus the marginal cost difference (eggs a small share of the grocery's products, most of the diner's), with fairness or gouging accusations as the motive.

4 points After a hurricane, a state's anti-price-gouging law keeps the price of bottled water and generators at pre-storm levels. What does the textbook supply and demand model predict? Name two factors the model leaves out and how they change the conclusion.

Show answer

The model treats the law as a binding price ceiling. Demand jumps after the storm, but price cannot rise, so quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied: a shortage. Price no longer rations the goods, so there is no guarantee the people with the highest willingness to pay get them, and sellers have less incentive to bring in more supply.

Two missing factors (from his Problem Set 1 key): consumers value fairness, so a sudden price spike on essentials can feel unfair and the cap protects that; and sellers may have market power after a shock (few sellers can reach the area), so the high price may reflect market power rather than a competitive price. Combating unfair pricing from market power is part of what gouging laws are for.

Full credit: shortage and no guarantee high-willingness-to-pay buyers get the good, plus fairness and market power as the missing factors.

2 points A quota limits a market to 3 units, while demand is P = 10 − Q and supply is P = Q. Which way does the DWL triangle point, and how big is it?

Show answer

At Q = 3, marginal benefit is $7 and marginal cost is $3, so too little is traded. The triangle lies between demand and supply from Q = 3 to Q = 5 and points right, toward the efficient quantity 5. DWL = ½ × 2 × 4 = 4.

Full credit: points toward Q = 5, DWL = 4, with MB above MC at Q = 3.

One-minute review

  • Diminishing marginal utility (Sarah: MU 50, 40, 30, 20, 10, then −10) is why demand slopes down. Demand = MB = MU = willingness to pay. Supply = MC, rising because of opportunity cost.
  • CS = area under demand and above price. PS = area above supply (MC) and below price: producers get the price on each unit but pay only its marginal cost (area under MC = total variable cost).
  • Total surplus = CS + PS = area between MB and MC up to the quantity traded. With P = 10 − Q and P = Q: CS = PS = 12.5, total 25.
  • Efficiency needs MB = MC, the good to the highest-MB buyers, and production by the lowest-MC sellers.
  • DWL triangle points toward the efficient quantity and grows out to the current quantity. Too little and too much both create it.
  • First Welfare Theorem: competitive equilibrium maximizes total surplus. It ignores equity; willingness to pay depends on income.
  • Binding ceiling (below equilibrium): neither curve shifts, quantity traded = QS, shortage = QD − QS, DWL from QS to Q1, misallocation on top, CS can rise or fall, PS falls. Ceiling $3 example: CS 16.5, PS 4.5, DWL 4.
  • Binding floor (above equilibrium): quantity traded = QD, excess supply = QS − QD, DWL. Floor $7 example: CS 4.5, PS 16.5, DWL 4.
  • Rent control helps insiders, hurts newcomers and landlords. Price gouging laws: shortage in the model, but the model misses fairness and market power.
  • Trader Joe's eggs: a below-market price with a limit is a shortage rationed by limits; it worked because eggs are a small part of its costs, unlike a diner's.
